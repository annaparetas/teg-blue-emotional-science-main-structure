## What Chronic means here

A response can persist for many reasons.

The danger may still be present. A cue may have acquired wider significance. A newer association may be difficult to retrieve outside the place where it was learned. An action may have become habitual. Anticipation or rumination may repeatedly reconstruct the demand. Recovery may remain incomplete in some response channels. Development may have changed the person's expectations, sensitivities or available actions.

TEG-Blue uses **Chronic organisation** for this family of recurrent or persistent patterns. It does not propose one chronic state, one trauma pathway or one biological signature.

> **Chronic organisation:** organisation that is readily reconstructed, broadly generalised, recurrently narrowed, prolonged or slow to revise because continuing conditions, prior learning, developmental adaptation, restricted action, repeated mental representation or interrupted recovery have changed what is recruited, weighted, expressed or released.

This is a project-owned integration. Research can ground its component routes, but it cannot validate Chronic as a natural kind or locate a person at a single biological depth.

## The persistence routes

| Route | What may persist or recur | What would need to be assessed | What must not be inferred |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Continuing conditions** | Protection remains recruited because danger, coercion, deprivation, pain, instability or demand continues. | Present material, relational and bodily conditions; power; available alternatives. | That the response belongs mainly to the past. |
| **Learned weighting** | Some signals, outcomes or actions receive greater or lesser priority after experience. | Specific cue, outcome history, current expectation, attention and action value. | A unitary “threat system” or a false reading. |
| **Generalisation** | Responding spreads to similar cues or contexts. | Generalisation gradient, similarity dimension, context and comparison cues. | That all broad distress reflects overgeneralisation. |
| **Reduced discrimination** | Danger and safety cues evoke insufficiently differentiated responses. | Responses to both danger and safety cues across several measures. | That high intensity equals poor discrimination. |
| **Context-bound retrieval** | Newer learning is available in some contexts but earlier learning returns elsewhere. | Acquisition, updating, retention, context change and retrieval conditions. | That the newer learning disappeared or the person chose not to use it. |
| **Controllability and constrained action** | Low or unreliable action–outcome relations narrow expected or attempted action. | Actual contingencies, detected control, perceived control, capability, resources and power. | Global helplessness, zero agency or personal responsibility for structural constraint. |
| **Habit and repeated action** | Behaviour becomes less sensitive to current outcome value under some conditions. | Outcome devaluation, contingency degradation, practice and current goal knowledge. | That every repetition is a habit or that habit means unconscious. |
| **Perseverative representation** | Worry, rumination, imagery or anticipation re-present demand across time. | Form, content, timing, intrusiveness, function and physiological association. | That thinking is the original cause or that stopping thought would remove actual danger. |
| **Prolonged response or interrupted recovery** | Some channels remain altered after the event or are recruited again before settling. | Channel-specific trajectory, baseline, event timing, sleep, health and repeated exposure. | Whole-person non-recovery from one biomarker. |
| **Developmental adaptation** | Experience during development changes later attention, learning, expectation, regulation or available action. | Exposure dimensions, timing, duration, developmental stage, protective conditions and longitudinal outcome. | A fixed brain change, inevitable disorder or reconstructable personal history. |
| **Social transaction** | Earlier adaptations and current responses alter relationships, which may then change future conditions. | Reciprocal pattern over time, others' actions, power, support, exclusion and repair. | That the affected person generated the harm or carries equal power. |

## ESS-04 · Learned weighting

Learned weighting is the ESS component that connects condition-reading to Chronic organisation. It does not mean that the organism stores a fixed emotional verdict. It means that experience can alter which information becomes prominent, what outcome is expected, which action seems available and how readily a response is recruited.

### Revised component claims

- **Definition:** the ESS grouping for experience-shaped changes in the priority given to cues, contexts, bodily signals, outcomes and possible actions.
- **Phenomenon:** a small or ambiguous feature may quickly become important, while other information receives less attention or fails to change the response.
- **Mechanism:** associative learning, generalisation, discrimination, attention, prediction, context retrieval, habit learning, sensitisation and action–outcome learning may contribute through different routes.
- **Development:** timing, repetition, controllability, caregiving, culture, health, disability, material conditions and later repair may change what is learned and how flexibly it is expressed.
- **Application:** asks what history of outcomes may have made a signal or action important without assuming that the response is irrational, accurate or permanent.
- **Boundary:** learned weighting is not stored truth, a trauma detector, one memory system or proof of past adversity. Present conditions can warrant the same weighting, and behaviour alone cannot reconstruct its source.

### What may be weighted

| Target of weighting | Example question |
| --- | --- |
| Cue | Which feature begins to attract attention? |
| Context | Where or with whom is the response more likely? |
| Bodily signal | Which sensation becomes urgent, threatening or easy to overlook? |
| Outcome | What does the person expect will happen next? |
| Action | Which response seems possible, effective, costly or unsafe? |
| Social information | Whose expression, authority or withdrawal carries particular significance? |
| New evidence | Under what conditions can contradictory information influence the next response? |

## Formation does not move in one direction

```text
conditions and outcomes
        ↓
selection and organism-relative significance
        ↓
action, consequence and recovery
        ↓
changes in weighting, expectation or retrieval
        ↺
future condition-reading and available action
```

Several loops can operate together. A person may accurately detect ongoing coercion while also generalising from earlier harm. New safety learning may be present but difficult to retrieve in a familiar context. A repeated action may be partly habitual and still chosen because alternatives remain unsafe.

The categories help us ask better questions. They do not divide lived experience into isolated mechanisms.

The [Signal Recruitment, Persistence and Recovery map](../../01-signal-map/grounding/recruitment-persistence-and-recovery.html) presents these routes as trajectories after recruitment begins. It keeps episode-level persistence, later reconstruction, altered recruitment and cumulative cost separate so that “Chronic” does not become shorthand for one response remaining continuously active.

## Developmental account

Development changes both the organism and the conditions in which learning occurs. Earlier environments can shape attention, reward learning, threat processing, regulation and executive control, but findings vary by exposure, task, developmental stage and person. Threat and deprivation show partially different associations in current research, while unpredictability and harshness are treated differently across models.

For TEG-Blue, this means:

- replace one adversity dose with measured exposure dimensions;
- preserve timing, duration, recurrence and developmental stage;
- record protective relationships, resources and later environments;
- separate adaptation under earlier conditions from present cost;
- avoid calling a pattern latent pathology when no disorder is present;
- never infer childhood exposure from an adult behaviour or Gradient Position.

## Fluid–Chronic depth is multidimensional

The scientific spine does not support one Fluid–Chronic severity line. At minimum, a profile would need to distinguish:

1. recruitment threshold;
2. response magnitude;
3. generalisation breadth;
4. danger–safety discrimination;
5. context sensitivity;
6. persistence duration;
7. recovery profile;
8. access to alternative action;
9. responsiveness to changed outcomes;
10. current environmental demand.

Two people could therefore appear equally “deep” while differing across nearly every relevant process. This strengthens PEND-008: depth should become a multidimensional profile rather than a single hidden quantity.

## Boundaries for behaviour and harm

Chronic organisation may help explain why behaviour becomes easier to recruit or harder to revise. It does not determine whether the behaviour is harmful, whether another person has less power, whether feedback was available or whether repair was attempted.

The science therefore supports two statements at once:

- behaviour can be shaped by processes outside current conscious explanation;
- explanation does not remove impact, agency, power or responsibility.

These claims belong together. Separating them would weaken both the scientific and ethical architecture.

## Evidence state and next work

This spine now governs the definition and routes of Chronic organisation. It completes the first evidence pass for ESS-04 learned weighting and establishes the profile dimensions required by PEND-008.

The next scientific pass should add trauma-specific and developmental contradictory evidence to the routes needed by F2. Implementation in active diagrams should wait until those claims have been reviewed and the multidimensional Fluid–Chronic profile has been agreed.
