# Harm, Power and Repair Grounding

Status: working foundation and claim register
Origin: recovered from the old TEG-Blue Engine’s Chronic Survival D / Domination evidence dossier
Purpose: keep nervous-system organisation, harmful behaviour, power use, responsibility and repair connected without treating them as the same thing

## The distinction this layer protects

The body can organise around protection. A person can then act in many possible ways. Some actions protect without governing another person. Some use power to make another person carry the cost, surrender agency or accept an imposed outcome.

These are connected questions, but they are not one question.

> **Internal organisation may help explain pressure on behaviour. Power, agency, impact, feedback and recurrence help us examine whether the behaviour became harmful.**

The governing separation is:

> **Organisation ≠ behaviour pattern ≠ power use**

This means:

- Extreme Defensive Mobilisation does not establish Domination.
- Chronic Survival organisation does not establish abusive behaviour.
- Calm behaviour does not establish safety.
- High activation does not establish coercion.
- A protective history does not remove impact or responsibility.
- Harmful behaviour does not allow us to infer one internal mechanism or developmental history.

## Three connected planes

| Plane | Main question | Appropriate evidence | Cannot establish by itself |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Organisation** | What is being prioritised, narrowed, recruited, inhibited or held available within the person? | Within-person change, physiology, report, cognition, action readiness, learning and recovery | Whether another person was harmed, coerced or deprived of agency |
| **Behaviour pattern** | What did the person do, omit, repeat or make costly? | Observation, reports from those involved, records, context, consequences and recurrence | One hidden motive, nervous-system Position, diagnosis or origin story |
| **Power use** | Whose options, safety, credibility, resources or agency changed, and through what means? | Power distribution, consent, demands, threats, surveillance, restriction, deprivation, force, impact and response to refusal | A complete account of the actor’s internal experience |

The planes should be examined together only after each has been described on its own terms.

## The enforcement threshold

Power is present in many ordinary relationships and roles. Parents, teachers, clinicians, employers, institutions and emergency leaders may have legitimate but bounded authority. A person may also have physical strength, social standing, money, information, credibility or control over access.

Power becomes a harmful enforcement question when it is used to make refusal, separation, contradiction or independent action meaningfully less possible.

> **The enforcement threshold is crossed when fear, punishment, surveillance, restriction, deprivation, force or another power advantage is used to secure an outcome through another person’s reduced agency.**

This is a behavioural and relational threshold. It is not a physiological state.

## Twelve claim families

### HPR-01 · Power and asymmetry

- **Definition:** Unequal capacity to shape conditions, consequences, access, credibility, resources or another person’s available options.
- **What to observe:** Formal authority, physical capacity, economic dependence, social status, control of information, institutional credibility, caregiving responsibility and the practical ability to leave or seek support.
- **Why it matters:** The same sentence or behaviour can have different effects when one person controls housing, care, income, reputation, children, legal status or access to a group.
- **Boundary:** Asymmetry is not automatically abuse. The question is how the advantage is used, bounded, reviewed and made accountable.
- **Current connections:** IC-01 Conditions · IC-07 Situation Signals · BE-02 Behaviour · BE-03 Cost · BE-04 Responsibility and repair · TB-01 integrative grounding.

### HPR-02 · Agency and viable refusal

- **Definition:** The practical possibility of choosing, refusing, revising, delaying or leaving without an additional coercive cost being imposed.
- **What to observe:** Whether refusal is permitted; what follows a “no”; whether options remain realistically available; whether fear, dependence or prior retaliation alters apparent consent.
- **Why it matters:** A person may say yes while experiencing that refusal is unsafe, futile or too costly. Compliance and consent are therefore not interchangeable.
- **Boundary:** Every choice occurs within constraints. Viable refusal does not require the absence of all consequences; it requires separating real consequences from punishment added to secure compliance.
- **Current connections:** IC-09 Conscious Participation · BE-01 Organising pull · BE-02 Behaviour · BE-04 Responsibility and repair.

### HPR-03 · Coercion and enforcement

- **Definition:** The use of threats, punishment, intimidation, surveillance, restriction, deprivation, force or credible power to narrow another person’s options and secure an outcome.
- **What to observe:** Demands, threatened consequences, monitoring, isolation, restricted access, fear, force, punishment after refusal and patterns spanning several life domains.
- **Why it matters:** Coercion is defined by the option field and power relationship, not only by the visible form of one request or conflict.
- **Boundary:** Influence, persuasion, negotiation, caregiving limits and emergency constraint are not automatically coercion. Scope, necessity, proportionality, refusal and accountability must be examined.
- **Current connections:** NG-09 Strategic Management boundary · NG-10 Extreme Defensive Mobilisation boundary · BE-02 Behaviour · BE-04 Responsibility and repair.

### HPR-04 · Consent and compliance

- **Definition:** Consent is a voluntary and sufficiently informed agreement that remains open to revision; compliance is behaviour that matches a request, rule or demand regardless of how freely it was chosen.
- **What to observe:** Information, capacity, voluntariness, power, ability to withdraw, persistence after hesitation, fear of consequence and whether the person’s participation remains active.
- **Why it matters:** A compliant outcome can reduce conflict while leaving the person’s agency, safety or preference unrepresented.
- **Boundary:** A later negative feeling does not alone prove earlier coercion, and an outward yes does not alone prove free consent. Context and the available refusal route matter.
- **Current connections:** IC-08 ME · IC-09 Conscious Participation · BE-02 Behaviour · BE-04 Responsibility and repair.

### HPR-05 · Surveillance, restriction and deprivation

- **Definition:** Control of information, movement, relationships, money, care, privacy, health, sexuality, children, legal status or other conditions in ways that narrow another person’s agency.
- **What to observe:** Monitoring, required reporting, blocked contact, financial limitation, sleep or care disruption, information withholding, interference with work or treatment, and punishment tied to access.
- **Why it matters:** Harm can be organised through ordinary resources and routines without a dramatic violent incident.
- **Boundary:** Safety planning, legitimate confidentiality, role-bounded supervision and mutually agreed coordination can involve limits. The test is whose safety or function the restriction serves, how far it reaches and whether it remains reviewable.
- **Current connections:** IC-01 Conditions · IC-07 Situation Signals · BE-03 Cost · HPR-01 Power and asymmetry · HPR-03 Coercion.

### HPR-06 · Epistemic harm and imposed reality

- **Definition:** Behaviour that persistently undermines a person’s ability to know, describe, trust or have others recognise their own experience.
- **What to observe:** Gaslighting, credibility attacks, strategic ambiguity, contradictory rules, selective evidence, audience recruitment, isolation from corroboration, DARVO and punishment for maintaining a separate account.
- **Why it matters:** Harm can affect not only what a person is allowed to do but what they can safely say, believe or know.
- **Boundary:** Disagreement, imperfect memory, different interpretation, uncertainty and correction are not automatically epistemic harm. Pattern, power, consequence and the treatment of independent evidence matter.
- **Current connections:** IC-05 reflective and symbolic processing · IC-07 Situation Signals · IC-08 ME · IC-09 Conscious Participation · BE-04 Responsibility and repair.

### HPR-07 · Instrumental social understanding

- **Definition:** Accurate or useful understanding of another person’s feelings, intentions, vulnerabilities or likely responses used to predict, influence or control rather than to support their welfare.
- **What to observe:** Tactical perspective-taking, selective reassurance, vulnerability targeting, anticipation of resistance and communication designed around another person’s fear or dependence.
- **Why it matters:** Understanding another person and caring about their welfare are separable capacities.
- **Boundary:** Mentalizing does not prove manipulation, and reduced affective sharing does not prove harm. The use of the information becomes visible through action, power and impact.
- **Current connections:** RC-02 Affective sharing · RC-03 Mentalizing · BE-01 Organising pull · BE-02 Behaviour.

### HPR-08 · Response to feedback

- **Definition:** What happens when another person introduces contradiction, impact, refusal, correction or a separate account.
- **What to observe:** Listening, checking, delay, revision, selective concession, dismissal, retaliation, punishment, discrediting, role reversal, intimidation and whether future truth-telling becomes more costly.
- **Why it matters:** Initial defensiveness is common. The stronger power question is whether the feedback source remains safe and able to retain their account.
- **Boundary:** Surprise, shame, anger, explanation, disagreement, withdrawal or delayed response do not establish Domination. Feedback can also be mistaken, manipulative or unsafe.
- **Current connections:** IC-10 Updating · NG-02 Chronic organisation · BE-02 Behaviour · BE-04 Responsibility and repair.

### HPR-09 · Pressure reduction versus completion

- **Definition:** Pressure reduction is any outcome that lowers immediate conflict or internal demand; completion involves the condition-relevant action, update or resolution that allows the protective route to relinquish its job.
- **What to observe:** Whether conflict stopped through safety, action, information and repair—or through compliance, silence, reassurance, regained access, withdrawal or exhaustion.
- **Why it matters:** The nervous system may register real relief when another person gives in, even though the relational problem and enforcement route remain unchanged.
- **Boundary:** Immediate relief is not false. It is simply insufficient evidence of resolution, repair or changed power use.
- **Current connections:** IC-10 Updating · IC-11 Return · NG-02 Chronic organisation · BE-03 Cost.

### HPR-10 · Responsibility and reparation

- **Definition:** Actor-side work that stops harmful enforcement, recognises specific impact, accepts appropriate consequences, addresses material or relational harm and changes future conduct.
- **What to observe:** Specific ownership, cessation, restitution, changed access, acceptance of boundaries, practical repair, learning and later behaviour under similar pressure.
- **Why it matters:** Feeling shame, producing an explanation or offering an apology may be meaningful without yet repairing what happened.
- **Boundary:** Accountability is not global self-condemnation. Collapse, self-punishment or demands for reassurance can recentre the actor without restoring another person’s agency.
- **Current connections:** IC-09 Conscious Participation · IC-10 Updating · IC-11 Return boundary · BE-04 Responsibility and repair.

### HPR-11 · Forgiveness, access and reconciliation

- **Definition:** Three separate outcomes: forgiveness concerns the harmed person’s relationship with what occurred; access concerns contact or participation; reconciliation concerns voluntary rebuilding of a relationship.
- **What to observe:** Whether apology becomes a claim on forgiveness, whether restitution attempts to purchase access, whether refusal is accepted and whether trust is allowed to remain absent.
- **Why it matters:** Reparation can continue without forgiveness, renewed access or reconciliation.
- **Boundary:** No completed repair script creates entitlement to contact, trust, reassurance or reunion. The harmed person’s recovery is not an instrument for completing the actor’s regulation.
- **Current connections:** NG-06 Connection / Belonging boundary · IC-11 Return boundary · BE-04 Responsibility and repair.

### HPR-12 · Recurrence under the next boundary

- **Definition:** What happens when a later refusal, contradiction, frustration, loss of access or loss of control recreates the conditions that previously preceded harm.
- **What to observe:** Whether enforcement returns, changes form, spreads across domains, becomes subtler, reduces or remains absent; whether impact continues to influence action when pressure rises again.
- **Why it matters:** Promises and temporary improvement are present evidence. Repeated conduct under comparable conditions provides stronger information about updating.
- **Boundary:** Recurrence is not required before someone may protect themselves. One serious act can justify distance, intervention or legal protection.
- **Current connections:** IC-10 Updating · NG-02 Chronic organisation · NG-04 persistence and flexibility · BE-02 Behaviour · BE-04 Responsibility and repair.

## Six distinctions for practical use

### Self-boundary versus command

- **Self-boundary:** “If shouting continues, I will leave.”
- **Command over another:** “You are not allowed to leave, disagree or speak to that person.”

A self-boundary governs my participation. A command governs yours. Either can still require contextual review, but they are not the same use of agency.

### Request versus coercive demand

A request leaves refusal sufficiently viable. A coercive demand makes refusal unsafe, punishing or practically impossible through an added use of power.

### Protective constraint versus deprivation as leverage

A protective constraint is tied to a defined risk and legitimate responsibility. Deprivation as leverage removes body needs, information, money, care, contact or access to obtain submission beyond that risk.

### Protective withdrawal versus punitive silence

Withdrawal can protect capacity, stop escalation or end unwanted contact. Silence becomes punitive when recognition or information is withheld to destabilise, punish, extract pursuit or make another person surrender their account.

### Persuasion versus manipulation

Persuasion can state its interest and permit refusal. Manipulation covertly engineers the option field through deception, pressure, vulnerability or hidden consequence.

### Decisive action versus closed revision

Decisive action can remain open to new evidence, stop when wrong and accept later review. Closed revision excludes contradiction or makes it costly regardless of outcome and impact.

## Harm can be loud or quiet

Harmful power use may involve visible force, anger or intimidation. It may also involve low-output, planned or socially acceptable behaviour:

- surveillance presented as care;
- isolation presented as loyalty;
- deprivation presented as a consequence;
- imposed urgency presented as necessity;
- strategic ambiguity presented as uncertainty;
- punitive silence presented as a boundary;
- credibility attacks presented as concern;
- forced dependence presented as support;
- role expansion presented as legitimate authority;
- apology presented as entitlement to renewed access.

The form does not settle the function. The surrounding power, option field, impact and recurrence help clarify what the behaviour is doing.

## Feedback as a change test

Feedback should be evaluated in six steps:

1. **Separate feedback from truth.** It may be accurate, partial, mistaken, manipulative, unsafe or badly timed.
2. **Observe initial access.** Could the person hear, remember, understand or hold the information at that time?
3. **Observe the treatment of the source.** Did contradiction remain safe, or was the person punished, discredited or restricted?
4. **Observe model revision.** Did the information change interpretation, action or later planning?
5. **Observe behavioural revision.** Did impact alter what happened next?
6. **Observe recurrence.** Did the update remain available under the next comparable pressure?

Agreement, apology or therapeutic language may occur without steps four to six.

## Four repair territories

| Territory | What belongs here | What does not follow automatically |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Actor-side reparation** | Stop, specific ownership, consequences, restitution and changed behaviour | Forgiveness, restored identity or renewed access |
| **Harmed-person recovery** | Safety, distance, support, justice, meaning, grief, forgiveness or none of these | Responsibility for reassuring, educating or rehabilitating the actor |
| **Relational repair** | Mutual recognition, negotiated repair and possible rebuilding | A duty to preserve the relationship |
| **Trust and reconciliation** | Voluntary renewed confidence and relationship over time | Completion merely because an apology or restitution occurred |

Repair can be real without reunion. Some consequences cannot be reversed. Changed conduct may still matter.

## Rules for connecting this material

1. Connect by claim ID, not by copying whole sections.
2. Begin with observable behaviour and power before inferring an internal organisation.
3. Keep Actual Conditions independent of the configured read.
4. Name whose agency, resources or options changed.
5. Record form, function, impact and recurrence separately.
6. Preserve legitimate authority, emergency action, self-boundaries and safe withdrawal as counterexamples.
7. Preserve low-output coercion; do not make visible activation a requirement.
8. Keep mentalizing, affective sharing, empathic concern and ethical action separate.
9. Do not require recurrence before protection is justified.
10. Do not treat explanation, apology or reduced conflict as evidence that power changed.

## Source-record extension for harmful-behaviour claims

The existing source template should add:

| Field | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Power location | What capacity, role, resource or credibility was unequally held? |
| Agency effect | Which choices became less available, more dangerous or more costly? |
| Behavioural form | What was observably done or withheld? |
| Proposed function | What outcome did the behaviour appear to secure, and how certain is that inference? |
| Domain | Body, movement, privacy, relationships, money, care, information, sexuality, legal status, work, children or another area |
| Impact | What happened to the person, relationship, material conditions or wider system? |
| Feedback response | What happened when the behaviour or impact was challenged? |
| Recurrence | Did the pattern repeat, change form, spread, reduce or stop? |
| Counterexample | What similar behaviour could occur without coercion or Domination? |
| Protection boundary | What evidence is enough to justify safety action without waiting for certainty or recurrence? |

## Working terminology decisions

- **Extreme Defensive Mobilisation** should describe the internal Position D organisation under review.
- **Domination** should describe an enforced power pattern, not the biological state.
- **Coercive control** should be used for patterned restriction of liberty or agency, not as a synonym for every controlling act.
- **Reduced agency** should name an effect on available choice, not a claim that the person had no agency at all.
- **Pressure reduction** should remain distinct from completion, Return and repair.
- **Reparation** should name actor-side work; **recovery**, **forgiveness**, **access** and **reconciliation** should remain separate.
