Human sociality · shared biological premise

Human Sociality and the Two Survival Functions

Humans survive and develop within social ecologies. Other people can alter access to care, resources, information, protection, effort and recovery. TEG-Blue uses that observation to distinguish two interacting ways viability can be served: through direct protection of the organism and through maintaining access to consequential others.

Central biological proposition

Relationship is not outside survival. For a social organism, it can be one of survival’s conditions.

Humans did not evolve as fully self-sufficient individuals who later added relationships for comfort. Across human life history, other people have participated in provisioning, childcare, learning, defence, cooperation, risk sharing and the regulation of demand. This does not make every relationship necessary or beneficial. It makes social availability biologically consequential.

Survival / viabilityThe organism remains alive, workable and able to participate.
Through direct protection

Organism-protection

Conditions require maintenance, restoration, distance, defence, escape, shelter, nourishment or another action that changes bodily demand or exposure.

  • bodily integrity and viable internal conditions
  • resources, distance, shelter and effective action
  • direct protection from harmful conditions
Through access to others

Connection-preservation

Access to a consequential person or group affects care, cooperation, recognition, information, shared effort, protection or the possibility of relational restoration.

  • availability, belonging and reciprocal access
  • care, shared information and coordinated action
  • recognition, accountability and repair where mutuality is required
↔

Early dependency couples both

For dependent young, caregiver connection may also provide warmth, nourishment, transport, protection, orientation and conditions for recovery. Preserving connection can therefore participate directly in organism-protection. F2 Entry 002 follows this developmental route.

Same ultimate purpose

Both functions can serve viability. The distinction concerns where the immediate problem sits and what kind of change could answer it.

Different possible answer-source

A boundary or environmental change can answer some direct-protection problems. Mutual recognition or repair cannot be produced by one person alone.

Not sociality as personality

Introversion, chosen solitude and independence remain ordinary. “Social animal” describes a species history and ecology, not a requirement for constant company.

Convergent grounding

Convergent evidence grounds the functional territory; TEG-Blue gives it an architectural form.

The question is not whether one paper has already named connection-preservation. The question is whether independent findings jointly support the biological importance of maintaining access to socially consequential others. They do. TEG-Blue then organises that grounded territory so it can operate consistently across the models, frameworks, diagrams and Signal Map. What research directly supports

Human dependency, attachment behaviour, cooperation, social buffering, interpersonal influence and consequential responses to social availability, loss and exclusion.

2 · Grounded synthesis

What convergence lets us conclude

Maintaining access to socially consequential others is a biologically meaningful problem for humans, even though its processes are distributed across fields and levels of explanation.

How the architecture organises it

Connection-preservation names that functional territory; organism-protection names the interacting territory of direct maintenance and protection. The terms can now be used consistently across TEG-Blue.

Prolonged dependency and cooperative care

Supported territory

Human children develop over a long period and receive care, provisioning and protection from networks that can extend beyond one parent. Comparative and developmental accounts treat cooperative care as important in human life history.

Boundary: cooperative-breeding accounts are evolutionary hypotheses with competing and composite explanations; they do not establish one origin story for human sociality.

Tomasello & Gonzalez-Cabrera, 2017

Cooperation, provisioning and shared risk

Supported territory

Human cooperation includes food sharing, care, information exchange and coordinated work among kin and non-kin. Field evidence also shows that the form of sharing changes with socio-ecological conditions.

Boundary: humans are not uniformly cooperative. Reciprocity, demand sharing, kinship, power, norms, conflict and ecology produce different arrangements.

Smith et al., 2016

Attachment and caregiver availability

Research programme

Attachment research studies how young children use familiar caregivers when alarmed and how separation, reunion, exploration and caregiver response vary under specified conditions.

Boundary: attachment classifications are not Gradient Positions, diagnoses, moral types or complete accounts of a relationship.

Granqvist et al., 2017

Social Baseline Theory

Active theory

Social Baseline Theory proposes that access to familiar, interdependent others participates in estimates of risk, resources and effort, rather than functioning only as an optional reward added to an otherwise solitary baseline.

Boundary: this is a theoretical synthesis, not proof that every brain expects the same relationships or that being alone necessarily means greater demand.

Coan & Sbarra, 2015

Social buffering and measured stress responses

Conditionally supported

Across species and developmental periods, the presence or assistance of another organism can alter measured responses to threat or challenge. Human effects depend on age, relationship, context and the outcome measured.

Boundary: buffering is not state transfer. Lower cortisol, lower neural threat response or visible settling does not establish completion, safety or beneficial regulation by itself.

Hostinar, Sullivan & Gunnar, 2014

Interpersonal emotion regulation

Research model

Another person's actions can alter the situation, attention, interpretation, expression or response processes through which emotion unfolds. The regulator and the person being affected remain separate participants.

Boundary: interpersonal influence is not automatically accurate, caring, consensual or helpful. It can also misread, suppress, escalate or control.

Reeck, Ames & Ochsner, 2016

Rejection, separation and social pain

Real distress · disputed mapping

Rejection, exclusion and loss can produce consequential distress, attention and action. Some studies report overlap with processes involved in physical pain; quantitative reviews contest a simple shared “pain matrix.”

Boundary: social pain should not be presented as physical injury, one neural circuit or proof that a particular relationship must be restored.

Cacioppo et al., 2013 · quantitative boundary

The two survival functions

TEG-Blue integration

TEG-Blue organises these converging territories into organism-protection and connection-preservation so the active problem, available information and completing condition can be distinguished across ESS, CLS, ME, the Fluid Positions, ESC and the Situation Signal families.

Evidence boundary: these are architectural survival functions, not claims of two dedicated anatomical systems or a one-to-one mapping between one function and one emotion, pathway or Gradient Position.

Apply the Science Grounding Protocol
Changing scale

A species-level dependency becomes a developmental condition, then a present event.

The same proposition changes meaning as the level of explanation changes. Keeping those levels separate prevents evolutionary plausibility from becoming a childhood story or a prediction about present behaviour.

F1 · repertoire

Social capacities become available

Caregiving, signalling, attachment, cooperation, learning and shared action belong to an inherited and culturally elaborated repertoire.

F2 · development

Other people change conditions

Caregivers provide resources and responses while the child's own organism senses, acts, reorganises and learns from what follows.

Models 1 + 2

Social information meets organisation

Another person's availability, expression and action enter the information field while present organisation changes access, priority and capacity.

ESC · event

Response becomes feedback

What the other person does may change the condition, support updating, leave demand open or become carryover into the next cycle.

Development can shape which relational routes become easier to recruit. It cannot be reconstructed from one adult response.

Relational conditions and Return

Another person can be part of the completing condition without becoming the only route to Return.

When the unresolved condition is mutual recognition, reciprocity, accountability, trust or repair, one person cannot produce relational restoration alone. The organism may still update to the relationship's unavailability through evidence, grief, an effective boundary or revised expectation.

Outcome 1

Relational restoration

The relevant people participate in recognition, reciprocity, accountability, changed conduct or repair. Mutuality is part of what changed.

Outcome 2

Organism-level updating and Return

Demand changes enough for recruitment to release and wider participation or restoration to become available. Another person may help without mutual repair being the active condition.

Outcome 3

Return without relational restoration

The relationship remains unavailable or unrepaired while grief, protection, revised expectation or reorganisation allows the organism to respond to that reality.

The Fluid Signal Map compares four relational cases; F2 Entry 003 records where the developmental completion distinction currently lives.

Fluid and Chronic boundary

Social dependence explains why relational information matters. It does not determine what repetition will produce.

Repeated non-response, unpredictability, punishment or role reversal may shape expectation, attention, expression, help-seeking and protective practice. But no single relational history produces one inevitable organisation, interpretation or behaviour.

Fluid question

Does organisation change as current social availability, uncertainty, threat or repair changes?

Chronic question

Have repeated outcomes altered thresholds, expectations, signal weighting, recovery or the routes that remain easy to use?

Behaviour boundary

History and organisation may constrain capacity. They do not establish intention, excuse harm or decide whether accountability and repair occurred.

This is why the social-animal premise reaches the Chronic Gradient and F3 only through learning, persistence and updating evidence—not by direct evolutionary inference.
