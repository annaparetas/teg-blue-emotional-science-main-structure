F4 · Shared Rules and Collective Organisation

How groups create, transmit, apply and revise explicit and implicit expectations, and how those expectations shape participation, belonging, behaviour and access. The first framework whose main unit of analysis is explicitly collective. It connects individual participation to social, cultural and institutional conditions without treating a group as one organism.

Does not establish
One person's developmental origin, the internal state behind compliance, the biological organisation of a group, a universal theory of culture, the moral value of every rule, the full structure of hierarchy, or the assessment of harm and coercion. Nor shared belief from compliance, collective nervous-system organisation from group behaviour, or institutional persistence from one person's adaptation.

The decision

Keep F4; revise its title and its movement from personal adaptation to group rule.

"Rules Regulate" preserved an important observation: rules do more than describe preferred behaviour. They can alter uncertainty, coordination, belonging, consequences and the options people realistically have. The phrase becomes misleading if regulation is read as calm, health or one nervous-system mechanism. A rule may support coordination or safety. It may also produce ambiguity, exclusion, concealment, unequal cost or coercive compliance. Different people may experience the same rule differently because their roles, resources and ability to refuse are different.

F4 therefore belongs at the social, cultural and systemic level of TEG-Blue. It can examine how collective expectations organise conditions. It should not turn a family, group, culture or institution into a single nervous system. The provisional title is Shared Rules and Collective Organisation.

The question F4 owns

How does an expectation become shared, authorised, enforced or revised, and whose options change?

How does an expectation become shared, repeated, authorised, enforced or revised within a group, and whose participation or options change as it operates?

F4 owns questions about

How explicit rules and implicit norms emerge; who communicates, models or authorises them; how people learn what is expected and what follows non-compliance; what coordination, protection, identity or continuity a rule supports; whose interpretation of the rule counts; how roles and power affect application; whether refusal, exception, disagreement and appeal remain possible; how a rule changes when conditions or consequences become visible; how practices continue across relationships, generations and institutions.

F4 does not own

One person's developmental origin, the internal state behind compliance, the biological organisation of a group, a universal theory of culture, the moral value of every rule, the full structure of hierarchy, or the assessment of harm and coercion. Those last two belong to F5 and to Harm, Power and Repair.

The core insight

Repeated expectations can become part of the conditions people organise within.

People often encounter rules before they can see that alternatives exist. Some rules are written. Others become visible only when someone breaks them. A child may learn that sadness is private because adults repeatedly redirect it. A team may learn that disagreement is costly because objections are ignored or punished. A community may preserve a practice because it carries knowledge, identity and continuity. These are all forms of social learning, but they are not the same process and do not have the same meaning.

Repeated expectations can become part of the conditions people organise within, especially when belonging, authority, resources or consequences make those expectations difficult to question.

Distinctions that follow

A shared expectation is not automatically an explicit rule. Repeated behaviour is not proof that everyone endorses the norm. Compliance is not consent. Tradition is not proof of legitimacy or harm. Coordination is not the same as conformity. A rule can protect some people while placing costs on others. Flexibility is not the absence of rules. Contestability is not the same as constant negotiation. A group pattern is not a shared Gradient Position. An individual's explanation cannot establish why an institution persists.

The six F4 construct families

Six questions replace "rules regulating".

FamilyGoverning questionBoundary
F4-A · Formation and sourceWhere did the expectation come from, who names it, and what problem or purpose is it understood to address?Origin stories may be disputed; present function cannot be inferred from historical origin.
F4-B · Transmission and participationHow do people learn, repeat, negotiate, resist or reinterpret the expectation?Repetition does not prove internalisation, agreement or one developmental mechanism.
F4-C · Coordination and collective functionWhat does the rule help the group organise: safety, care, timing, identity, knowledge, work, boundaries or continuity?A stated purpose may differ from actual effects, and useful coordination does not remove unequal cost.
F4-D · Authority, consequence and enforcementWho can require compliance, grant exceptions, impose consequences or make refusal costly?Authority is not automatically coercive; Harm, Power and Repair owns the enforcement threshold and the assessment of reduced agency.
F4-E · Distribution and differential effectWho gains predictability, access, recognition or resources, and who carries effort, silence, risk or exclusion?Unequal effect does not by itself establish intent; systematic worth-sorting hands to F5.
F4-F · Visibility, contestability and revisionCan the rule be named, questioned, appealed, tested against consequences and changed?Stability is not proof of rigidity, and change is not proof that repair or redistribution has occurred.

F4 is not a group nervous-system model

Four connected but distinct objects.

The original sentence, "What one nervous system does with a story, many nervous systems do with rules", creates a useful bridge but the wrong unit of explanation. Groups do not detect, feel, predict or Return as one organism. People within them may coordinate, imitate, disagree, comply, conceal, lead, resist or enforce. Documents, procedures, technologies, roles, money, architecture and law can also keep a pattern in place after the original participants have left.

  1. Individual participationHow one person follows, questions, repeats or resists.
  2. Interaction and relationship patternsWhat happens between people around the expectation.
  3. Shared meanings, norms and rolesThe field of interpretation through which conduct becomes understandable.
  4. Material and institutional arrangementsDocuments, procedures, resources, architecture and law.

Influence runs in more than one direction. An individual can help maintain or challenge a rule, while the rule changes the conditions, consequences and available actions that person encounters. Neither level can replace the other. "Boys don't cry" and "I don't do emotions" are not the same operation: one is a group norm and the other a personal statement, and either can have several meanings. The contrast shows how a person may repeat, resist or reinterpret a social norm without collapsing the levels.

Working vocabulary

Several terms rather than one broad use of "rule".

TermWorking meaningBoundary
Explicit ruleA stated expectation with a defined or implied scope, source and consequence.A written rule may be ignored, selectively applied or contradicted by practice.
Implicit normAn expectation communicated through approval, disapproval, repetition, modelling or anticipated social consequence.Similar behaviour does not prove shared belief or free agreement.
Role expectationBehaviour expected because of a person's place in a family, group, workplace or institution.A role can be negotiated, imposed, chosen, inherited or contested.
Practice or routineA repeated way of doing something that may organise participation without being stated as a rule.Repetition may reflect convenience, constraint or lack of alternatives rather than endorsement.
Policy or institutional ruleA formally authorised expectation connected to procedures, roles, resources or sanctions.Formal legitimacy does not settle fairness, proportionality or impact.
Cultural meaningA shared or contested field of interpretation through which conduct, identity and value become understandable.A culture is not internally uniform and cannot be represented by one rule or voice.

Working distinctions for architecture. Each will need claim-level grounding before it becomes an active F4 model.

A field of questions

Not one step in a fixed F3 to F7 escalation chain.

  1. Expectation, practice or ruleWhat is actually expected, stated or repeated.
  2. Source, stated purpose and scopeF4-A.
  3. Participation, authority and consequenceF4-B and F4-D.
  4. Coordination and differential effectsF4-C and F4-E.
  5. Contest, revision, persistence or enforcementF4-F.

"Flexible, rigid, surveilled, enforced" read as a universal escalation pathway, even though surveillance and enforcement can occur without those stages. The safer direction is separate dimensions: clarity, scope, discretion, monitoring, sanction, refusal and reviewability. Groups under threat may centralise, cooperate, fragment, protect exceptions or intensify enforcement depending on leadership, history, institutions and power, so threat-related rule change is a claim requiring specified conditions, measures and timescale. F6 and F7 may later add perception and enforcement questions, but F4 does not pre-commit them to a universal sequence.

How F4 connects to the whole

Reciprocal connections, and three handoffs that stay clear.

From F1, F2 and F3

F1 contributes broad capacities for social learning, communication, cooperation, competition, affiliation and collective action, but cannot establish the origin, function or legitimacy of a specific modern rule. F2 socialisation may shape how a person notices, interprets, follows or questions rules; caregivers are one important route within a wider social ecology of peers, institutions, media and material conditions. F3 adults interpret rules and choose among compliance, negotiation, refusal, concealment, enforcement or revision; the connection is reciprocal, not a scale-up from private coherence to public rule.

Across Model 1, Model 2 and the Chronic plane

A rule may be part of the present conditions a person accurately detects and organises within. The group does not occupy a Gradient Position, and compliance cannot reveal one person's nervous-system organisation. Repeated rule exposure may contribute to recurrent expectation, inhibited participation or difficult updating within a person, but institutional persistence and individual Chronic organisation are different phenomena and timescales.

To F5 and Harm, Power and Repair

To F5 when rules repeatedly sort worth, credibility, access, labour, protection or resources; not every division of role or difference in outcome is a worth hierarchy. To Harm, Power and Repair when power or imposed cost makes refusal, contradiction, exit or independent action meaningfully less possible; an unpopular or restrictive rule is not automatically coercive, and behaviour, power, proportionality, refusal and impact must be examined.

To F10, and Return and repair

To F10 when the question becomes how practices, stories, roles and material conditions continue or change across generations; intergenerational continuity is not automatically genetic or epigenetic transmission. A person may regain flexibility within a rule-bound context while a group separately revises its rule. Individual Return does not constitute institutional change, and policy change does not by itself repair accumulated harm.

F6 receives the observation that repeated norms may shape attention, credibility and what is treated as ordinary or thinkable; F4 cannot infer a hidden bias from rule-following alone, and F6 requires its own review. A personal pattern cannot be scaled directly into a collective rule; F4 requires group- and system-level evidence.

Boundaries and provenance

Narrowed wording, and the grounding route.

Narrowed or retired

"What one nervous system does with a story, many nervous systems do with rules." A rule as "whatever a group absorbs because it stabilises". "Boys don't cry" and "I don't do emotions" as the same operation. Rules becoming common sense because they stop being visible; people differently positioned may see the rule very clearly. Rules serving identifiable regulatory functions inferred from persistence. Rules tightening "predictably" as collective threat rises. Rules shaping children mainly through adults. Blocked expression creating a gap in the next generation's access to its own states; the statement moves too quickly from a social rule to an internal developmental outcome. "Cost: flexibility"; rules can reduce or increase flexibility depending on clarity, fit, scope, exception and revision.

Grounding fields

Sociology; social psychology; social and cultural anthropology; linguistic anthropology, sociolinguistics and communication science; political science and public administration; organisational science and industrial and organisational psychology; institutional and behavioural economics; law-and-society and sociolegal research; criminology and criminal-justice research; forensic and legal psychology, only within a psycholegal assessment; history and historical sociology; archaeology and material-culture studies; education research; public health and social epidemiology; human geography and demography; social work and community psychology; critical race, feminist, disability, decolonial and Indigenous scholarship; science and technology studies; ethics and political philosophy. Each claim names the particular discipline and level it needs. Priority evidence: ethnography, interviews, observation, surveys, experiments, social-network analysis, organisational and administrative records, policy evaluation, historical comparison and participatory research, comparing the stated rule with actual practice, identifying authority and sanctions, examining who can refuse or appeal, and tracing different effects across positions within the group.

Old map title: Rules Regulate. Evidence status of the original source list: a lineage inventory, not yet an F4 evidence base. Bourdieu and social reproduction offer a social and interpretive contribution, not a nervous-system mechanism. Norms, conformity and obedience research does not establish one universal path from pressure to compliance. System justification, terror management, authoritarianism and social dominance concern different constructs and levels and should not be combined into a single rule-tightening mechanism. "Pain circuitry" is too broad to explain why a group rule persists. Emotion socialisation research cannot by itself establish a permanent loss of access to emotion. Caregiving research in nonhuman animals cannot directly ground human intergenerational transmission of social rules.
