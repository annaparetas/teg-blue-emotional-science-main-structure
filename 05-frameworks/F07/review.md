## Decision

**Keep F7 as a Foundational Framework, but replace the proposed internal domination mechanism with a behavioural, relational and systemic account of coercive enforcement.**

Provisional title:

> **Coercive Enforcement, Power and Restricted Agency**

Suggested explanatory line:

> How power, threats, punishment, surveillance, restriction, deprivation or force can make another person's refusal or independent action meaningfully less possible.

The original framework is right to mark a threshold between internal pressure and making another person carry the solution. That threshold cannot be found in activation, shame, empathy, diagnosis or developmental history. It becomes visible in behaviour and in what happens to another person's options.

F7 therefore does not explain domination as a way of regulating the actor's nervous system. It asks how an outcome is enforced, what power makes enforcement possible, whose agency changes and what evidence is needed for protection, accountability and repair.

The Harm, Power and Repair layer already carries the detailed claim architecture. F7 should organise access to that work rather than create a competing mechanism.

## The question F7 owns

> When does influence, authority or control become coercive enforcement, through what use of power, and with what effect on another person's viable refusal, safety, credibility, resources and independent action?

F7 owns questions concerning:

- what outcome a person, group or institution sought to secure;
- what formal, relational, material, physical or informational power was available;
- whether a request became a demand through imposed or credible consequence;
- what happened after delay, disagreement, refusal, separation or contradiction;
- whether fear, punishment, surveillance, restriction, deprivation or force was used;
- whether consent remained informed, voluntary and open to revision;
- how the affected person's practical options changed;
- whether enforcement formed a pattern, crossed domains, adapted or escalated;
- how credibility, support and routes to protection were affected;
- what happened when impact or accountability was introduced;
- whether the behaviour stopped and whether agency, resources and safety were restored.

It does not own one hidden motive, a nervous-system configuration, a developmental origin, diagnosis, dangerousness prediction, legal guilt, the truth of every disputed event or completed repair.

## The governing distinction

The body can organise around protection. Behaviour remains branching.

A person may pause, leave, ask for support, negotiate, set a boundary, seek formal help, comply, appease, retaliate, threaten, restrict or use force. Internal pressure does not select the branch by itself.

The governing separation is:

```text
organism organisation
        ≠
observable behaviour pattern
        ≠
use of power and effect on another person's agency
```

This means:

- high activation does not establish coercion;
- calm behaviour does not establish safety;
- Chronic organisation does not establish abuse;
- reduced empathy does not establish harmful action;
- strong empathy does not establish restraint;
- a protective history does not remove impact or responsibility;
- harmful behaviour does not reveal one internal mechanism;
- explanation is not exoneration;
- legal findings and scientific explanations answer different questions.

## The enforcement threshold

Power exists in ordinary care, work, education, medicine, government and relationships. It may be legitimate and necessary. The question is how it is used and bounded.

F7 crosses into coercive enforcement when:

> **Fear, punishment, surveillance, restriction, deprivation, force or another power advantage is used to secure an outcome through another person's reduced agency.**

The threshold is behavioural and relational. Relevant context includes necessity, proportionality, scope, reciprocity, transparency, viable refusal, review, appeal and the least restrictive workable alternative.

One serious act may justify immediate protection. Recurrence can help establish a pattern or assess later conduct, but no one should have to wait for repetition before taking danger seriously. Legal definitions of pattern, intent and serious effect vary by jurisdiction and must be checked separately.

## Working vocabulary

| Term | Working meaning | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Power** | Unequal capacity to shape conditions, consequences, credibility, resources or another person's available options | Power is not automatically coercive; it may enable care, protection, coordination or repair |
| **Influence** | An attempt or effect that changes another person's view or action | Influence occurs in all relationships and does not by itself remove choice |
| **Persuasion** | Offering reasons, information or appeal while the other person retains a viable route to disagree | Repetition, deception, authority or consequence can change persuasion into pressure or enforcement |
| **Boundary** | A statement and action concerning one's own participation, access or protection | A command governing another person's independent action is not a self-boundary merely because it is called one |
| **Role-bounded authority** | Power attached to a defined responsibility, such as caregiving, clinical care, teaching or emergency protection | Authority must remain necessary, proportionate, reviewable and connected to the role's legitimate purpose |
| **Control** | Behaviour intended or functioning to direct an outcome | Structuring one's own action, coordinating a shared task and governing another person's options are different uses |
| **Coercion** | Use of credible threat, punishment, force or imposed cost to secure compliance | Every consequence is not coercion; the source, added cost, power and viable alternatives matter |
| **Coercive control** | A pattern of surveillance, isolation, degradation, intimidation, resource restriction or other tactics that narrows autonomy and practical routes | It is not established by decisiveness, jealousy, one argument or preference for order |
| **Domination** | A sustained relation or system in which one party's agency is subordinated through power and difficult-to-refuse control | The term has different sociological, political and legal uses and should be paired with observable evidence |
| **Compliance** | Behaviour that matches a request, rule or demand | Compliance is not proof of consent, agreement, safety or absence of fear |
| **Consent** | Sufficiently informed and voluntary agreement that remains open to revision or withdrawal | An outward yes does not settle consent when refusal carries credible punishment or deprivation |
| **Retaliation** | Imposed cost following refusal, contradiction, reporting, separation or accountability | Anger, disagreement or withdrawal does not establish retaliation without the behaviour and consequence |

## Eight F7 construct families

| Construct family | Governing question | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **F7-A · Power and asymmetry** | What role, resource, authority, credibility or practical advantage could shape the other person's conditions or options? | Asymmetry is not automatically harmful; use, limits and accountability matter |
| **F7-B · Sought outcome and claimed authority** | What result was being secured, and what right, duty, emergency or entitlement was offered as justification? | A legitimate goal does not validate every method used to achieve it |
| **F7-C · Enforcement means** | Were fear, threat, punishment, surveillance, restriction, deprivation, force, discrediting or another imposed cost used? | Pressure must be behaviourally described rather than inferred from tone, diagnosis or distress alone |
| **F7-D · Viable refusal and consent** | Could the person delay, disagree, leave, seek help or retain a separate account without added punishment? | Every choice has constraints; the question is whether power added cost to secure compliance |
| **F7-E · Pattern, scope and adaptation** | Did enforcement recur, spread across domains, change form, continue after separation or become harder to detect? | Pattern is not required before protection, and one repeated behaviour does not prove one internal mechanism |
| **F7-F · Impact and option-field restriction** | What changed in safety, movement, money, care, work, relationships, information, health, credibility or legal position? | Impact does not by itself settle intent, but intent is not required for the effect to matter |
| **F7-G · Social and institutional enablement** | Which norms, bystanders, procedures, technologies or institutions enabled, ignored, amplified or constrained the enforcement? | Institutional failure cannot be reduced to the actor's psychology or one bystander's attitude |
| **F7-H · Cessation, accountability and repair** | Did the enforcement stop, were options restored, was specific impact recognised, and did conduct change under later pressure? | Apology, shame, insight or temporary calm does not by itself establish repair |

These families are a routing architecture into HPR. They are not a risk score, legal test, stage model or validated psychological type.

## Domination is not an internal configuration

The original account proposes that domination emerges when threat, relief through control, reduced empathy and power combine into a self-reinforcing configuration. This appears explanatory but allows the framework to infer the actor's inner state from the harm.

F7 should reverse the order of inquiry:

```text
observable act or omission
        ↓
power and claimed authority
        ↓
means of enforcement
        ↓
effect on viable refusal and agency
        ↓
impact, recurrence and response to accountability
        ↓
only then: bounded hypotheses about contributing processes
```

Several processes may contribute to recurrence: reward, impunity, social approval, entitlement, material gain, habit, organisational incentive, ideology, fear, anticipated status loss or strategic calculation. None should be assumed in advance, and more than one may be present.

This order protects both sides of the inquiry. It does not use nervous-system language to soften harm, and it does not use evidence of harm to invent a diagnosis or developmental story.

## Reading the original F7 language against the current system

### Preserve, with precise translation

| Original direction | Current reading |
| --- | --- |
| There is a threshold between seeking safety and making another person provide it | Internal need becomes an HPR question when power or imposed cost reduces another person's agency to secure an outcome |
| Control can recur when it achieves an immediate result | Compliance, silence, restored access, avoided consequence or institutional impunity may make a route easier to repeat; the actual contingency must be observed |
| Boundaries can reveal an enforcement pattern | What follows refusal, separation or contradiction provides important evidence about viable agency and retaliation |
| Apology may not equal repair | Language, emotion, restitution, restored options, accepted limits and later conduct should be assessed separately |
| Institutions can amplify harm | Rules, incentives, authority, silence, credibility and weak accountability may enable enforcement beyond one relationship |
| Explanation and harm can both matter | Contributing processes may be investigated without cancelling impact, protection or responsibility |

### Narrow, revise or retire

| Map wording or implication | Why it needs care | Safer direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Domination regulates” | It makes the actor's proposed internal function the definition of the behaviour | Define coercive enforcement through power, means, option restriction and impact; investigate function separately |
| “Domination is built, not born” | It offers a universal developmental conclusion without specifying pathways or evidence | Behaviour may be shaped and revised; biological, developmental, relational, cultural and strategic contributions require separate claims |
| Sustained threat makes control produce the “fastest relief” | It assumes threat, motive and reinforcement after observing control | Record the condition, behaviour, consequence and recurrence before proposing relief as one route |
| The hinge is “I will make you behave so I can feel safe” | The phrase may describe some lived accounts but cannot identify coercion from motive | Ask what outcome was demanded, what power was used and what happened to refusal |
| Domination has “no internal brake” | It implies inevitability and removes the importance of choice, context, accountability and external constraint | Examine response to feedback, consequences, boundaries, monitoring and later comparable situations |
| Harm normally produces shame, guilt or remorse that corrects behaviour | These experiences vary and do not guarantee repair; some repair occurs without a particular feeling | Measure cessation, ownership, restitution, restored agency and later conduct directly |
| A domination configuration “cannot process” corrective emotions | It infers an internal incapacity and risks becoming a diagnosis or excuse | State what the person did when impact was introduced and what information remained usable |
| Power makes consulting the body unnecessary | Power changes available action and consequence; it does not establish bodily access or absence | Keep power behavioural and material; assess inner participation separately if relevant |
| Control builds tolerance like a substance | The addiction analogy does not establish coercive escalation and can obscure material gain, entitlement, strategy and impunity | Retire the tolerance claim unless a specific process and matching evidence are identified |
| Institutions select for a nervous-system configuration | Institutions may reward, enable, conceal or fail to constrain behaviours without selecting one biological type | Examine hiring, incentives, authority, complaints, oversight, protection and consequences |
| “Cost: everything” | The phrase is rhetorically strong but analytically empty | Name whose body, safety, agency, credibility, resources, relationships and future options carry each cost |

## Evidence status of the original source list

The original list combines learning theory, power experiments, empathy research, neuroscience, addiction analogy, coercive-control research and organisational theory. These fields do not establish one traceable domination mechanism.

- **Operant and negative reinforcement** may help explain recurrence only when the behaviour and immediate consequence are observed. Relief is not the definition of coercion.
- **Power research** suggests that perspective-taking and other-orientation vary with goals, role construction and accountability. Power does not inevitably reduce concern.
- **Affective sharing and mentalizing research** shows that social understanding has separable components. Neither low affect sharing nor accurate mentalizing predicts coercive behaviour by itself.
- **Lesion research** should not be used to explain ordinary domination or infer a brain mechanism from harmful conduct.
- **The allostatic model of addiction** does not ground an escalation-and-tolerance account of coercive control. The analogy should be retired from F7.
- **Coercive-control, violence and stalking research** are the central empirical and conceptual territories because they examine tactics, patterns, autonomy, credible threat and practical restriction.
- **Moral disengagement** may inform some justifications or reductions in self-sanction. It is not a complete cause of institutional or interpersonal harm.
- **Institutional isomorphism** concerns organisational similarity and diffusion, not selection for a harmful psychological configuration.
- **Systems and leverage-point work** may guide institutional intervention but should not be presented as direct evidence of individual mechanism.

HPR and Pilots 05, 06 and 09 provide a stronger existing foundation than the original F7 alignment. Dedicated source review is still needed for institutional domination, violence prevention, bystander systems, risk assessment and repair outcomes.

## F7's handoffs

F7 does not form the final step of a fixed F3–F7 escalation chain. It begins whenever behaviour and power cross the enforcement threshold, regardless of which internal route preceded it.

The clean assessment sequence is:

```text
behaviour + sought outcome
        ↓
available power + claimed authority
        ↓
enforcement means + response to refusal
        ↓
change in option field, safety and agency
        ↓
impact + recurrence + institutional response
        ↓
protection, cessation, accountability and possible repair
```

Three handoffs matter most:

1. **To immediate protection and appropriate services:** when there is credible danger, coercion, violence, stalking, deprivation or another serious restriction. Framework interpretation must not delay practical protection.
2. **To HPR:** for the full assessment of power, agency, behaviour, impact, feedback, responsibility and reparation.
3. **To F8:** only after harmful enforcement has stopped sufficiently for repair work to be possible. F8 cannot turn self-understanding into renewed access, trust or reconciliation.

## Result of this pass

- **Framework status:** retain, relocate and substantially reframe.
- **Collection role:** behavioural and systemic threshold framework that routes from internal and social explanation into direct assessment of power, enforcement and restricted agency.
- **Title:** revise from “Domination Regulates” to “Coercive Enforcement, Power and Restricted Agency.”
- **Domination:** retain only as a carefully defined relational or systemic recognition term, not a nervous-system configuration or person-type.
- **Core architecture:** use eight construct families—power, sought outcome, enforcement means, viable refusal, pattern, impact, enabling systems, and cessation and repair.
- **Governing foundation:** Harm, Power and Repair; F7 should not create a competing mechanism.
- **Main protection:** do not infer coercion from activation or diagnosis, infer internal mechanism from harm, or allow explanation to replace protection and responsibility.
- **F6 connection:** perception does not inevitably become enforcement; F7 begins with observed behaviour and power.
- **F8 connection:** self-reconnection is not relational repair, and no repair process creates entitlement to access or reconciliation.
- **Evidence status:** architectural first pass with strong internal companions; legal, violence, institutional and intervention claims still require jurisdiction- and claim-specific grounding.

## Grounding science and knowledge fields

F7 needs disciplines that can examine behaviour, power, law, violence, institutions, impact and repair. Biological and psychological research may inform contributing processes, but they cannot identify coercion without relational and behavioural evidence.

| Field | What it can ground in F7 | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sociology of power, family, organisations and institutions** | Authority, dependence, social control, role, institutional enablement and patterned inequality | A nervous-system state or one person's motive inferred from social position |
| **Criminology and criminal-justice research** | Offending patterns, coercion, stalking, violence, enforcement, victimisation, prevention and system response | Guilt, dangerousness or individual prediction from a category or group association |
| **Violence, coercive-control and victimology research** | Tactics, patterns, credible threat, autonomy restriction, post-separation behaviour, impact and help-seeking | One universal perpetrator type, victim response or developmental origin |
| **Forensic and legal psychology** | Psycholegal assessment, testimony, memory, risk communication, legal decision-making and evidence limits | A lie detector, motive reader or diagnosis-based conclusion about coercion or truth |
| **Law-and-society and sociolegal research** | Legal definitions, rights, procedural access, institutional practice, evidence and unequal remedy | That a legal threshold is universal, scientifically complete or identical to ethical harm |
| **Political science and political sociology** | State power, authoritarianism, repression, legitimacy, institutions and collective enforcement | Interpersonal coercion explained directly through political theory |
| **Human-rights research and ethics** | Liberty, dignity, bodily integrity, due process, non-discrimination and state or institutional duty | Empirical prevalence, causal mechanism or legal finding without corresponding evidence |
| **Social psychology** | Obedience, conformity, entitlement, retaliation, moral disengagement, bystander response and power effects | A complete account of coercion, institution or individual dangerousness from an experiment |
| **Organisational science and institutional research** | Authority, incentives, complaints, silence, whistleblowing, oversight, impunity and organisational change | That one organisational climate reflects every member's private psychology |
| **Public health and epidemiology** | Prevalence, risk and protective factors, health outcomes, prevention and service access at population level | An individual case conclusion or unique mechanism from a population association |
| **Clinical psychology and psychiatry** | Trauma effects, distress, treatment needs, behaviour-change interventions and comorbidity | Abuse, coercion, responsibility or risk established by diagnosis |
| **Developmental and family research** | Children's exposure, caregiving authority, psychological control, development and intergenerational context | That being exposed to control predicts later coercive behaviour or one inevitable outcome |
| **Social work and safeguarding research** | Multi-agency response, practical safety, service systems, dependency, capacity, protection and survivor-defined needs | Independent proof of every mechanism used by a practice model |
| **Anthropology and cross-cultural violence research** | Cultural meaning, kinship, authority, community response, variation and local routes to protection | Culture used to excuse harm or treated as internally uniform |
| **Gender, critical race, disability, queer, decolonial and Indigenous scholarship** | How power, dependency, credibility, state systems and access to protection vary across social positions | One generic oppression theory or a uniform experience within any group |
| **Economics and political economy** | Resource dependence, labour, housing, debt, economic abuse, incentives and institutional distribution | Human behaviour reduced to rational choice or coercion inferred from poverty alone |
| **Communication, discourse and media research** | Threats, strategic ambiguity, credibility attacks, audience recruitment, propaganda and reporting environments | Intent or truth settled by wording alone |
| **Technology-facilitated abuse and cybersecurity research** | Monitoring, location tracking, account access, impersonation, platform design and digital restriction | That technology caused the abuse or that one technical indicator establishes the full pattern |
| **Behavioural and learning sciences** | Reinforcement, punishment, habit, deterrence and response to consequence under specified conditions | A master mechanism for domination or exoneration through learned behaviour |
| **Neuroscience and affective science** | Bounded questions about stress, empathy components, decision and response to others' distress | Coercion, remorse, intent, responsibility or dangerousness inferred from brain or physiology |

### Priority evidence

F7 should favour behaviourally specified and context-rich evidence: contemporaneous records, reports from affected people, observation, digital and financial records, institutional documents, longitudinal pattern data, legal and administrative evidence, validated risk tools used within their actual limits, and research on later conduct under comparable conditions. Qualitative and survivor-led research is essential for identifying hidden restrictions and meaningful outcomes. Every assessment should record the power available, demanded outcome, response to refusal, imposed cost, changed options, impact, recurrence, jurisdiction and response to accountability.
