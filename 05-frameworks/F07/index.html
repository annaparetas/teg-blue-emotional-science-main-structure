F7 · Coercive Enforcement, Power and Restricted Agency

How power, threats, punishment, surveillance, restriction, deprivation or force can make another person's refusal or independent action meaningfully less possible. F7 marks the threshold between internal pressure and making another person carry the solution. That threshold cannot be found in activation, shame, empathy, diagnosis or developmental history. It becomes visible in behaviour and in what happens to another person's options.

The decision

Keep F7; replace the internal domination mechanism with an account of coercive enforcement.

"Domination Regulates" explained domination as a way of regulating the actor's nervous system. The original framework is right to mark a threshold between internal pressure and making another person carry the solution, but that threshold cannot be found in activation, shame, empathy, diagnosis or developmental history. F7 therefore asks how an outcome is enforced, what power makes enforcement possible, whose agency changes and what evidence is needed for protection, accountability and repair.

The Harm, Power and Repair layer already carries the detailed claim architecture. F7 organises access to that work rather than creating a competing mechanism. It is a behavioural and systemic threshold framework, an explanatory lens that routes from internal and social explanation into direct assessment of power, enforcement and restricted agency.

The question F7 owns

When does influence, authority or control become coercive enforcement?

When does influence, authority or control become coercive enforcement, through what use of power, and with what effect on another person's viable refusal, safety, credibility, resources and independent action?

F7 owns questions about

What outcome a person, group or institution sought to secure; what formal, relational, material, physical or informational power was available; whether a request became a demand through imposed or credible consequence; what happened after delay, disagreement, refusal, separation or contradiction; whether fear, punishment, surveillance, restriction, deprivation or force was used; whether consent remained informed, voluntary and open to revision; how the affected person's practical options changed; whether enforcement formed a pattern, crossed domains, adapted or escalated; how credibility, support and routes to protection were affected; what happened when impact or accountability was introduced; whether the behaviour stopped and whether agency, resources and safety were restored.

F7 does not own

One hidden motive, a nervous-system configuration, a developmental origin, diagnosis, dangerousness prediction, legal guilt, the truth of every disputed event or completed repair. F7 does not form the final step of a fixed F3 to F7 escalation chain. It begins whenever behaviour and power cross the enforcement threshold, regardless of which internal route preceded it.

The governing distinction

The body can organise around protection. Behaviour remains branching.

A person may pause, leave, ask for support, negotiate, set a boundary, seek formal help, comply, appease, retaliate, threaten, restrict or use force. Internal pressure does not select the branch by itself.

  1. Organism organisationIs not the same as
  2. Observable behaviour patternIs not the same as
  3. Use of power and effect on another person's agencyThe level at which F7 works.

What this means

High activation does not establish coercion. Calm behaviour does not establish safety. Chronic organisation does not establish abuse. Reduced empathy does not establish harmful action. Strong empathy does not establish restraint. A protective history does not remove impact or responsibility. Harmful behaviour does not reveal one internal mechanism. Explanation is not exoneration. Legal findings and scientific explanations answer different questions.

The enforcement threshold

Power exists in ordinary life; the question is how it is used and bounded.

Power exists in ordinary care, work, education, medicine, government and relationships. It may be legitimate and necessary.

F7 crosses into coercive enforcement when

Fear, punishment, surveillance, restriction, deprivation, force or another power advantage is used to secure an outcome through another person's reduced agency.

The threshold is behavioural and relational. Relevant context includes necessity, proportionality, scope, reciprocity, transparency, viable refusal, review, appeal and the least restrictive workable alternative. One serious act may justify immediate protection. Recurrence can help establish a pattern or assess later conduct, but no one should have to wait for repetition before taking danger seriously. Legal definitions of pattern, intent and serious effect vary by jurisdiction and must be checked separately.

Working vocabulary

Twelve terms, each with a boundary.

TermWorking meaningBoundary
PowerUnequal capacity to shape conditions, consequences, credibility, resources or another person's available options.Not automatically coercive; it may enable care, protection, coordination or repair.
InfluenceAn attempt or effect that changes another person's view or action.Occurs in all relationships and does not by itself remove choice.
PersuasionOffering reasons, information or appeal while the other person retains a viable route to disagree.Repetition, deception, authority or consequence can change persuasion into pressure or enforcement.
BoundaryA statement and action concerning one's own participation, access or protection.A command governing another person's independent action is not a self-boundary merely because it is called one.
Role-bounded authorityPower attached to a defined responsibility, such as caregiving, clinical care, teaching or emergency protection.Must remain necessary, proportionate, reviewable and connected to the role's legitimate purpose.
ControlBehaviour intended or functioning to direct an outcome.Structuring one's own action, coordinating a shared task and governing another person's options are different uses.
CoercionUse of credible threat, punishment, force or imposed cost to secure compliance.Every consequence is not coercion; the source, added cost, power and viable alternatives matter.
Coercive controlA pattern of surveillance, isolation, degradation, intimidation, resource restriction or other tactics that narrows autonomy and practical routes.Not established by decisiveness, jealousy, one argument or preference for order.
DominationA sustained relation or system in which one party's agency is subordinated through power and difficult-to-refuse control.The term has different sociological, political and legal uses and should be paired with observable evidence.
ComplianceBehaviour that matches a request, rule or demand.Not proof of consent, agreement, safety or absence of fear.
ConsentSufficiently informed and voluntary agreement that remains open to revision or withdrawal.An outward yes does not settle consent when refusal carries credible punishment or deprivation.
RetaliationImposed cost following refusal, contradiction, reporting, separation or accountability.Anger, disagreement or withdrawal does not establish retaliation without the behaviour and consequence.

The eight F7 construct families

Eight questions route into Harm, Power and Repair.

FamilyGoverning questionBoundary
F7-A · Power and asymmetryWhat role, resource, authority, credibility or practical advantage could shape the other person's conditions or options?Asymmetry is not automatically harmful; use, limits and accountability matter.
F7-B · Sought outcome and claimed authorityWhat result was being secured, and what right, duty, emergency or entitlement was offered as justification?A legitimate goal does not validate every method used to achieve it.
F7-C · Enforcement meansWere fear, threat, punishment, surveillance, restriction, deprivation, force, discrediting or another imposed cost used?Pressure must be behaviourally described rather than inferred from tone, diagnosis or distress alone.
F7-D · Viable refusal and consentCould the person delay, disagree, leave, seek help or retain a separate account without added punishment?Every choice has constraints; the question is whether power added cost to secure compliance.
F7-E · Pattern, scope and adaptationDid enforcement recur, spread across domains, change form, continue after separation or become harder to detect?Pattern is not required before protection, and one repeated behaviour does not prove one internal mechanism.
F7-F · Impact and option-field restrictionWhat changed in safety, movement, money, care, work, relationships, information, health, credibility or legal position?Impact does not by itself settle intent, but intent is not required for the effect to matter.
F7-G · Social and institutional enablementWhich norms, bystanders, procedures, technologies or institutions enabled, ignored, amplified or constrained the enforcement?Institutional failure cannot be reduced to the actor's psychology or one bystander's attitude.
F7-H · Cessation, accountability and repairDid the enforcement stop, were options restored, was specific impact recognised, and did conduct change under later pressure?Apology, shame, insight or temporary calm does not by itself establish repair.

These families are a routing architecture into Harm, Power and Repair. They are not a risk score, legal test, stage model or validated psychological type. Harm, Power and Repair provides F7's governing definitions, twelve claim families, enforcement threshold and repair boundaries; F7 does not duplicate or overrule that evidence architecture.

Domination is not an internal configuration

Reverse the order of inquiry: start from the observable act.

The original account proposed that domination emerges when threat, relief through control, reduced empathy and power combine into a self-reinforcing configuration. This appears explanatory but allows the framework to infer the actor's inner state from the harm. F7 reverses the order.

  1. Observable act or omissionWhat was done or withheld.
  2. Power and claimed authorityWhat made it possible and what justified it.
  3. Means of enforcementFear, threat, punishment, surveillance, restriction, deprivation, force, discrediting.
  4. Effect on viable refusal and agencyWhat happened to the other person's options.
  5. Impact, recurrence and response to accountabilityWhat changed, whether it recurred, what followed when impact was named.
  6. Only then: bounded hypotheses about contributing processesReward, impunity, social approval, entitlement, material gain, habit, organisational incentive, ideology, fear, anticipated status loss or strategic calculation. None assumed in advance; more than one may be present.

Dominance and Domination are not the same word

In this structure, Dominance names an internal mode that a person may read in their own self-reflection, described in the Inner Compass four modes. Domination names observable conduct: a sustained relation or system in which one party's agency is subordinated through power and difficult-to-refuse control. Neither establishes the other. A reading of one's own mode does not establish coercive conduct, and observed coercive conduct does not reveal a mode, a nervous-system configuration or a person-type. Domination is retained only as a carefully defined relational or systemic recognition term. This order protects both sides of the inquiry: it does not use nervous-system language to soften harm, and it does not use evidence of harm to invent a diagnosis or developmental story.

Boundaries and provenance

Retired wording, and the grounding route.

Narrowed or retired

"Domination regulates." "Domination is built, not born." Sustained threat makes control produce the "fastest relief." The hinge as "I will make you behave so I can feel safe." Domination has "no internal brake." Harm normally produces shame, guilt or remorse that corrects behaviour. A domination configuration "cannot process" corrective emotions. Power makes consulting the body unnecessary. Control builds tolerance like a substance; the addiction analogy is retired unless a specific process and matching evidence are identified. Institutions select for a nervous-system configuration. "Cost: everything," replaced by naming whose body, safety, agency, credibility, resources, relationships and future options carry each cost.

Grounding fields

Sociology of power, family, organisations and institutions; criminology and criminal-justice research; violence, coercive-control and victimology research; forensic and legal psychology; law-and-society and sociolegal research; political science; human-rights research and ethics; social psychology; organisational and institutional research; public health and epidemiology; clinical psychology and psychiatry; developmental and family research; social work and safeguarding; anthropology and cross-cultural violence research; gender, critical race, disability, queer, decolonial and Indigenous scholarship; economics and political economy; communication and media research; technology-facilitated abuse and cybersecurity research; behavioural and learning sciences; neuroscience and affective science, for bounded questions only. Biological and psychological research may inform contributing processes; it cannot identify coercion without relational and behavioural evidence.

Priority evidence

Behaviourally specified and context-rich evidence: contemporaneous records, reports from affected people, observation, digital and financial records, institutional documents, longitudinal pattern data, legal and administrative evidence, validated risk tools used within their actual limits, and research on later conduct under comparable conditions. Qualitative and survivor-led research is essential. Every assessment should record the power available, demanded outcome, response to refusal, imposed cost, changed options, impact, recurrence, jurisdiction and response to accountability.

Evidence status of the original source list

The original list combined learning theory, power experiments, empathy research, neuroscience, addiction analogy, coercive-control research and organisational theory. These fields do not establish one traceable domination mechanism. Coercive-control, violence and stalking research are the central empirical and conceptual territories. Lesion research should not explain ordinary domination; the allostatic model of addiction does not ground an escalation-and-tolerance account; institutional isomorphism concerns diffusion, not selection for a harmful configuration. Harm, Power and Repair and Pilots 05, 06 and 09 provide a stronger existing foundation than the original alignment.
