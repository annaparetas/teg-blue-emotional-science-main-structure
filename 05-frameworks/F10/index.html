F10 · Intergenerational Pathways, Caregiving and Change

How health, caregiving, learning, relationships, resources, institutions and cultural practices may remain continuous or change across generations. A person's history does not dictate what they will do with a child, and children do not receive one hidden parental state: they encounter behaviour, conditions, stories and the wider circumstances of the people who care for them. F10 names the routes separately and asks what changed, for whom, through which process and with what evidence across time.

The decision

Keep F10; replace one processed-versus-inherited claim with a route-specific account.

The original framework notices something important. A person's history does not dictate what they will do with a child. People may reflect, learn, seek support, change their behaviour, repair harm and create conditions they did not receive themselves. Family and social patterns are therefore neither fixed nor inevitable.

The difficulty lies in describing every route as the adult's unprocessed experience reaching the child. Children do not receive one hidden parental state. They encounter behaviour, touch, voice, attention, absence, routines, limits, conflict, repair, stories, housing, food, health, school, community, law and the wider conditions affecting the people who care for them. Prenatal and possible germline routes raise different scientific questions again. F10 names these routes separately, so that it can ask what changed, for whom, through which process and with what evidence across time.

The question F10 owns

Across generations, which conditions remain continuous or change, and what evidence connects them?

Across generations, which biological, relational, behavioural, material, cultural and institutional conditions remain continuous or change, and what evidence connects a change in one generation to an outcome in another?

F10 owns questions about

Who the generations and caregiving figures are; what condition, behaviour, resource, rule, story, exposure or outcome is said to continue; whether the route involves genetic inheritance, prenatal exposure, possible epigenetic processes, caregiving, learning, family roles, material conditions, culture or institutions; what the child directly experiences rather than what is inferred from an adult's private state; how children differ in temperament, health, disability, neurodivergence, communication and response; how children and caregivers affect one another over time; which wider conditions support or constrain caregiving; whether harm stopped, repair occurred and changed behaviour remained available later; what support, protection, policy, treatment, education or material intervention altered the route; which outcomes changed and which did not; and whether connection to a biological family or lineage is wanted, safe and freely chosen.

F10 does not own

A universal parenting standard, prediction of a child's future, reconstruction of a parent's history, diagnosis, proof of inherited trauma, biological destiny, assignment of blame to one caregiver, a requirement to preserve family contact, or a promise that one adult's healing prevents every later difficulty. Understanding how a pattern arrived can widen context and compassion; it does not make relationship obligatory.

The governing separation

Each arrow is a question, not a conclusion.

The word transmission can make unlike processes look as though they travel through one channel. F10 keeps six positions apart.

  1. Adult historyWhat happened to the adult, in their own generation.
  2. Adult present organisation or meaningNot the same as the history, and not the caregiving act.
  3. Caregiving behaviour and available supportWhat is done, and what help is there when capacity is low.
  4. Conditions the child directly encountersBehaviour, routines, resources, rules, stories, danger.
  5. The child's developing responseShaped by the child's own health, temperament and needs.
  6. The child's later outcomeFollowed across context and development, not assumed.

A parent may understand their history and still need practical support to behave differently under exhaustion or pressure. Another may have little language for their history and provide reliable care. A child may experience difficulty despite sensitive caregiving, or do well across important domains despite serious adversity. F10 therefore uses intergenerational pathway only when the generations, exposure, intermediate process, context and outcome are named.

Working vocabulary

Words that are not interchangeable.

Generations

Generation is a specified position in a family, population or historical sequence; parent, caregiver, grandparent, child, cohort and descendants are not interchangeable. Intergenerational involves effects between adjacent or identified generations and is not shorthand for genetic or epigenetic inheritance. Multigenerational involves three or more generations; presence across generations does not establish one mechanism. Transgenerational is a biological research term for effects beyond directly exposed generations and should not be used loosely for family patterns or cultural continuity.

Biological routes

Genetic inheritance carries DNA sequence variation, not a memory, social rule, family narrative or specific traumatic event. Prenatal or perinatal exposure acts during pregnancy, birth or the early postnatal period; a fetus exposed during pregnancy is directly exposed, which is not proof of germline inheritance. An epigenetic process regulates gene activity without changing sequence and does not by itself establish causation, permanence, trauma specificity or transmission. Germline epigenetic inheritance has stronger evidence in some animal models than in humans, where confounding routes are difficult to separate.

Caregiving and learning

Caregiving is behaviour and conditions providing protection, nourishment, attention, guidance, limits, communication and support; it is not one person's nervous-system state, and it occurs within material and institutional conditions. Emotion socialisation is not a simple transfer of the caregiver's feeling into the child. In modelling and social learning, resemblance does not prove imitation, and different children may learn different things from the same event. A family narrative may be coherent without being accurate, embodied, safe or behaviourally consequential.

Measures, rupture, lineage

Attachment representation is a research construct measured in specified ways, not a fixed identity or a general measure of parental health. Parental reflective functioning is a measured capacity; verbal reflection does not guarantee accuracy, sensitivity, safe behaviour or a particular child outcome. Rupture ranges from minor mismatch to abuse, which require different levels of analysis. Repair is not mere reconnection; serious harm requires cessation, accountability and protection. Intergenerational change in one route does not mean all routes changed. Lineage creates no automatic entitlement to access, loyalty, forgiveness or reunion.

The eight F10 construct families

A route inventory, not a family score.

FamilyGoverning questionBoundary
F10-A · Generations, history and claimed continuityWho and what are being connected across which period, and what evidence shows continuity rather than resemblance?A repeated outcome does not establish that the same process produced it in each generation.
F10-B · Biological and developmental pathwaysDo genetic variation, prenatal conditions, birth, health, nutrition, toxic exposure or a specifically proposed epigenetic route contribute?Biological evidence cannot stand in for caregiving, culture or material conditions, and a molecular association does not establish inheritance.
F10-C · Caregiving behaviour and interactionWhat happens in protection, responsiveness, conflict, limit-setting, support, mismatch and repair over time?The adult's stated intention or internal organisation cannot substitute for observed behaviour and the child's experience.
F10-D · Learning, language and family meaningWhat is modelled, reinforced, named, hidden, narrated or made costly to question?Words and stories are not disembodied channels, and silence does not reveal one motive or unprocessed emotion.
F10-E · Roles, power, harm and protectionWho carries responsibility, care, secrecy, authority or blame, and what happens to refusal, reporting and boundaries?Family-systems language must not distribute responsibility so widely that an actor's coercion or a child's protection disappears.
F10-F · Material, community and institutional continuityWhich housing, income, work, education, healthcare, discrimination, law, migration, violence, community and policy conditions persist or change?Structural conditions cannot be reduced to parenting quality or one family's psychology.
F10-G · Child contribution, variation and developmental outcomeHow do the child's health, temperament, disability, neurodivergence, communication, relationships and action affect the developing interaction?Bidirectionality does not make a child responsible for an adult's harmful behaviour or remove the caregiver's duty of care.
F10-H · Intervention, support and observed changeWhich protection, treatment, parenting support, resource, relationship, school, community or policy change altered which route and outcome?Improvement does not prove that one intervention repaired the whole family or permanently prevented recurrence.

These construct families are a route inventory, not a family score, inheritance model or sequence through which every family must pass. They are the core architecture of an architectural first pass; each still needs route- and population-specific grounding.

Several routes can cross the same generation boundary

The routes can interact. They should not be merged.

Biological

DNA sequence: inherited variation relevant to health, development or temperament. Prenatal and perinatal conditions: nutrition, illness, substances, stress-related physiology, healthcare, toxic exposure and birth conditions. Possible germline or other epigenetic routes: exposure-related regulatory effects requiring evidence beyond ordinary genetic and environmental continuity.

Relational and learned

Caregiving and relationship: availability, protection, responsiveness, conflict, coercion, repair, touch, routine and support. Social learning and language: modelling, reinforcement, emotion talk, warnings, silence, stories and expectations. Family roles and power: responsibility, loyalty, secrecy, hierarchy, access, credibility and response to boundaries.

Material and institutional

Material conditions: income, housing, food, work, healthcare, education, care labour and inherited resources or debt. Community, culture and institution: discrimination, violence, migration, law, religion, school, policy, collective memory and sources of belonging or resistance.

Children encounter behaviour and conditions, not intention alone

Wanting to provide safety is not the same as providing it. Children may respond to what adults repeatedly do when need, distress, disagreement or error appears; to voice, face, posture, touch, timing and distance; to whether speech and action correspond over time; to whether limits are predictable, proportionate and open to repair; to whether the adult can seek help, pause or transfer care when capacity is low; to whether food, housing, sleep, healthcare and safety are available; to whether another adult, sibling, peer, teacher or community provides a different experience; and to their own sensory, health, communication and developmental needs. Language and bodily expression are not two separate transmission systems; they are parts of a wider interaction, and a child's reading of either can be uncertain. The practical question is what the child repeatedly encountered, what became predictable and what later evidence changed that expectation.

A grandparent's forced displacement may affect a family through lost land, changed language, economic exclusion, community disruption, parental distress, family narrative and public policy. Calling the whole pattern epigenetic inheritance of trauma would hide the routes for which evidence is strongest and the places where change is possible.

Replace "unprocessed" with a specified adult-to-child route

The word "may" matters at every step.

"Unprocessed grief becomes suppression" and "unprocessed shame becomes performance" offer recognisable family patterns. They also skip every step between an adult experience and a child's outcome. A more responsible formulation names the route.

  1. Adult grief, shame, fear or historymay affect
  2. Attention, expectation, health, behaviour, availability, family meaningwithin
  3. Work, housing, care load, culture, support, current dangerthe child directly encounters
  4. Response, role, rule, silence, consequence, resource, relationshipmay contribute to
  5. Particular expectations, actions, meanings, health or participationfollowed across development.
Adult coherence is neither nothing nor everything

Attachment research reports a moderate group-level association between caregivers' attachment representations and caregiver and child attachment, varying across ecological conditions; the term transmission gap exists because proposed mediators such as sensitivity do not explain the whole relationship. So adult attachment-related meaning may relate to caregiving; coherence can be one useful source of information; present behaviour, support, ecology and the particular relationship still matter; group associations do not predict an individual dyad; the Adult Attachment Interview measures discourse in a specific assessment, not whether a person has processed their history; visible feeling is not a universal requirement for safe caregiving; and changed narrative does not establish changed conduct under pressure. Parental reflective functioning shows a similar pattern, with small, context-dependent associations in current reviews. Neither is a master mechanism that converts a difficult history into secure development.

An adult can feel unresolved grief while allowing a child to express sadness freely. Another can tell a coherent story about shame while continuing to demand performance. The inner experience is relevant, but it does not determine the caregiving act.

Family continuity does not create a duty to reconnect

Repair can mean rebuilding a relationship, or refusing to pass the same obligation forward.

The boundary worth preserving

Biological relation does not override consent or protection. A child or adult child is not responsible for completing a parent's healing. Family therapy is not appropriate when it would expose someone to coercion or retaliation. Forgiveness does not require access. Reparation can continue without reconciliation. Cultural respect does not require silence about harm. Chosen family and community may provide continuity that biological relatives did not. Some generational change occurs through distance, legal protection or ending a harmful role.

Repair is important, but it does not teach one lesson

Ordinary caregiver and child interaction contains mismatch, and what happens after a disruption matters. That is different from claiming every rupture followed by reconnection teaches that disconnection ends. A child may learn that a misunderstanding can be named and revised, that limits can remain while warmth returns, that harm is followed by accountability; or that an apology has no consequence, that reconnection is demanded before safety is restored, that the pattern will recur, or that distance, another caregiver or outside protection is the safer repair. The outcome depends on the rupture, power, response, recurrence and the child's experience. Brief laboratory mismatch is not evidence for repair after maltreatment, coercion or violence.

"Good enough" can remain a humane clinical idea: children do not require flawless adults. It should not become a measurable universal threshold or a reason to minimise preventable harm. The relevant standard is specific enough to observe, safety, care, support, repair, development and rights, rather than perfect versus imperfect parenting.

How F10 connects to the whole

A route-tracing sequence, and four handoffs.

Name the generations, exposure and outcome; separate genetic, prenatal, caregiving, learning, material and institutional routes; measure the adult condition and proposed intermediate process; measure what the child directly encounters; follow the child's outcome across context and development; change a route and observe benefit, cost, recurrence and unintended effect.

To child health, developmental and safeguarding services

When there is current danger, maltreatment, developmental concern, sudden change, unmet disability need or significant caregiver incapacity. A lineage explanation must not delay direct support.

To family, parenting and material support

When changing care load, health, housing, income, education, communication, respite or parenting practice may alter the child's actual conditions.

To Harm, Power and Repair

When adult or institutional behaviour has harmed a child or family member. Explanation, family loyalty and adult recovery do not replace cessation, accountability, reparation and protection. Understanding lineage never overrides impact, boundaries, safeguarding or the affected person's agency.

To F11

When the person is holding love and harm, continuity and refusal, grief and relief, compassion and boundaries, or more than one valid account of a family history. Complexity must not become ambiguity about what happened or who holds responsibility.

F1 supplies genetic variation and broad human capacities as background, not an explanation of a particular family pattern. F2 identifies caregiving, learning, context and developmental pathways within one child; F10 must specify how a condition crosses generations rather than relabelling F2 as inherited trauma. F3 contributes adult expectations and behaviour routes that may affect caregiving and can change, though insight does not predict parenting without observed action. F4, F5, F6 and F7 carry rules, valuation, bias and coercive enforcement that may travel through law, school or economics rather than private family learning, and family-systems explanation cannot distribute responsibility away from the person using coercive power. F8 recovery and reparation may alter some routes without guaranteeing child safety; F9 reminds F10 that difference is not failed attachment and normalising a child is not generational repair. Model 1 and Model 2 shape momentary access and recovery within each person, but a Gradient Position does not transmit from one nervous system to another, and Chronic organisation is not inherited as one family state.

Boundaries and provenance

Narrowed or retired wording, and the grounding route.

Narrowed or retired

"What the adult processes, the child does not inherit." Children calibrate to regulation, not intention. Words and body as two transmission channels, with the child reading the body when they disagree. "The consequence of what went unprocessed travels." Grief becomes suppression; shame becomes performance. A processed history as the precise protective factor. Coherence with feeling attached as the standard. Rupture followed by repair teaches that disconnection ends. Epigenetic effects of care are reversible. "Rebuilding bridges" as the required direction: a bridge implies renewed family connection, which may be unsafe or unwanted, so change is framed as altered pathways and reconciliation remains one optional outcome.

Grounding fields

Developmental science and developmental psychopathology; attachment, caregiving and infant mental-health research; emotion socialisation and family process; parenting-intervention and prevention science; behavioural and social learning; genetics and behavioural genetics; epigenetics and molecular biology; reproductive, prenatal and perinatal medicine; paediatrics and child health; life-course epidemiology; sociology of family and generations; anthropology, kinship studies and cultural psychology; economics, demography and social policy; history; education; social work and community psychology; maltreatment and violence research; family therapy; clinical psychology and psychiatry; disability studies and neurodiversity; communication and narrative research; law, child rights and safeguarding; ethics and human rights; participatory and survivor-led research; implementation research; forensic psychology in child-protection and family-court contexts. Priority evidence: prospective, longitudinal and genuinely multigenerational research that measures each proposed route; repeated observation, intervention follow-up, sibling and adoption designs, genetically informed studies, natural experiments, prenatal cohorts, linked administrative data, historical and policy comparison, and participatory work. Sampling should extend beyond one caregiver and infant to fathers, grandparents, siblings, adoptive, foster, queer and chosen families, collective caregiving, varied cultures, disabled and neurodivergent people, and different material and political conditions.

Old map title: Rebuilding Generational Bridges. Evidence status of the original source list: it combines attachment assessments, clinical family theories, mentalizing research, animal and human epigenetics, infant interaction studies and grief concepts, and they do not establish one intergenerational repair mechanism. Adult attachment coherence and child attachment are associated at group level with a transmission gap; earned security is a contested retrospective category; parental reflective functioning shows small, context-dependent associations; Bowen's multigenerational transmission process is clinical theory, not direct evidence; human intergenerational epigenetic research cannot attribute effects to one determinant, and animal caregiving research should not be translated into human prescriptions or claims that care reverses inherited trauma; still-face research does not establish what adult processing prevents or whether reconciliation is safe; good-enough parenting is a clinical idea, not a fixed threshold; ambiguous loss names grief without establishing a mechanism. WHO guidance supports multi-route intervention rather than one inner prerequisite, with evidence of varying certainty and often shorter-term follow-up. Change can be supported, but it must be observed at the outcome being claimed.
