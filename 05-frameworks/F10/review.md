## Decision

**Keep F10 as a Foundational Framework, but replace one processed-versus-inherited claim with a route-specific account of intergenerational continuity and change.**

Provisional title:

> **Intergenerational Pathways, Caregiving and Change**

Suggested explanatory line:

> How health, caregiving, learning, relationships, resources, institutions and cultural practices may remain continuous or change across generations.

The original framework notices something important. A person's history does not dictate what they will do with a child. People may reflect, learn, seek support, change their behaviour, repair harm and create conditions they did not receive themselves. Family and social patterns are therefore neither fixed nor inevitable.

The difficulty lies in describing every route as the adult's unprocessed experience reaching the child. Children do not receive one hidden parental state. They encounter behaviour, touch, voice, attention, absence, routines, limits, conflict, repair, stories, housing, food, health, school, community, law and the wider conditions affecting the people who care for them. Prenatal and possible germline routes raise different scientific questions again.

F10 should name these routes separately. It can then ask what changed, for whom, through which process and with what evidence across time.

## The question F10 owns

> Across generations, which biological, relational, behavioural, material, cultural and institutional conditions remain continuous or change—and what evidence connects a change in one generation to an outcome in another?

F10 owns questions concerning:

- who the generations and caregiving figures are;
- what condition, behaviour, resource, rule, story, exposure or outcome is said to continue;
- whether the route involves genetic inheritance, prenatal exposure, possible epigenetic processes, caregiving, learning, family roles, material conditions, culture or institutions;
- what the child directly experiences rather than what is inferred from an adult's private state;
- how children differ in temperament, health, disability, neurodivergence, communication and response;
- how children and caregivers affect one another over time;
- which wider conditions support or constrain caregiving;
- whether harm stopped, repair occurred and changed behaviour remained available later;
- what support, protection, policy, treatment, education or material intervention altered the route;
- which outcomes changed and which did not;
- whether connection to a biological family or lineage is wanted, safe and freely chosen.

It does not own a universal parenting standard, prediction of a child's future, reconstruction of a parent's history, diagnosis, proof of inherited trauma, biological destiny, assignment of blame to one caregiver, requirement to preserve family contact or promise that one adult's healing prevents every later difficulty.

## The governing separation

The word *transmission* can make unlike processes look as though they travel through one channel.

```text
adult history
        ≠
adult present organisation or meaning
        ≠
caregiving behaviour and available support
        ≠
conditions the child directly encounters
        ≠
the child's developing response
        ≠
the child's later outcome
```

Each arrow is a question, not a conclusion. A parent may understand their history and still need practical support to behave differently under exhaustion or pressure. Another may have little language for their history and provide reliable care. A child may experience difficulty despite sensitive caregiving, or do well across important domains despite serious adversity.

F10 should therefore use *intergenerational pathway* only when the generations, exposure, intermediate process, context and outcome are named.

## Working vocabulary

| Term | Working meaning | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Generation** | A specified position in a family, population or historical sequence | Parent, caregiver, grandparent, child, cohort and descendants must not be treated as interchangeable |
| **Intergenerational** | Involving effects, conditions or relationships between adjacent or identified generations | It is not shorthand for genetic or epigenetic inheritance |
| **Multigenerational** | Involving three or more generations or several generations present in one family or system | Presence across generations does not establish one transmission mechanism |
| **Transgenerational** | In biological research, effects observed beyond generations directly exposed through pregnancy or germline, with definitions depending on the exposure and lineage | It should not be used loosely for family patterns or human cultural continuity |
| **Genetic inheritance** | Transmission of DNA sequence variation through reproduction | It does not carry a memory, social rule, family narrative or specific traumatic event |
| **Prenatal or perinatal exposure** | Conditions acting during pregnancy, birth or the early postnatal period through biological and environmental routes | A fetus exposed during pregnancy is directly exposed; this is not proof of germline inheritance |
| **Epigenetic process** | Regulation affecting gene activity without changing DNA sequence, within cells and across development | Epigenetic difference does not by itself establish causation, permanence, trauma specificity or transmission to descendants |
| **Germline epigenetic inheritance** | Persistence of an exposure-related regulatory effect through sperm or egg into a later generation | Evidence is stronger in some animal models than in humans, where confounding routes are difficult to separate |
| **Caregiving** | Behaviour and conditions through which adults or others provide protection, nourishment, attention, guidance, limits, communication and support | It is not one person's nervous-system state, and it occurs within material and institutional conditions |
| **Emotion socialisation** | Ways emotion is modelled, noticed, named, responded to, expressed, constrained and connected to action | It is not a simple transfer of the caregiver's feeling into the child |
| **Modelling and social learning** | Learning through observation, participation, consequence and instruction | Resemblance does not prove imitation, and different children may learn different things from the same event |
| **Family narrative** | Stories, explanations, silences and meanings through which a family represents its past and present | A coherent story is not necessarily accurate, embodied, safe or behaviourally consequential |
| **Attachment representation** | A research construct concerning the organisation of attachment-related discourse or expectation, measured in specified ways | It is not a fixed attachment identity, whole relationship or general measure of parental health |
| **Parental reflective functioning** | A measured capacity to consider behaviour in relation to possible mental states in oneself and a child | Verbal reflection does not guarantee accuracy, sensitivity, safe behaviour or a particular child outcome |
| **Rupture** | A disruption, mismatch or injury within an interaction or relationship | Minor mismatch, harmful conduct, coercion and abuse require different levels of analysis |
| **Repair** | Work addressing a rupture's impact and restoring coordination, agency or relationship where possible and wanted | Repair is not mere reconnection, and serious harm requires cessation, accountability and protection |
| **Intergenerational change** | A measured difference in a condition, practice, resource, exposure or outcome across specified generations | Change in one route does not mean all routes or outcomes changed |
| **Lineage** | A biological, familial, cultural, legal, spiritual or chosen relation across time | Lineage creates no automatic entitlement to access, loyalty, forgiveness or reunion |

## Eight F10 construct families

| Construct family | Governing question | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **F10-A · Generations, history and claimed continuity** | Who and what are being connected across which period, and what evidence shows continuity rather than resemblance? | A repeated outcome does not establish that the same process produced it in each generation |
| **F10-B · Biological and developmental pathways** | Do genetic variation, prenatal conditions, birth, health, nutrition, toxic exposure or a specifically proposed epigenetic route contribute? | Biological evidence cannot stand in for caregiving, culture or material conditions, and a molecular association does not establish inheritance |
| **F10-C · Caregiving behaviour and interaction** | What happens in protection, responsiveness, conflict, limit-setting, support, mismatch and repair over time? | The adult's stated intention or internal organisation cannot substitute for observed behaviour and the child's experience |
| **F10-D · Learning, language and family meaning** | What is modelled, reinforced, named, hidden, narrated or made costly to question? | Words and stories are not disembodied channels, and silence does not reveal one motive or unprocessed emotion |
| **F10-E · Roles, power, harm and protection** | Who carries responsibility, care, secrecy, authority or blame, and what happens to refusal, reporting and boundaries? | Family-systems language must not distribute responsibility so widely that an actor's coercion or a child's protection disappears |
| **F10-F · Material, community and institutional continuity** | Which housing, income, work, education, healthcare, discrimination, law, migration, violence, community and policy conditions persist or change? | Structural conditions cannot be reduced to parenting quality or one family's psychology |
| **F10-G · Child contribution, variation and developmental outcome** | How do the child's health, temperament, disability, neurodivergence, communication, relationships and action affect the developing interaction? | Bidirectionality does not make a child responsible for an adult's harmful behaviour or remove the caregiver's duty of care |
| **F10-H · Intervention, support and observed change** | Which protection, treatment, parenting support, resource, relationship, school, community or policy change altered which route and outcome? | Improvement does not prove that one intervention repaired the whole family or permanently prevented recurrence |

These construct families are a route inventory, not a family score, inheritance model or sequence through which every family must pass.

## Several routes can cross the same generation boundary

Intergenerational continuity may involve:

1. **DNA sequence:** inherited variation relevant to health, development or temperament.
2. **Prenatal and perinatal conditions:** nutrition, illness, substances, stress-related physiology, healthcare, toxic exposure and birth conditions.
3. **Possible germline or other epigenetic routes:** exposure-related regulatory effects requiring evidence beyond ordinary genetic and environmental continuity.
4. **Caregiving and relationship:** availability, protection, responsiveness, conflict, coercion, repair, touch, routine and support.
5. **Social learning and language:** modelling, reinforcement, emotion talk, warnings, silence, stories and expectations.
6. **Family roles and power:** responsibility, loyalty, secrecy, hierarchy, access, credibility and response to boundaries.
7. **Material conditions:** income, housing, food, work, healthcare, education, care labour and inherited resources or debt.
8. **Community, culture and institution:** discrimination, violence, migration, law, religion, school, policy, collective memory and sources of belonging or resistance.

These routes can interact. They should not be merged.

A grandparent's forced displacement may affect a family through lost land, changed language, economic exclusion, community disruption, parental distress, family narrative and public policy. Calling the whole pattern *epigenetic inheritance of trauma* would hide the routes for which evidence is strongest and the places where change is possible.

## Children encounter behaviour and conditions, not intention alone

The original claim that children calibrate to an adult's regulation rather than intention protects against a common mistake: wanting to provide safety is not the same as providing it. The replacement should remain behavioural without making the caregiver's body a second message that always overrides speech.

Children may respond to:

- what adults repeatedly do when need, distress, disagreement or error appears;
- voice, facial movement, posture, touch, timing and physical distance;
- whether speech and action correspond over time;
- whether limits are predictable, proportionate and open to repair;
- whether the adult can seek help, pause or transfer care when capacity is low;
- whether food, housing, sleep, healthcare and physical safety are available;
- whether another adult, sibling, peer, teacher or community provides a different experience;
- the child's own sensory, health, communication and developmental needs.

Language and bodily expression are not two separate transmission systems. They are parts of a wider interaction. A child's interpretation of either can be uncertain, and cultural or neurodivergent differences may change what a gesture, tone or silence means.

The practical question is not whether the child read the adult's real body instead of their words. It is what the child repeatedly encountered, what became predictable and what later evidence changed that expectation.

## Replace “unprocessed” with a specified adult-to-child route

The phrases *unprocessed grief becomes suppression* and *unprocessed shame becomes performance* offer recognisable family patterns. They also skip every step between an adult experience and a child's outcome.

A more responsible formulation names the route:

```text
adult grief, shame, fear or history
        ↓ may affect
attention · expectation · health · behaviour · availability · family meaning
        ↓ within
work · housing · care load · culture · support · current danger
        ↓ child directly encounters
response · role · rule · silence · consequence · resource · relationship
        ↓ may contribute to
particular expectations, actions, meanings, health or participation
```

The word *may* matters at every step. An adult can feel unresolved grief while allowing a child to express sadness freely. Another can tell a coherent story about shame while continuing to demand performance. The inner experience is relevant, but it does not determine the caregiving act.

## Adult coherence is neither nothing nor everything

Attachment research has found an association between caregivers' attachment representations and child–caregiver attachment. A large synthesis and later individual-participant meta-analysis describe a moderate association that varies across ecological conditions. The term *transmission gap* exists because proposed mediators such as sensitivity do not explain the whole relationship.

This supports a narrower conclusion:

- adult attachment-related meaning may relate to caregiving and child attachment;
- coherence can be one useful source of information;
- present behaviour, support, ecology and the particular relationship still matter;
- group associations do not predict an individual dyad;
- the Adult Attachment Interview measures discourse in a specific assessment, not whether a person has fully processed their history;
- emotional vividness or visible feeling is not a universal requirement for safe caregiving;
- changed narrative does not establish changed conduct under pressure.

Parental reflective functioning shows a similar pattern. It may support curiosity about a child's separate mind and is associated with aspects of parenting, usually with small and context-dependent effects in current reviews. It is not a master mechanism that converts a difficult history into secure development.

## Repair is important, but it does not teach one lesson

Ordinary caregiver–child interaction contains mismatch. Research on interaction and repair helps show that coordination need not be perfect and that what happens after a disruption matters. This is different from claiming that every rupture followed by reconnection teaches that disconnection ends.

The child may learn that:

- a misunderstanding can be named and revised;
- distress changes another person's behaviour;
- limits can remain while warmth returns;
- harm is followed by specific accountability and restitution;
- an apology has no behavioural consequence;
- reconnection is demanded before safety is restored;
- the pattern will recur;
- distance, another caregiver or outside protection is the safer repair.

The outcome depends on the rupture, power, response, recurrence and child's experience. Brief mismatch in a laboratory interaction cannot be used as direct evidence for repair after maltreatment, coercion or violence.

“Good enough” can remain a humane clinical idea: children do not require flawless adults. It should not become a measurable universal threshold or a reason to minimise preventable harm. The relevant standard is specific enough to observe—safety, care, support, repair, development and rights—rather than perfect versus imperfect parenting.

## Family continuity does not create a duty to reconnect

F10's original boundary is worth preserving. Understanding how an adult, family or community arrived at a pattern can widen context and compassion. It does not make relationship obligatory.

- Biological relation does not override consent or protection.
- A child or adult child is not responsible for completing a parent's healing.
- Family therapy is not appropriate when it would expose someone to coercion or retaliation.
- Forgiveness does not require access.
- Reparation can continue without reconciliation.
- Cultural respect does not require silence about harm.
- Chosen family and community may provide continuity that biological relatives did not.
- Some generational change occurs through distance, legal protection or ending a harmful role.

Repair can mean rebuilding a relationship. It can also mean refusing to pass the same obligation forward.

## Reading the original F10 language against the current system

### Preserve, with precise translation

| Original direction | Current reading |
| --- | --- |
| A difficult history does not dictate the next generation | Development is probabilistic; adult behaviour, support, material conditions, community and child variation can change outcomes |
| Intention alone does not determine the child's experience | Observe caregiving behaviour, conditions, consistency, repair and the child's access rather than relying on stated motive |
| Several pathways operate at once | Keep biological, developmental, relational, behavioural, material, cultural and institutional routes separate enough to test |
| Adult reflection may matter | Meaning and reflective capacity may affect attention, behaviour and help-seeking, but their contribution must be observed |
| Repair can teach something different from unrepaired rupture | Examine the particular rupture, response, restored agency, recurrence and child's later expectation |
| Perfection is not the standard | Use developmentally and ethically specified care, safety, support and repair rather than demanding flawless coordination |
| Compassion does not create obligation | Biological or cultural lineage creates no entitlement to access, forgiveness or reconciliation |

### Narrow, revise or retire

| Map wording or implication | Why it needs care | Safer direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “What the adult processes, the child does not inherit” | It promises prevention, merges several routes and implies that unprocessed experience is inherited | Name the adult change, transmission route and child outcome separately; measure what changed |
| Children calibrate to regulation, not intention | It reduces development to one caregiver state and excludes behaviour, resources, other relationships and child variation | Compare stated intention with observable caregiving, context and the child's experience |
| Words and body are two transmission channels | It reinstates a body–mind split and omits action, timing, touch, consequence, material care and institutions | Treat communication and bodily expression as parts of a wider interaction |

| The consequence of what went unprocessed travels | The phrase does not identify the intermediate behaviour, condition or biological route | Trace adult experience to a measurable mediator, direct child exposure and later outcome |
| Grief becomes suppression; shame becomes performance | These are possible narratives presented as deterministic conversions | State the observed family rule, behaviour and contingency without assigning one hidden origin |
| A processed history is the precise protective factor | Attachment coherence is associated with outcomes but is not sufficient, causal proof or the only route | Include behaviour, support, material conditions, ecology, child variation and intervention evidence |

## Evidence status of the original source list

The source list combines attachment assessments, clinical family theories, mentalizing research, animal and human epigenetics, infant interaction studies and grief concepts. They do not establish one intergenerational repair mechanism.

- **Adult attachment coherence and infant attachment** are associated at group level. Meta-analytic work reports a moderate relationship and meaningful variation across ecological conditions. The attachment *transmission gap* remains; coherence is not a guarantee or a complete mediator.
- **Earned security** is a contested retrospective category when inferred from Adult Attachment Interview discourse and reported childhood experience. It should not stand for processed history or general interruption of intergenerational harm.
- **Parental reflective functioning** offers a way to examine curiosity about the child's and caregiver's mental states. Reviews find generally small, context-dependent associations with parenting, and intervention evidence is mixed. Verbal reflective capacity is not the same as behaviour under pressure.
- **Bowen's multigenerational transmission process** is part of a clinical family-systems theory. It may support questions about roles, anxiety and differentiation but does not provide direct evidence for a biological or universal family mechanism.
- **Human intergenerational epigenetic research** is methodologically difficult because genetic, prenatal, postnatal, social and material routes coexist. Reviews describe possible pathways but emphasise that current human evidence cannot attribute observed effects to one determinant. Controlled evidence is stronger in animal models.
- **Meaney's caregiving and epigenetic work** is influential animal research. Cross-fostering and experimental control help isolate processes in rodents; the findings should not be translated directly into human parenting prescriptions or claims that care reverses inherited trauma.
- **Yehuda's work** helps distinguish prenatal, postnatal and possible preconception pathways while stressing human evidential limits. It should not be summarised as proof that trauma passes epigenetically from parent to child.
- **Still-face and mutual-regulation research** examines brief perturbation, infant response and dyadic coordination under specific laboratory conditions. It does not establish what adult processing prevents, how abuse is repaired or whether reconciliation is safe.
- **Good-enough parenting** is a psychoanalytic and clinical idea associated with Winnicott, not an empirically fixed threshold shared across cultures and developmental conditions.
- **Ambiguous loss** is a clinical and interpretive framework for losses without clear closure or recognition. It can help name grief; it does not establish a mechanism of transmission or prescribe contact with family.

Authoritative guidance supports multi-route intervention rather than one inner prerequisite. The WHO's Nurturing Care Framework includes health, nutrition, responsive caregiving, security, safety and early learning within wider systems. WHO parenting guidelines find benefits from structured parenting interventions for maltreatment, harsh parenting, relationships and child or parent outcomes, while the underlying evidence varies in certainty and often covers shorter-term follow-up. Change can be supported, but it must be observed at the outcome being claimed.

## F10's handoffs

F10 should use a route-tracing sequence:

```text
Name the generations, exposure and outcome
        ↓
separate genetic · prenatal · caregiving · learning · material · institutional routes
        ↓
measure the adult condition and proposed intermediate process
        ↓
measure what the child directly encounters
        ↓
follow the child's outcome across context and development
        ↓
change a route and observe benefit, cost, recurrence and unintended effect
```

Four handoffs matter most:

1. **To child health, developmental and safeguarding services:** when there is current danger, maltreatment, developmental concern, sudden change, unmet disability need or significant caregiver incapacity. A lineage explanation must not delay direct support.
2. **To family, parenting and material support:** when changing care load, health, housing, income, education, communication, respite or parenting practice may alter the child's actual conditions.
3. **To HPR:** when adult or institutional behaviour has harmed a child or family member. Explanation, family loyalty and adult recovery do not replace cessation, accountability, reparation and protection.
4. **To F11:** when the person is holding love and harm, continuity and refusal, grief and relief, compassion and boundaries or more than one valid account of a family history.

## Result of this pass

- **Framework status:** retain and substantially reframe.
- **Collection role:** intergenerational route framework connecting development, caregiving, material conditions, institutions, support and change.
- **Title:** revise from “Rebuilding Generational Bridges” to “Intergenerational Pathways, Caregiving and Change.”
- **Transmission:** use only when generations, exposure, mediator, timescale and outcome are specified.
- **Core architecture:** use eight construct families—claimed continuity, biological pathways, caregiving, learning and meaning, roles and power, material and institutional conditions, child contribution, and intervention.
- **Main distinction:** genetic inheritance, prenatal exposure, possible epigenetic routes, caregiving, social learning, family roles, material continuity and institutional history are not interchangeable.
- **Parent–child relation:** behaviour and conditions matter; neither intention nor visible bodily state alone determines what the child receives.
- **Processing:** adult reflection and coherence may contribute without guaranteeing safe care or preventing every child outcome.
- **Repair:** mismatch and repair are developmentally relevant, but serious harm requires separate protection and accountability, and no repair requires reunion.
- **Epigenetics:** retain as a carefully bounded research route; do not use inherited-trauma shorthand or promise reversal.
- **Agency:** biological relation and lineage never override safety, consent, boundaries or freedom from family contact.
- **Evidence status:** architectural first pass; every biological, developmental, clinical, intervention and cultural claim requires route- and population-specific grounding.

## Grounding science and knowledge fields

F10 needs fields that can follow people, relationships and conditions over time without turning association into inheritance. Caregivers, children, adult children and communities should participate in deciding which outcomes matter and which family assumptions require challenge.

| Field | What it can ground in F10 | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Developmental psychology and developmental science** | Change in emotion, cognition, relationship, communication, action and participation across childhood | One universal developmental route or later outcome inferred from one early interaction |
| **Developmental psychopathology** | Multiple pathways to similar outcomes, different outcomes after similar exposures, timing, risk, protection and resilience | Inevitability, inherited disorder or one family cause from a diagnosis |
| **Attachment, caregiving and infant mental-health research** | Caregiver–child attachment, sensitivity, availability, interaction, mismatch and repair | A complete relationship, parenting capacity or individual future from an attachment classification |

| **Communication science, discourse and narrative research** | Family stories, silence, language, interaction, testimony and meaning-making | Story coherence as proof of truth, processing, bodily integration or safe behaviour |
| **Law, child rights and safeguarding** | Duties of care, protection, consent, custody, family integrity, disability rights and legal remedy | Developmental mechanism, treatment need or identical legal threshold across jurisdictions |
| **Ethics and human-rights scholarship** | Dignity, dependency, autonomy, protection, relational obligation and justice across generations | Empirical outcome or requirement for forgiveness, loyalty or reconciliation |

| **Forensic psychology** | Psycholegal assessment in child protection, custody, testimony, capacity and family-court contexts | General science of parenting, credibility, maltreatment or intergenerational transmission |

### Priority evidence

F10 should favour prospective, longitudinal and genuinely multigenerational research that measures each proposed route rather than using adult recollection or descendant outcome as proof of transmission. Useful designs include repeated observation, intervention follow-up, sibling and adoption designs, genetically informed studies, natural experiments, prenatal cohorts, linked administrative data, historical and policy comparison, and qualitative or participatory work that reveals routes missed by standard measures.

Every claim should identify the exposed generation, directly exposed descendants, proposed mediator, confounders, child outcome, timing and alternative routes. Biological claims should distinguish DNA sequence, prenatal exposure, placental or developmental programming, germline evidence and postnatal environment. Relational claims should distinguish adult report, observed caregiving, child report, child behaviour and later outcome.

Sampling should extend beyond mother–infant dyads to fathers, grandparents, siblings, adoptive and foster families, queer and chosen families, collective caregiving, varied cultures, disabled and neurodivergent children and caregivers, and families living under different material and political conditions. Evidence of risk should be paired with evidence of variation, protection and change. The framework should make support more precise, not make families feel biologically condemned by their history.
