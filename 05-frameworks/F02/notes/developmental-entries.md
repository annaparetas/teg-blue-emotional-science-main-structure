## Purpose

This file holds developmental observations that may later help F2 explain how human capacities become available, shaped and practised across relationships.

An entry does not enter F2 merely because it fits the model. Each one must distinguish:

- the observation being described;
- established constructs and evidence;
- the TEG-Blue synthesis proposed from them;
- which model owns each part of the explanation;
- what the idea can and cannot explain about later behaviour.

The register allows the idea to develop without making the current F2 draft heavier before its internal connections are ready.

## Entry 001 · Mirroring and the organism↔organism information loop

Scientific-facing subtitle: **Bidirectional social-information acquisition, contingent response and developmental updating across infant–caregiver interaction**

**Entry status:** captured · terminology decomposition required · evidence audit pending · F2 integration pending

### The observation

Humans begin using other people as information before they can explain what another person is communicating or what they themselves feel.

An infant's state can alter expression, posture, movement, voice and behaviour. Another person detects some of those changes and responds. The infant then detects the response, and the interaction becomes part of the conditions shaping what happens next.

```text
infant condition and organisation change
        ↓
expression, movement, voice or behaviour changes
        ↓
caregiver detects and interprets some of the change
        ↓
caregiver expression, voice, touch, posture or action changes
        ↓
infant detects the caregiver's response
        ↓
the infant's organisation, action and learning may change
```

The important developmental observation is:

> Before a child can explain “what I feel”, another person is already responding to parts of what the child is experiencing.

That response does more than alter immediate arousal. It may become additional information about the child's state, the situation, the relationship and what is likely to happen next.

### The broader process beneath mirroring

**Mirroring** may be too narrow to name the original process.

The broader candidate process is:

> **One organism detects changes in another organism and uses that information to update its own organisation, interpretation or action readiness.**

TEG-Blue can use the accessible working name **organism↔organism information loop** while the scientific-facing language remains **bidirectional social-information acquisition and updating**.

This is presently a TEG-Blue synthesis. It may organise several established research territories, but it should not be presented as one discovered biological mechanism.

### Two directions in the loop

#### Other → ME

```text
another organism changes
        ↓
I detect expression · voice · posture · movement · attention · action
        ↓
the information is weighted within my present organisation
        ↓
my perception, feeling, prediction or action readiness may change
```

This direction can help an organism orient towards opportunity, care, interest, alarm, hostility, withdrawal or a shared object of attention. It does not require language or conscious inference.

#### ME → Other → ME

```text
something becomes relevant within me
        ↓
my expression, posture, voice, movement or behaviour changes
        ↓
another person detects and responds to me
        ↓
I detect their response
        ↓
their response becomes additional information about
my state · the situation · the relationship · available action
```

This second direction is especially important for F2. It suggests that self-understanding does not develop through inward observation alone. Other people's contingent responses may help make some internal changes more noticeable, differentiated, nameable and usable.

### Three processes that should remain distinguishable

The same interaction may involve all three, but they answer different questions.

| Process | Question | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Social-information acquisition** | What does another organism's change tell me about them, the environment, the relationship or what may happen next? | Information from another person can be incomplete, ambiguous, inaccurate or strategically produced |
| **Interpersonal state coordination** | How does each person's changing organisation alter the conditions encountered by the other? | Coordination and synchrony do not by themselves establish safety, care or beneficial regulation |
| **Developmental self-information** | How can another person's response help a child notice, differentiate, interpret or mistrust their own state? | The caregiver does not directly reveal the child's inner state; the response is another source of information to be learned from |

Mirroring may participate in all three. It should not become a synonym for all three.

### Developmental boundary at birth

F2 should avoid saying that a newborn has no capacity to regulate independently. Newborns arrive with active endogenous capacities for arousal modulation, orientation, withdrawal, sleep–wake organisation, signalling and learning. At the same time, their survival and much of their state organisation depend heavily on what other people do.

A more precise formulation is:

> **Early regulation includes substantial organism-level capacity and substantial dependence on caregiver-provided conditions. It is neither entirely self-generated nor something one nervous system simply does to another.**

The caregiver may change temperature, contact, nourishment, sensory input, movement, distance, danger, predictability and opportunities for recovery. Face and voice matter, but so do material actions and conditions.

### What the caregiver's response can provide

A response may:

- detect that the child's condition has changed;
- make the change more visible through face, voice, touch or language;
- alter the intensity or direction of the interaction;
- connect bodily change with a possible situation or need;
- offer an action that changes the condition;
- provide words, distinctions or a revisable explanation;
- misread, ignore, amplify, shame or override the signal;
- communicate what the relationship appears able or willing to tolerate.

The caregiver therefore does not function as a neutral mirror. The response can reflect, transform, interpret, act, misattune or impose meaning. F2 needs to follow which of these occurred.

### How the loop can support emotional participation

Across repeated interactions, a child may begin learning associations among:

```text
internal bodily change
        · external conditions
        · another person's response
        · expression and action
        · whether the condition changes
        · whether contact remains available
        · whether recovery or repair follows
```

When the response is sufficiently contingent and revisable, it may help the child practise:

- noticing that an internal change has occurred;
- differentiating one state from another;
- connecting a feeling with context without treating the first interpretation as final;
- expecting that communication or action may change an outcome;
- distinguishing the child's state from the caregiver's state;
- learning that relationship can remain available while emotion is examined.

This does not mean that another person creates the emotion or possesses privileged access to its complete meaning.

### The invalidation branch

The same loop can teach something very different when the adult repeatedly dismisses, punishes, mislabels or centres their own response.

| Child's information | Adult response | Additional information available to the child | Possible learning with repetition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Distress, pain, fear or need becomes visible | The condition is noticed and investigated | “Something changed; it can be examined and acted upon” | Signal access, differentiation and effective help-seeking may receive practice |
| Anger or refusal becomes visible | The boundary is recognised while harmful behaviour is limited | “The feeling and the behaviour can be evaluated separately” | Anger may remain available as information without authorising harm |
| Feeling becomes visible | The adult mocks, withdraws, punishes or calls it false | “Showing this threatens contact or credibility” | Expression, trust or help-seeking may become costly |
| The child's report conflicts with the adult's account | The adult's explanation is the only permitted version | “Their interpretation carries more relational authority than mine” | Borrowed certainty, self-doubt or concealment may become easier to recruit |
| The adult becomes distressed by the child's state | The child must calm, reassure or protect the adult | “Their state must be read before mine can be used” | Monitoring, appeasement, role reversal or self-backgrounding may receive practice |

The biological signal need not disappear. What may change is its weighting, felt access, differentiation, expression, relation to action and inclusion in the child's self-explanation.

### Connection to the acceptable character

The organism↔organism information loop may help explain how an **acceptable character** becomes developmentally possible.

```text
my state becomes visible
        ↓
I detect how the adult responds
        ↓
their response becomes information about relational cost
        ↓
I practise the expression, role or explanation that keeps connection available
        ↓
the practised version becomes easier to perform and represent
        ↓
“what works here” may gradually feel like “who I am”
```

This route does not require the child to consciously invent a false self. It describes how repeated feedback may make some forms of participation easier, safer or more intelligible than others.

The loop also explains why the acceptable character is relationally specific at first. A child may remain expressive alone, with peers or with another caregiver. Generalisation across contexts is a later question, not an assumption.

### Mirroring is not the same as regulation

Mirroring belongs close to **social-information acquisition and state updating**. Regulation asks what the organism's resources and capacities are being organised to accomplish with that information.

Another person's response may:

- provide new information;
- change the material condition;
- increase or reduce relational uncertainty;
- make an action possible;
- lower visible expression;
- alter physiological demand;
- support updating, recovery or repair.

These are not interchangeable outcomes. A child becoming quiet may indicate lower pressure, inhibition, attention to the adult, fatigue, changed conditions or recovery. The observed synchrony or stillness does not decide which occurred.

F2 should therefore ask:

1. What information did each person detect?
2. How did each person's response change the other's conditions?
3. What problem did the interaction organise around?
4. Whose state, need or interpretation received priority?
5. Did the original condition change?
6. Did the child retain access to the signal?
7. Did an effective action, updating, recovery or repair become possible?

### Terms that should not be collapsed

| Term or research territory | Distinct question for later review |
| --- | --- |
| **Contingent responsiveness** | How reliably and appropriately does a response relate to the child's preceding signal or action? |
| **Affective attunement** | How is the quality or intensity of another's state represented across a different expressive form? |
| **Emotion mirroring** | How does another person's response reflect or mark something about the child's apparent emotional state? |
| **Interpersonal synchrony** | How do behavioural or physiological patterns align across time, and at what timescale? |
| **Co-regulation** | How do interactions and shared conditions alter each person's regulatory demand and available capacities? |
| **Social referencing** | How does another person's response inform interpretation of an ambiguous object, event or situation? |
| **Mimicry and emotional contagion** | When do observed expressions recruit similar expression or feeling without deliberate inference? |
| **Affective sharing** | How does something of another person's affective state become represented while self and other remain distinguishable? |
| **Mentalizing** | How does the person infer, question and revise beliefs about another person's perspective or state? |
| **Empathic concern** | When does information about another person's state motivate care rather than only detection, resonance or prediction? |

The proposed organism↔organism information loop may connect these territories. It must not erase the differences among their mechanisms, measurements or developmental timelines.

### Behaviour boundary

F2 may help explain the developmental origins of capacities and learnt response routes. It cannot establish the origin of a present behaviour from the behaviour alone.

The same early interaction pattern may contribute to different later outcomes. Similar adult behaviours can also arise through different developmental histories, present conditions, goals, values and opportunities.

The model must therefore preserve this sequence:

```text
possible developmental history
        → present information and organisation
        → available actions and learnt predictions
        → behaviour chosen or enacted
        → impact, feedback, responsibility and repair assessed separately
```

Developmental explanation can add context. It cannot turn a possible origin into a behavioural verdict or excuse.

### Research traditions and evidence routes to examine

The names below identify traditions mentioned in the source entry. They are search routes, not evidence citations and not confirmation of the proposed synthesis.

- Edward Tronick: reciprocal interaction, mismatch and repair, including still-face research;
- Daniel Stern: affect attunement, vitality and early intersubjective interaction;
- Peter Fonagy and Mary Target: reflective function, mentalization and the development of self–other understanding;
- György Gergely and John Watson: social biofeedback, contingency detection and marked affect-mirroring accounts.

The evidence pass should also examine comparative social cognition, developmental psychobiology, infant communication, caregiver contingency, social referencing, interpersonal synchrony, emotion socialisation, interoception, attachment and cultural variation.

### Questions the evidence pass must answer

1. Which endogenous regulatory capacities are present at birth, and which conditions depend heavily on caregiver action?
2. Which aspects of caregiver response predict immediate infant change, later emotion differentiation or later self-report—and which do not?
3. What evidence distinguishes reflection, contingency, marking, attunement, synchrony and material problem-solving?
4. Does caregiver response support the child's access to internal information directly, indirectly through language and action, or through several routes?
5. Which proposed processes are shared across species, and which depend on human language, culture or reflective self-representation?
6. How do neurodivergence, disability, sensory difference, communication form and cultural display rules change what counts as a contingent or readable response?
7. When does interpersonal synchrony support recovery, and when can synchrony occur within fear, coercion, vigilance or compulsory adaptation?
8. What observations would disconfirm or narrow the organism↔organism information-loop synthesis?
9. Which developmental findings are longitudinal, experimentally supported, observational, retrospective or theoretical?
10. How far can any developmental result travel before it becomes an unsupported explanation of adult behaviour?

### Possible F2 integration paragraph

> A child does not learn to understand emotional information by looking inward alone. From the beginning, changes in the child alter other people, and their responses become part of the information the child receives next. A sufficiently contingent response may help connect bodily change, situation, expression, action and outcome. A dismissive or punitive response may instead teach that the signal is relationally costly, even while the underlying emotional–somatic activity continues. Across repetition, the child may learn not only what a feeling appears to mean, but which version of themselves keeps connection most available.

This paragraph is a working synthesis. It should enter F2 only after the relevant claims have been decomposed and grounded.

## Entry 002 · Borrowing Regulation

Scientific-facing subtitle: **Caregiver-provided conditions, infant physiological regulation and bidirectional biobehavioural coordination**

**Entry status:** captured · original wording substantially revised · initial F1/F2 integration completed · evidence audit pending

**F1 handoff:** [Early dependency couples connection-preservation and organism-protection](../../F01/crosswalk.html#survival-bridge-title)

**Shared biological premise:** [Human Sociality and the Two Survival Functions](../../../07-reference/sociality.html#development)

### The source proposition

The entry proposes that a mammalian infant cannot regulate its own physiology, must “borrow” a regulated adult nervous system, and receives restoration through touch, warmth, eye contact and a calm caregiver's rhythms. It further proposes that infant heart rate, breathing and stress hormones synchronise with the caregiver through the ventral vagal pathway.

The underlying observation matters:

> A young infant's physiological organisation is strongly affected by conditions another person provides, and repeated caregiver–infant interaction can alter distress, arousal, bodily demand and recovery.

The original wording goes further than this observation can presently carry.

### What should be preserved

- Human infants are highly dependent on caregivers for nourishment, thermal conditions, protection, movement, contact and access to changes they cannot produce alone.
- Touch, warmth, holding, feeding, voice, smell, movement, timing and contingent action can alter the conditions within which infant regulation occurs.
- Caregiver and infant physiology and behaviour can show coordination or covariation across some measures and timescales.
- Another person's response can support lower demand, effective action, updating, recovery and sleep or rest.
- Repeated interaction may contribute to expectations about whether distress brings help, whether conditions can change and whether recovery is likely.

These points can support F2. They do not require the claim that the infant has no regulatory capacity of its own.

### The central correction

An infant does not literally borrow another person's nervous system.

The caregiver changes the infant's conditions. The infant's own sensory, autonomic, endocrine, metabolic, motor and neural processes detect and respond to those changes.

```text
infant condition changes
        ↓
infant signals, or the caregiver detects another cue
        ↓
caregiver changes contact · warmth · nourishment · movement · voice
distance · stimulation · protection · timing or another condition
        ↓
the infant detects the changed conditions
        ↓
the infant's own organism reorganises
        ↓
demand may reduce · action may change · recovery may become possible
```

A more precise accessible formulation is:

> **An infant regulates through its own developing organism within conditions that caregivers often have to provide and change.**

“Borrowing regulation” may remain as a public metaphor only if this biological translation travels beside it.

### Claim-by-claim translation

| Original direction | What it helps us notice | Safer scientific-facing direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The infant cannot regulate its own physiology** | Early regulatory capacity is limited and heavily dependent on external conditions | Infants have endogenous regulatory capacities, but their range is developing and many essential conditions depend on caregiver action |
| **The infant cannot maintain body temperature** | Thermal dependence is developmentally important | Newborn thermoregulation is active but constrained; caregiver behaviour and environmental conditions can be essential, with large differences across species, gestational age and health |
| **The infant cannot feed itself** | The infant cannot independently obtain nourishment | Feeding is coordinated: the infant contributes active sensing, orientation, sucking, swallowing and signalling while another person provides access, positioning, timing and food |
| **The infant cannot return itself to baseline** | Recovery often depends on changed external conditions and caregiver help | Infants have several self-organising and self-soothing capacities, while caregiver action may substantially alter demand, action availability and recovery |
| **The infant borrows a regulated adult nervous system** | Another person's organisation affects the quality and timing of care | The infant's organism regulates within caregiver-provided conditions; no nervous system is transferred or temporarily shared |
| **Regulation is transmitted through touch, warmth and eye contact** | Social and sensory conditions can alter infant organisation | Touch, warmth, feeding, voice, odour, movement, posture, gaze, timing and contingency may each participate; none is a universal transmission channel |
| **A calm caregiver transmits calm** | Caregiver organisation may influence the interaction | Effective care does not require visible calm and does not transmit one state intact; an alert, active caregiver may regulate the situation by changing what is happening |
| **Heart rate, breathing and stress hormones synchronise** | Infant and caregiver measures may become coordinated | Specify the measure, direction, lag, timescale and context; coupling or covariation does not necessarily mean matching values, one-way influence or successful regulation |
| **The caregiver performs restoration for the infant** | Caregiver action may make recovery materially possible | The caregiver changes conditions and provides resources; restoration remains work performed by the infant's own organism |
| **The process is routed through the ventral vagal pathway** | Autonomic participation matters | Retire the single-pathway claim unless a specific measured pathway is supported; examine distributed sensory, autonomic, endocrine, metabolic, thermoregulatory and behavioural participation |

### Mammalian is too broad without qualification

Mammalian infants vary substantially. Some species are highly altricial; others are mobile and comparatively developed soon after birth. Thermoregulation, feeding behaviour, sensory availability, parental care and social dependence differ across species and developmental stages.

F1 may investigate the evolutionary distribution of parental care and social buffering. F2 should focus on human development unless a comparative claim has been checked species by species.

The phrase “a mammalian infant” should therefore not carry a universal human developmental claim.

### Conditions, not state transfer

The caregiver may provide or alter:

- temperature and physical contact;
- food, hydration and feeding position;
- protection from danger or overstimulation;
- movement, holding and changes in posture;
- light, sound, voice, smell and sensory predictability;
- proximity, distance and opportunities for orientation;
- timing, pacing and contingent response;
- access to sleep, rest and reduced demand;
- medical help or another form of material action.

These changes can affect the infant's organisation without implying that the caregiver's internal state has been copied into the infant.

This distinction matters for the Gradient. Regulation is not a substance transmitted from a calm body to an unregulated body. It is the continuing organisation of resources and capacities in relation to detected or predicted conditions.

### Coordination is not identical synchrony

Caregiver–infant coordination may involve:

- matching or similar behavioural timing;
- turn-taking rather than simultaneous matching;
- one measure rising while another falls;
- a response following with a delay;
- temporary coupling around a specific event;
- repair after mismatch;
- shared response to the same environmental condition.

Calling all of these **synchrony** can hide the process being measured.

Physiological coupling also does not establish direction. The caregiver may affect the infant, the infant may affect the caregiver, both may respond to the same event, or the pattern may be bidirectional.

Most importantly, synchrony is not automatically beneficial. Coordination can occur during mutual vigilance, shared distress, coercive monitoring or escalating interaction. The evidence pass must ask what the coordination accomplished and whether it supported updating, recovery or wider participation.

### Connection to Entry 001

Entry 001 describes the organism↔organism information loop. Entry 002 asks what happens when information within that loop leads another person to change the infant's conditions.

```text
ENTRY 001 · SOCIAL INFORMATION

What do I detect in you, and what does your response tell me?

                    ↓ may inform

ENTRY 002 · REGULATORY PARTICIPATION

What conditions change, how does my organism reorganise,
and does demand reduce, updating occur or recovery become available?
```

Mirroring, detection or synchrony may provide information. They are not, by themselves, proof that regulation succeeded.

### F2 developmental significance

Repeated caregiver-provided changes can teach more than immediate relief.

The infant or child may gradually learn:

- whether signals tend to bring effective action;
- which forms of expression another person detects;
- whether distress changes the conditions or only changes the adult;
- whether support remains available during activation;
- whether lower pressure is followed by recovery;
- whether the child's state can remain visible without threatening connection;
- whether the adult's state must be managed before the child's condition can be addressed.

This connects caregiver-provided conditions to emotional participation without reducing attachment or development to autonomic synchrony.

### The invalidation and acceptable-character branch

A caregiver may stop visible distress without resolving the child's condition.

```text
the child signals distress
        ↓
the adult becomes uncomfortable, punitive or withdrawn
        ↓
the child inhibits expression, attends to the adult or performs calm
        ↓
interactional pressure falls
        ↓
the adult experiences the situation as settled
        ↓
the child's original condition, signal or recovery demand may remain
```

If this route repeats, the child may practise the version of themselves that makes care easier to obtain or prevents the adult's organisation from becoming the next problem.

The acceptable character may therefore become a **relational regulation strategy**: calm, cheerful, undemanding, mature or helpful behaviour reduces immediate relational pressure while the child's own information remains backgrounded.

F2 must not call this co-regulation merely because two people became quieter.

### Regulation audit

For every “borrowed regulation” claim, ask:

1. What condition or demand changed for the infant?
2. What did the caregiver detect, and how accurate was the read?
3. What did the caregiver materially do?
4. Which infant systems or behaviours were measured?
5. Did the measures match, covary, alternate or change with a delay?
6. Was influence infant→caregiver, caregiver→infant, bidirectional or shared-context?
7. Did visible distress reduce because the condition changed, expression was inhibited or capacity was depleted?
8. Did updating, recovery, restoration or sleep become available?
9. Did the child retain access to the original signal and need?
10. What was learnt about help, connection, action and future recovery?

### Evidence routes to examine

- developmental psychobiology and infant autonomic regulation;
- neonatal thermoregulation and feeding coordination;
- caregiver touch, skin-to-skin contact, carrying, voice and movement;
- social buffering of stress responses;
- parent–infant behavioural and physiological coupling;
- mismatch and repair rather than synchrony alone;
- attachment and emotion-socialisation research;
- preterm, neonatal-intensive-care and medical-context evidence;
- cross-cultural caregiving and sleep or contact practices;
- comparative parental-care and altricial–precocial research;
- critiques and empirical tests of Polyvagal phylogenetic and pathway claims.

### Questions the evidence pass must answer

1. Which infant regulatory processes are endogenous at birth, and how do they change with gestational age and development?
2. Which regulatory conditions require caregiver action, and which can the infant influence through signalling or behaviour?
3. What does each study mean by co-regulation, synchrony, physiological linkage, social buffering, settling, recovery or restoration?
4. Which measures support matching, covariation, lagged influence or bidirectional coupling?
5. Do changes in heart rate, respiration and cortisol occur on compatible timescales, or are they being grouped rhetorically?
6. Which caregiver behaviours alter the child's material conditions, and which primarily provide social information?
7. When does reduced distress correspond to changed conditions, and when might it reflect inhibition, withdrawal, fatigue or depleted capacity?
8. Which claims generalise across mammalian species, and which are specific to human infants or particular developmental windows?
9. What evidence supports a specific vagal contribution, and what would justify naming ventral vagal participation rather than distributed autonomic regulation?
10. What later developmental outcomes are supported prospectively rather than inferred retrospectively?

### Possible F2 integration paragraph

> A young infant regulates through its own developing organism, but many of the conditions that make regulation and recovery possible must be provided or changed by other people. A caregiver may alter warmth, nourishment, contact, movement, sensory demand, protection or opportunities for rest. The infant detects those changes and reorganises through its own physiological systems. Across repetition, the child can learn not only that distress changes, but whether signals bring effective help, whether connection remains available and whether recovery can be expected.

This formulation retains developmental dependence without treating regulation as a state transmitted from one nervous system to another.

## Entry 003 · Where Completion Lives

Scientific-facing subtitle: **Relationally contingent completion, protective resolution and their coupling during early dependence**

**Entry status:** captured · initial integration completed across F1, F2, Fluid emotions and Return · evidence audit pending

### The observation

Connection-preservation and organism-protection do not differ only in what is
at stake. They can also differ in where the condition required for updating
exists.

```text
ORGANISM-PROTECTION

the relevant condition may change through
one's own action · environmental change · practical help

CONNECTION-PRESERVATION

the relevant condition may exist between people and change through
availability · recognition · reciprocity · accountability · repair
```

When the unresolved condition is genuinely mutual, one person cannot produce
relational restoration alone. When the unresolved condition concerns bodily
integrity, resources, exposure or an effective boundary, another person may be
helpful or even practically necessary without reciprocity being inherent to the
problem.

### The developmental coupling

For a dependent child, preserving connection can participate directly in
organism-protection. The same caregiver whose response carries relational
information may also control access to warmth, nourishment, shelter,
protection, orientation and effective action.

```text
caregiver connection
        ↓ is also access to
material and regulatory conditions
        ↓ therefore
possible loss of connection can carry more than relational cost
```

This helps explain why a child may inhibit a signal, monitor the adult, appease
or practise an acceptable role when expression appears to threaten connection.
The process need not be deliberate. Expression, relational consequence and
access to care are being learnt together.

### Three outcomes that must remain separate

| Outcome | What changed | Is another person's response inherent? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Relational restoration** | Mutual availability, recognition, reciprocity, accountability, trust or repair became available | Often yes, because the condition exists between people |
| **Organism-level updating and Return** | Demand changed enough for recruitment to release and wider participation or restoration to become available | No single route; another person may help |
| **Return without relational restoration** | The relationship remained unavailable or unrepaired, while grief, a boundary, revised expectation or reorganisation permitted updating | No, although support may assist |

Another nervous system may therefore be necessary to restore a relationship
without being necessary for every form of regulation or Return.

### Emotion-family boundary

This distinction belongs to the active survival problem and completion
structure, not to fixed emotion buckets. The same fear, sadness, anger, shame or
longing may participate in connection-preservation, organism-protection or both
depending on the condition, available action and what the signal is trying to
preserve.

### Ownership across the TEG-Blue system

| Model owner | What it owns |
| --- | --- |
| **F1 · Foundations of the Fluid Gradient** | Evolutionary availability of both problem-spaces and their coupling through care and dependence |
| **F2 · Developmental Shaping of Emotional Participation** | How early coupling can shape expression, help-seeking, role performance, acceptable-self learning and later expectation |
| **Fluid emotions** | Which condition a signal concerns and which completion pathways could allow it to update |
| **ESC, Regulation and Return** | Whether the live route produced relational restoration, organism-level Return, Return without repair or continued demand |
| **Chronic Gradient and F3** | How unresolved demand, repeated relational cost or practised protection may persist or generalise |
| **Behaviour, Harm, Power and Repair** | Whether another person's conduct was responsive, withholding, coercive, harmful, accountable or reparative |

### Evidence routes to examine

- social buffering and interpersonal emotion regulation;
- attachment, caregiving and developmental dependence;
- infant and caregiver behavioural and physiological coordination;
- relational need, ostracism, belonging and social pain;
- grief, continuing bonds, acceptance and adaptation to irreversible loss;
- boundary efficacy, controllability and action-outcome learning;
- developmental changes in reliance on caregivers and peers;
- cross-cultural variation in care, autonomy, relatedness and emotion socialisation.

### Integration boundary

The component claims can be investigated empirically. The three-outcome
architecture and the distinction between relationally contingent completion and
non-relationally necessary protection remain a TEG-Blue synthesis until tested
as such.
