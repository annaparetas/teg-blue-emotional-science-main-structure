## Decision

**F2 has two inseparable developmental jobs.**

1. It explains what embodied, emotional, learning, communicative and reflective capacities are available from birth; how a dependent child regulates through their own developing organism within conditions other people often have to provide or change; and how participation in bodily, emotional, relational and reflective information may widen through early adulthood.
2. It explains how repeated relational responses shape which signals can be noticed, differentiated, trusted, expressed, investigated and acted upon—particularly when preserving access to care, protection or belonging appears to require the child to conceal their information, adopt another person's explanation or practise an acceptable version of themselves.

These are not separate stories. The same organism↔organism loops through which children receive information, changed conditions and support for recovery are also the loops through which invalidation, relational danger and borrowed certainty can be learned. Emotional information can organise the child before the child has enough language, perspective, independence or power to explain what is happening. As later capacities develop, they may help the child understand experience—but they may also be recruited to construct and maintain the version of self that appears most likely to preserve connection.

Accessible title:

> **Developmental Shaping of Emotional Participation**

Scientific-facing explanatory line:

> How developing capacities and repeated relational consequences shape what a person can notice, differentiate, trust, express, act upon and include in their understanding of themselves.

“Developmental Calibration” captures the fact that experience matters, but it can imply that a child begins with blank settings that another person configures once and for all. The current framework is more precise. It concerns several distinguishable capacities and expectations that remain dynamic, context-sensitive and open to later learning.

The word **calibration** can remain when its target is named: calibration of a prediction, recruitment threshold, attentional priority, danger–safety discrimination or expectation that an action will change an outcome. It should not stand for the person's whole developmental organisation.

## The question F2 owns

> What capacities are available at birth, how do they widen across development, and how may repeated relational outcomes shape whether emotional information can be noticed, identified, expressed, trusted, used for action and included in the child's developing understanding of who they are?

F2 owns developmental learning across seven separable layers:

1. condition detection and signal formation;
2. bodily representation;
3. subjective access;
4. identification and differentiation;
5. expression;
6. action;
7. beliefs, self-explanations and learned expectations concerning emotion, relationship and worth.

These layers can change differently. A child may feel but hide, notice but not identify, understand but be unable to act, or retain access in one relationship while suppressing expression in another. “Emotion override” must therefore identify what was overridden rather than imply that the underlying emotion ceased to exist.

## The core insight worth preserving

The strongest original insight is a developmental asymmetry:

> A child can be affected, learn relational consequences and organise action before they can fully explain what is happening or place another person's behaviour in a wider context.

Repeated outcomes can therefore become part of what the child learns about a signal. Expression may bring support, dismissal, punishment, withdrawal, role reversal or no effective change. Over time, those outcomes may shape what receives attention, what feels relationally costly, which actions appear viable and what later explanations become available.

This is a possible developmental learning route, not a universal account of childhood and not a verdict about one caregiver, culture or adult outcome.

## The developmental story F2 tells

F2 follows two processes across the same years:

```text
CAPACITIES WIDEN

embodied detection and response
        · learning, memory and prediction
        · communication and language
        · emotion differentiation
        · self–other representation
        · perspective and reflection
        · deliberate comparison, choice and revision

AT THE SAME TIME, EXPERIENCE TEACHES

what can be noticed · what can be shown · whose account carries authority
        · which actions change anything
        · what preserves connection
        · what kind of self appears acceptable
```

Development does not take place first and social learning afterwards. The child's capacities widen while those capacities are already being used inside relationships, cultures and material conditions.

## What the child begins with

A newborn is not a blank organism waiting for adults to install settings. From birth, the child already has active capacities for internal and external detection, bodily coordination, arousal and distress, orienting, approach and withdrawal, communication, learning, memory and expectation. These capacities are substantial, but they are not yet adult forms of language, explanation, perspective-taking or deliberate reflective choice.

This creates the asymmetry F2 needs to keep visible:

> The child can experience, learn and adapt before they can fully explain, compare accounts, leave the relationship or understand the adult as a separate person with their own history, limits and motives.

Dependence matters here. For a young child, connection to adults can affect food, shelter, protection, comfort, orientation, language and access to the wider world. The child does not need to reason consciously that connection is necessary for repeated relational outcomes to become biologically and behaviourally important.

### Development begins through dependent regulation and social information

A young infant regulates through their own developing organism, but many of
the conditions that make regulation and recovery possible must be provided or
changed by other people. A caregiver may alter warmth, nourishment, contact,
movement, sensory demand, protection or opportunities for rest. The infant
detects those changes and reorganises through their own sensory, autonomic,
endocrine, metabolic, motor and neural processes.

**Borrowing regulation** can remain as the accessible name for this dependence
if its biological translation remains beside it:

> The infant does not literally borrow another person's nervous system. The
> infant regulates through their own developing organism within conditions
> another person often has to provide or change.

Another person's response can participate in development in at least three
distinguishable ways:

| Developmental process | Question it answers | F2 significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Organism↔organism information loop** | What does another person's response tell the child about their state, the situation and the relationship? | Another response can support differentiation and self-understanding, or make the child's information appear mistaken, dangerous or relationally costly |
| **Caregiver participation in regulation** | What conditions does another person change, and how does the child's organism reorganise? | Effective action can support updating and recovery; silence or lower visible distress does not establish that the condition changed |
| **Where completion lives** | Does the completing condition exist within the organism, the environment or between people? | Some outcomes require relational response; others can occur through environmental change, effective action, grief, boundaries or revised expectation |

Across repetition, these processes can help the child learn whether signals
bring effective help, whether connection remains available during activation,
whether conditions can change and whether recovery can be expected. They can
also teach that the adult's response must be managed before the child's own
condition can be addressed.

This is the bridge between F2's two jobs. The relationships through which
emotional participation develops are also the relationships within which some
signals may become difficult or costly to use.

### When connection is also protection

Early dependence can couple the two survival-relevant problem-spaces that F1
keeps distinct. The caregiver is both another person whose availability matters
and a source of conditions the child cannot yet reliably produce or change
alone.

```text
connection to the caregiver
        ↓ may provide
warmth · nourishment · protection · orientation · effective action
        ↓ can alter
organism-level demand · recovery conditions · what the child learns to expect
```

This coupling helps explain why emotional invalidation can shape more than
visible expression. If showing a feeling appears to threaten the same
relationship through which care and protection remain available, the child may
face two competing demands:

```text
keep the organism's information visible
                    ↕
preserve access to the person on whom the child depends
```

Repeatedly protecting connection may make concealment, appeasement, monitoring,
role performance or an acceptable explanation easier to recruit. The point is
not that a child consciously chooses relationship over an authentic self. It is
that expression, action, relational consequence and access to care are being
learnt together while independence and reflective comparison are still
developing.

The distinction remains important later. Another person's response may be
necessary for mutual recognition or relational repair, but not for every form
of organism-level regulation or Return. A person may eventually update through
grief, an effective boundary, revised expectation or reorganisation even when
the original relationship is not restored. F2 therefore distinguishes
**relational restoration**, **organism-level Return** and **Return without
relational restoration**.

## A broad developmental timeline

The windows below overlap. They describe capacities becoming more available, not deadlines, maturity scores or a claim that all children develop in the same way. Culture, health, disability, neurodivergence, language, relationships, stress and opportunity can change the timing and form of participation.

| Broad window | Capacities already present or becoming more available | What relational experience can begin teaching |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Birth** | Internal and external detection, pain and distress, autonomic organisation, orienting, approach–withdrawal responses, early learning, memory and expectation | Repeated changes in contact, warmth, feeding, discomfort and response begin forming expectations without requiring words or reflective explanation |
| **First year** | Rapid sensory–affective learning, increasing intentional action, familiarity, categorisation, social attention, joint attention and communication | Whose presence changes distress, whether signals bring contact or action, and which patterns of another person's availability can be anticipated |
| **1–3 years** | Language and symbols expand; bodily and emotional patterns can acquire words; autonomy, refusal, intention and a more explicit self-representation become possible | Which needs, feelings, boundaries and forms of visibility bring support, conflict, ridicule, withdrawal or approval |
| **3–5 years** | Emotion knowledge, simple causal explanation, temporal language, perspective-taking and early executive functions expand | The child can begin building simple explanations such as “I am difficult”, “good children do not cry” or “their feelings matter first” from the accounts and outcomes repeatedly available |
| **5–8 years** | Working memory, inhibition, shifting, rule use, deliberate attention and the ability to revisit experience become more reliable but remain uneven | Family, school and peer rules can become internal standards; controlled behaviour may be praised while the underlying condition or feeling remains unexamined |
| **8–12 years** | More complex coordination of motives, mixed emotions, fairness, competence, status, comparison and several perspectives | Shame, pride, belonging and evaluation can organise a more coherent acceptable self: the version that appears good, strong, useful, calm, successful or easy to keep close |
| **Adolescence** | Abstract reasoning, future simulation, metacognition, identity exploration, peer orientation and sensitivity to status and belonging expand | Earlier relational rules can be questioned, strengthened or transferred into peers, intimacy, achievement, appearance and group membership; self-presentation can become more deliberate |
| **Early adulthood** | Longer-horizon planning, independent action and integration of competing information may continue widening; no single developmental finish line exists | Earlier roles may be carried into work, friendship and intimate relationships, encounter contradictory evidence, remain context-bound or begin to be revised through new conditions and reflection |

The timeline should remain connected to ESS, CLS and ME without turning them into maturational stages:

- **ESS:** emotional–somatic information is active from birth and continues developing in precision, differentiation, weighting, learned prediction and relation to action.
- **CLS:** learning, memory, discrimination and prediction have early foundations; language-based comparison, explanation, counterfactual thinking and metacognition widen gradually.
- **ME:** conscious participation may become more able to notice, name, compare, question, choose and revisit. F2 does not date the beginning of consciousness or place ME above the organism.

No system waits for another to finish developing. They participate concurrently and recurrently with different ranges of availability across time and conditions.

## When emotion is treated as noise, error or wrongdoing

F2 needs a clearer account of invalidation because the social response becomes
part of what the child can learn about emotional information.

In this framework, **emotion invalidation** is a working description for
repeated interactions in which a child's emotional experience or report is
treated as meaningless, false, excessive, shameful, dangerous, inconvenient or
evidence that the child is the problem—without helping them examine the signal,
the situation, the interpretation and the action separately.

Caregivers are one possible source of this learning. Peers, schools, health and
care systems, religious or community settings, media and wider cultural rules
can also teach which emotions are credible, permitted, shameful or ignored.

Invalidation is not every disagreement, correction or limit. An adult can
recognise that a child is frightened while checking whether danger is present;
acknowledge anger while preventing harm; or limit expression while helping the
child preserve access to what the feeling is communicating. Validation does not
require agreement with every conclusion or permission for every behaviour.

The developmental distinction is:

> **The experience can be real, the first interpretation can be incomplete, and
> the behaviour still requires its own evaluation.**

When this distinction is repeatedly unavailable, several parts of emotional
participation may change. They do not have to change together.

| What happens repeatedly | What may become harder or more costly | What does not follow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emotional expression is dismissed, mocked, punished or met with withdrawal | Showing the feeling, asking for help or testing its meaning with another person | That the underlying emotional–somatic activity stopped |
| The adult supplies the only acceptable explanation | Trusting one's own observations, holding uncertainty or comparing several possible meanings | That the child's first explanation was necessarily correct |
| Belonging depends on appearing calm, grateful, agreeable or unaffected | Differentiated expression of anger, grief, fear, disappointment, need or limits | That visible quiet established safety, updating or recovery |
| Attention shifts towards managing the adult's response | Noticing one's own condition before appeasing, performing, concealing or monitoring | That the resulting behaviour reveals one fixed attachment or Gradient Position |
| Action or protest repeatedly changes nothing, makes danger worse or brings no repair | Expecting boundaries, help-seeking or direct action to be effective | That passivity, numbness or delayed action is chosen or permanent |
| Contradictory emotional information is repeatedly treated as irrational | Allowing emotional–somatic information to revise a coherent reflective account | That logic disappeared; reflective–symbolic processing may help preserve the accepted explanation |

## The acceptable character

The word **character** can remain in F2 if we define what it means here.

> **The acceptable character is a practised organisation of expression, behaviour, attention and self-explanation built around the version of the child that appears most likely to preserve care, approval, safety or belonging.**

This is not necessarily a deliberate performance. It can develop gradually from thousands of ordinary comparisons:

- what the child feels and what adults say is happening;
- what the child needs and what the relationship can tolerate;
- what brings interest, warmth or praise and what brings ridicule, conflict or withdrawal;
- what the child observes privately and what they are allowed to acknowledge publicly;
- what protects the child's integrity and what appears to preserve connection.

The child may learn to be the one who is calm, cheerful, grateful, mature, useful, strong, successful, agreeable or never in need. Another child may learn to become entertaining, invisible, highly competent, endlessly understanding or responsible for everyone else's emotional state. These are examples of relationship-preserving roles, not personality types.

The route can be represented as:

```text
an emotional or bodily signal forms
        ↓
expression, need or action meets a repeated relational consequence
        ↓
the child detects what appears to preserve connection or reduce danger
        ↓
some signals remain private, mistrusted, redirected or difficult to use
        ↓
permitted behaviour and explanations receive more practice
        ↓
“this is what I must be” becomes easier to represent than
“this is what I learned to show here”
        ↓
the acceptable character can begin to feel like identity
```

The important point is not that a hidden “real self” remains intact behind a false mask. The developing person is real throughout. What can diverge are signal, felt access, public expression, available action, relational role and self-explanation. Some parts of experience receive recognition and practice; others remain costly, underdeveloped, context-bound or difficult to include in the person's account of themselves.

Nor does F2 need to conclude that the adults did not love the child. Adults can feel love while also making access to approval, calm, closeness or care conditional in practice. The developmental question is what the child repeatedly had to do, hide, manage or become in order for connection to remain most available.

### How both information systems can participate

The acceptable character is not created by cognition defeating emotion.

- Emotional–somatic processing continues registering need, hurt, alarm, anger, longing, relief and relational consequence.
- Reflective–symbolic processing learns names, rules, causal explanations, ideals and predictions about what a good or lovable person is expected to be.
- ME participates from whatever information and action are sufficiently available, sometimes consciously performing the role and sometimes experiencing it simply as “who I am”.

This is how a solution first organised around a relationship can later become self-maintaining without becoming the person's essence.

### What continues beneath reduced participation

Invalidation does not remove the emotional–somatic system. Bodily organisation,
attention, expectation and action readiness may continue changing even when the
person cannot identify a feeling, distrusts it, conceals it or explains it away.
Reflective–symbolic processing also continues. It may help question the social
message, but it may also build a coherent account in which the feeling is
irrelevant, dangerous or proof of personal wrongness.

The Two Biological Information Systems model therefore does not describe ESS
producing a signal that CLS later suppresses. Both forms of processing remain
active and recurrent. Development may shape what information passes between
them, what becomes consciously usable by ME and which explanations or actions
have previously carried relational cost.

### One possible handoff into Chronic participation

The Chronic emotions page offers a useful comparison: the original relevance
of a signal may remain while its weighting, felt access, interpretation,
expression or use changes. Repeated invalidation may contribute to several
possible patterns:

- **fusion:** feeling and anticipated rejection arrive together;
- **reduced access:** the feeling becomes faint, delayed, numb or difficult to
  differentiate;
- **performed emotion:** an acceptable display replaces or conceals what is
  privately available;
- **intensification:** quieter forms of communication repeatedly fail and
  mobilisation becomes easier to recruit or slower to settle;
- **borrowed certainty:** another person's account becomes easier to use than
  one's own incomplete or costly information;
- **restricted action:** help, protest, boundaries, grief or repair appear
  unavailable, unsafe or ineffective.

This is a developmental possibility, not a single causal chain. Similar
patterns can arise through continuing danger, pain, illness, sensory or
communication mismatch, discrimination, material instability, repeated loss,
interrupted recovery or other conditions. A present Chronic pattern cannot be
used to reconstruct a person's childhood.

### The Regulation warning

Invalidation may reduce visible conflict by producing silence, compliance,
concealment or an acceptable emotional display. F2 should not call that
co-regulation or successful settling without further evidence. It may be
**imposed stability**: the interaction became easier for the adult while the
child carried the uncertainty, inhibition or recovery cost.

The acceptable character can therefore become a regulation route. Being good,
useful, cheerful, invisible, accomplished or emotionally undemanding may help
preserve contact and reduce immediate relational pressure. That relief can be
real. But relief does not establish that the original condition changed, the
emotion became understandable, a viable action became available or recovery
completed.

F2 should follow regulation through the whole route:

```text
condition · signal · felt access · interpretation · expression · action
        · relational consequence · pressure reduction · updating · recovery
```

Stopping at visible behaviour would make the adult's experience of the
interaction the measure of whether the child regulated successfully.

The relevant questions are therefore not only whether expression stopped, but:

1. Did the child remain able to recognise the experience?
2. Could the condition and interpretation be examined?
3. Was a safe and effective action available?
4. Did the interaction provide credible information, support or repair?
5. Could recruitment reduce, recovery continue and wider participation return?
6. Did the child need to become a particular version of themselves for
   connection to remain available?

The answers may differ. A child can become quiet while the condition remains,
feel relief while learning concealment, or receive a limit while retaining full
access to the emotional information.

### Two opposite interpretive risks

The Chronic page identifies two risks that F2 can now connect developmentally:

- **Feeling–Fact Collapse:** “I feel it, therefore the external conclusion must
  be true.”
- **Emotional-State Disconnection:** “I do not feel it, therefore nothing is
  happening.”

Emotionally literate development avoids choosing between them. It helps the
person retain the signal as information, test its interpretation against
conditions and evidence, and keep behaviour and impact on their own plane.

| The caregiver unknowingly transmits their own state | Transmission is not one state-to-state mechanism, and awareness alone does not determine impact | Separate modelling, interaction, physiological coordination, emotion talk, contingency, material conditions, role assignment and repair |

## Relationship to ESS, CLS and ME

F2 clarifies development without turning the three TEG-Blue terms into a maturational ladder.

- **ESS:** embodied and affective information is active early; development may change its differentiation, weighting, accessibility and relation to action without proving that signal generation stopped.
- **CLS:** memory, discrimination and prediction have early foundations; language-based comparison, explanation, counterfactual reasoning and metacognition develop gradually rather than arriving as a separate ruler over emotion.
- **ME:** conscious participation can widen as the child becomes more able to notice, name, compare, question, choose and revisit. F2 does not date the beginning of consciousness or define ME as an executive controller.

The healthy relation is not logic defeating emotion. It is increasing capacity for embodied and reflective information to become jointly available to the person, who may then investigate meaning, conditions, options and consequences.

## Culture, context and power

F2 should not reduce development to a private caregiver–child nervous-system exchange. Emotional learning also occurs within:

- cultural display and interpretation rules;
- gender, race, class, disability and neurodivergence-related expectations;
- family roles and institutional demands;
- material safety, instability and available support;
- actual dependence and unequal power;
- peers, siblings, teachers and other caregivers;
- bidirectional interactions in which children also affect the socialisation process.

A limit on expression is not automatically invalidation. Caregivers and communities may constrain expression for immediate safety, coordination or respect while still helping a child recognise and use the underlying information. The key question is what remains readable, expressible and actionable across contexts, and whether support and repair remain possible.

## F2's handoff

F2 ends with developmentally shaped possibilities, expectations and practised routes. F3 begins with the adult maintenance-and-revision question:

> Given present conditions and the information now available, what prediction, meaning, self-position, response route and immediate consequence keep a pattern going—or allow it to change?

The clean handoff is:

```text
inherited repertoire (F1)
        ↓
developmental conditions + repeated relational outcomes (F2)
        ↓
access · expression · action availability · learned expectation
        ↓
present information and whole-organism organisation
        ↓
adult maintenance, revision and response-route selection (F3)
```

F2 can explain how a route may have become available or costly. It cannot reconstruct a person's history from the route they use now.

- **Two inseparable jobs:** explain how participation widens through developing capacities, organism↔organism information and caregiver-provided regulatory conditions, then show how the same relational loops can shape which signals remain accessible, credible, expressible and usable.

- **Evidence status:** initial component anchors and research routes exist; this record remains an architectural decision rather than final source-level validation.

## Grounding science and knowledge fields

F2 requires sciences that can follow change over time and distinguish development from present association. Adult retrospective report and cross-sectional group difference may contribute, but they cannot establish a developmental pathway by themselves.

| Field | What it can ground in F2 | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Developmental psychology and developmental science** | Change in emotion, cognition, relationship, communication and participation across childhood and adolescence | One universal timetable or an individual history inferred from an adult pattern |
| **Developmental neuroscience** | Maturation and experience-related change in distributed neural systems | That one brain measure explains a relationship, emotion or later behaviour |
| **Learning science and behavioural psychology** | Conditioning, reinforcement, discrimination, generalisation, extinction and learned expectation | The meaning of a relationship or the full development of a person |
| **Affective science and emotion socialisation research** | How emotion is noticed, labelled, expressed, responded to and used in context | That constrained expression means absent feeling or permanent loss of access |
| **Social epistemology, epistemic-injustice research and philosophy of emotion** | How credibility, interpretive resources and authority affect whose emotional reports can be recognised, questioned and understood | A biological mechanism, one developmental outcome or proof that an emotional interpretation is externally accurate |
| **Attachment, caregiving and family research** | Patterns of availability, buffering, expectation, interaction and repair | A diagnosis, fixed attachment identity or one-way caregiver causation |
| **Developmental psychopathology** | Multiple pathways to similar outcomes, different outcomes after similar exposures, timing, risk and protection | That difficulty is inevitable, permanent or produced by one event |
| **Paediatrics, child health and life-course epidemiology** | Health, sleep, pain, disability, stress exposure, social conditions and outcomes over time | A unique psychological mechanism from a population association |
| **Public health and social epidemiology** | Material conditions, inequality, discrimination, community exposure and population-level protection | An individual's internal state or personal developmental story |
| **Education and learning-environment research** | How classrooms, teaching, peer settings, language and access needs shape participation | That school performance reveals emotional capacity or family history |
| **Developmental and cultural anthropology** | Variation in caregiving, childhood, emotion meaning, roles and developmental expectations across communities | One culture as uniform or one practice as universally protective or harmful |
| **Sociology of childhood, family and education** | Roles, institutions, class, gender, race, disability, policy and unequal developmental conditions | A biological mechanism or a person's motives inferred from social position |
| **Communication science and psycholinguistics** | Language development, emotion vocabulary, interaction and the social availability of explanation | Direct access to unspoken feeling or proof that language created the signal |
| **Disability studies, neurodiversity research and participatory research** | Lived access, environmental fit, communication difference and questions missed by deficit models | A single biological mechanism or a claim that all members of a group share one experience |
| **Social work and community psychology** | Family–community interaction, service systems, protection, support and context-sensitive intervention | Independent proof of every developmental mechanism proposed by a practice model |
| **Genetics and epigenetics** | Heritable variation and carefully specified biological responses to experience | Genetic destiny, inherited trauma as shorthand, or direct transmission of a social rule |
| **Trauma, stress and adversity research** | Timing, dose, controllability, recurrence, recovery, buffering and heterogeneity following adverse conditions | That all developmental shaping is trauma or that adult presentation reveals the exposure |

Forensic psychology is a context-specific contributor when developmental knowledge is applied to child protection, custody, testimony, legal capacity or another psycholegal question. It is not the general science of maltreatment, credibility or childhood development, and a forensic opinion must not replace longitudinal evidence.

### Priority evidence

F2 should favour prospective longitudinal studies, repeated developmental observation, natural experiments, within-person change, culturally varied samples and intervention or recovery follow-up. Research should measure the proposed developmental condition, the process expected to change, present context and later participation rather than using diagnosis or adult recollection as a complete developmental record.
