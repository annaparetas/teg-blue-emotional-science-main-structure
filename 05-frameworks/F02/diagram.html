F2 · developmental working file

What develops when emotion must be overridden

Emotional signals can organise the infant long before the child can name or explain them. As reflective and conscious participation develops, the child also learns what follows when those signals are attended to, expressed or used. When doing so threatens safety, belonging or the stability of a relationship, participation may be overridden without the underlying organism simply ceasing to feel.

Working scope. This file first maps what ESS, CLS and conscious participation may make available across broad developmental windows. It then asks how repeated emotion socialisation may reorganise access, expression and action. It does not diagnose a person, infer their childhood from current behaviour or claim one inevitable outcome.

01 · Development before overriding

The organism can respond long before the child can explain

Before asking what emotional overriding changes, we need to ask what each part of the developing organism could already do. ESS begins with substantial embodied capacity. The foundations of CLS are present, but its reflective and symbolic functions develop gradually. Conscious participation also changes—not as a third information system, but as the child becomes increasingly able to turn towards, interpret and use what becomes available.

ESS · active from birth

Embodied information is already moving

The newborn detects internal and external change, organises arousal and distress, orients, responds and learns. What develops is its precision, differentiation, learned predictions and range of regulation.

CLS · foundations first

Learning is not yet reflective-symbolic thought

Memory, discrimination and expectation appear early. Naming, deliberate comparison, explanation and planning across time develop later and should not be projected back onto the newborn.

ME · participation develops

Access does not appear all at once

The person gradually becomes more able to notice, name, test, choose and revisit. This track does not date the beginning of consciousness or place an executive controller above the organism.

Birth Already active

Internal and external detection, autonomic organisation, pain and distress, orienting, approach–withdrawal responses and sensitivity to familiar prenatal sensory patterns are already present.

 Developmental foundations

Learning, memory, discrimination and expectation are possible. These capacities should not yet be redescribed as naming, explanation or deliberate symbolic reflection.

 No absence-of-consciousness claim

The newborn can experience and act without the reflective participation defined here. F2 does not assign an age at which consciousness begins.
First year Co-regulation and rapid learning

Arousal and attention are organised heavily through caregiver interaction. Familiarity, attachment, movement and repeated sensory–affective patterns expand what can be detected and predicted.

 Prediction and intentional action expand

Memory, categorisation, means–end action and increasingly coordinated social attention develop. Joint attention and social referencing become important later in this window and beyond.

 Participation remains deeply relational

The infant increasingly directs attention, action and communication, while relying on others to help organise arousal, meaning and response.
1–3 years Signals acquire words and social consequences

Bodily and emotional patterns become increasingly linked with language, memories, expectations and the consequences of autonomy, frustration and boundaries.

 Language changes what can be represented

Words and symbols make absent objects, simple event sequences, intentions and early narratives increasingly available.

 The self becomes more explicitly representable

The child can increasingly recognise aspects of their body and experience, use some emotion words and revisit an event with another person’s help.
3–5 years Differentiation and regulation expand

Emotional patterns and available responses become more varied, though their organisation remains strongly dependent on relationship and context.

 Explicit social and emotional explanation grows

Perspective-taking, causal explanation, temporal language, emotion knowledge and early executive functions develop rapidly without becoming adult-like.

 Reflection becomes more usable with support

The child can increasingly name, compare and revisit experience, while stress, language and the response of adults still shape what becomes readable.
5–8 years Signals can be held without immediate enactment

Internal states may become easier to recognise and behaviour easier to modulate, although this varies with emotion, demand, safety and learning.

 Executive functions strengthen

Working memory, inhibition, shifting, rule use and deliberate attention become more reliable but remain uneven and task-dependent.

 Monitoring and revision begin to broaden

The child can more often pause, explain, ask for help and reconsider, while confidence in an interpretation may still exceed its accuracy.
8–12 years The social field becomes more complex

Belonging, status, fairness, exclusion, embarrassment, pride, shame, competence and comparison can carry increasingly organised significance.

 Several perspectives can be coordinated

Planning, causal reasoning, mixed-emotion understanding and metacognitive monitoring continue to expand.

 Competing information can be considered

The child can increasingly compare motives, feelings and consequences. Pressure and belonging risk may still narrow that participation.
Adolescence onward Embodied priorities recalibrate and keep learning

Reward, attraction, novelty, status, belonging and social evaluation can acquire new weight. Embodied predictions and thresholds remain plastic into adulthood.

 Abstract and long-horizon cognition expands

Future simulation, hypothetical reasoning, inhibition, metacognition and coordination of complex goals continue refining; there is no single finish line at age 18 or 25.

 Integration can become more flexible

The person may increasingly use information from both systems to test conclusions and choose a response. Availability still depends on conditions, health, learning and support.

What the old diagrams were reaching for

The diagrams held one important developmental asymmetry: the child can already be affected, learn patterns and organise action before they can explain what is happening. Their original labels are too absolute for the present model, so F2 carries the mechanism forward in more precise language.

Old language · “feeling = being” Embodied-affective organisation comes before reflective explanation

Feeling is not the whole person, but bodily and affective processing can organise attention, action and learning before the child can describe the experience.

Old language · “emotion before cognition” Broad cognition is early; reflective logic develops later

Perception, memory, discrimination and prediction are already cognitive. Language-based explanation, deliberate comparison and metacognition require later-developing CLS capacities.

Old language · “real self / role mask” Signal, access, expression, action and self-explanation can diverge

A child may still feel while inhibiting expression, adopting a role or learning an explanation about what their feelings mean. None of those layers alone defines a hidden “real” identity.

Old language · “logic controls emotion” Reflective logic can investigate; it does not become the organism’s ruler

CLS may help contextualise, test and revise an interpretation. Its healthiest function here is participation with ESS information—not automatic override of it.

Emotion and logic do not replace one another

Emotion is not a fourth biological information system, and logic is not a late-arriving opposite of feeling. The two tracks below show the developmental asymmetry more accurately: embodied-affective learning accumulates while the reflective tools able to interpret it are still developing.

ESS-weighted sequence

Experience becomes embodied learning

conditions + event→bodily-affective response→attention + action→outcome→repeated prediction

This sequence can alter what feels urgent, safe, costly or possible without waiting for a verbal account.

CLS developmental sequence

Experience becomes increasingly investigable

perception + memory→symbols + language→causal explanation→self–other representation→perspective + metacognition

These capacities do not arrive together. As their range expands, experience can be represented across time, compared with other perspectives and deliberately reconsidered.

ME participation · the developing bridge

What becomes possible when information from both systems is sufficiently available?

experience→notice→name→understand→question an interpretation→compare it with conditions and evidence→choose, act and learn

This is not a one-way ladder and not a promise of constant access. It describes a widening possibility for ME to participate in what happens next, while ESS and CLS continue operating as interacting biological information systems.

Identity-making bridge

Later explanations may form around earlier learning

When a relational outcome repeats, ESS may learn what protects connection or reduces danger before the child can place the adult’s behaviour in a wider context. As CLS develops, the child may use the information available to construct explanations about self, emotion and relationship. Those explanations can become familiar and behaviourally powerful without becoming an essence or an inevitable lifelong identity.

“My need causes trouble.”“Anger costs connection.”“I must read the other person first.”“What I feel cannot be trusted.”

Developmental boundary

These are broad, overlapping windows rather than deadlines. Development varies across children, tasks, cultures, relationships and conditions. The next research pass must distinguish what is present at birth, learned rapidly, supported by another person and gradually available for independent reflective use.

02 · Conditions around emotion

“Override it” can be taught in several ways

The lesson may be explicit or implicit, harsh or affectionate, occasional or pervasive. What matters scientifically is the response the child repeatedly encounters, the emotion involved, the developmental timing and whether repair is available.

Punish or shame

“Stop crying.” “Don’t be angry.” Ridicule, threat, withdrawal or punishment makes expression costly.

Minimise or misread

The feeling is called excessive, unjustified, manipulative or irrelevant, so the child’s interpretation loses relational support.

Reward toughness or compliance

Belonging, praise or reduced conflict becomes easier when need, pain, fear, anger or disagreement stays out of view.

Require role reversal

The child monitors, comforts or stabilises the adult; the other person’s state receives priority over the child’s own signal.

Model suppression

Adults hide their own activation or avoid emotional language. The lesson is carried by interaction, not only by instruction.

Dampen positive emotion

Pride, excitement, affection, gratitude or joy may also be discouraged when visibility, pleasure or enthusiasm is treated as unsafe or inappropriate.

First boundary

Caregivers sometimes limit expression for safety, timing, cultural coordination or respect without teaching disconnection from emotion. The research question is not whether every limit is invalidating, but whether the child can still recognise the signal, receive help understanding it and use it flexibly later.

No one-to-one developmental route

These condition families are research territories, not causes assigned to a Gradient Position. Similar conditions can be met by different adaptations, and similar adult patterns can arise through different developmental and later-life routes. F2 cannot be used to infer a person’s history from present behaviour.

04 · Repetition and learning

What receives practice can become easier to recruit

This is the developmental bridge that Chronic research must test. It separates what ESS detects, what actions are possible, what CLS learns and what ME can practise. Each arrow needs evidence; the sequence is not assumed to occur in every child.

ESS · RELEVANCE

A signal forms

Pain, fear, anger, shame, sadness, joy, pride, love, need or another change becomes organism-relevant.

ACTION + OUTCOME

Some responses are possible; others are costly

Approach, refusal, escape, expression or help-seeking may bring support, dismissal, punishment, withdrawal—or no change at all.

CLS · LEARNING

A relational expectation may develop

Repeated outcomes may increase expectations such as “showing this brings help”, “anger costs connection”, “their state comes first” or “nothing I do changes it”. Other relationships and contexts may carry different learning.

ME · PRACTICE

Participation widens or narrows

The child practises what can be noticed, trusted, differentiated, checked, expressed and used for action.

RECRUITMENT + UPDATING

The available route becomes familiar

A practised solution may become quicker to reach. If the original requirement cannot be acted upon or repaired, the learned expectation may remain difficult to update.

ESS · WORKING TRANSLATION

The signal can still move first

Emotional and bodily organisation may begin before reflection. Developmental learning may change its weighting or accessibility without proving that the signal was never generated.

CLS · WORKING TRANSLATION

Cognition learns the relational rule

Prediction, attention, inhibition and explanation can be recruited to hide, redirect or overrule what would otherwise be expressed or acted upon.

ME · WORKING TRANSLATION

Conclusions are formed from what becomes available

The person may consciously decide that the feeling is excessive, unsafe or irrelevant—or later revisit that conclusion when wider access and safer conditions become possible.

Not destiny

Temperament, the specific emotion, other caregivers, peers, culture, present safety, language, health, adversity type and opportunities for repair can all alter this pathway. Children also affect caregivers; socialisation is dynamic and bidirectional.

05 · Connection to the three processes

Three forms of ME participation can develop unevenly

ME is not created or destroyed by adversity. What develops through experience is the person’s access to, trust in and use of information. These forms of participation need separate developmental questions and cannot be represented by one general “awareness” measure.

ME participation · what receives practice

A signal becoming conscious is only one part of the route

notice→differentiate→trust enough to investigate→integrate ESS + CLS→test the interpretation→choose and act→observe the outcome→update

A child may have access to a signal yet be unable to act on it, or understand another person accurately while their own experience remains backgrounded. Chronic change can therefore involve reduced access, but also fusion, mistrust, rigid certainty, compulsory use or instrumental use.

Configuration bridge

F2 asks how access and use may be shaped across development. The companion ME Access Dials page explores how the three forms of participation may combine in the present. The Chronic table must then distinguish what persists within one episode from what recovers incompletely, is reconstructed later, acquires a lower recruitment threshold, generalises, loses discrimination, updates only in some contexts or remains relevant because adverse conditions continue.

Several Chronic routes. A developmental pattern does not become Chronic through one universal sequence. Chronic organisation may involve prolonged persistence, interrupted recovery, repeated reconstruction, broader generalisation, reduced danger–safety discrimination, restricted expectations about effective action, context-bound updating, repeated mental reactivation or ongoing correspondence with continuing conditions. These routes may combine, and each requires different evidence.
06 · Possible outcomes to investigate

Several adaptations can look like “not feeling”

These are research distinctions, not personality types and not conclusions about any individual.

Expression inhibited

Felt but hidden

The child experiences and may understand the emotion, while face, voice, words or action remain controlled.

Access narrowed

Bodily pressure without a clear feeling

Activation or discomfort may be available while identification, localisation or meaning remains vague.

Agency interrupted

Felt and understood, but no viable action

The child can accurately recognise anger, fear, hurt or need while refusal, approach, escape, help-seeking or repair remains unsafe or ineffective.

Other foregrounded

The relationship is read before the self

The caregiver’s mood and likely response receive priority while the child’s signal waits or becomes difficult to locate.

Capacity dissociated

Another can be read without being affectively shared

Mentalizing may remain detailed while affective sharing, self-access or concern differs. High availability is not the same as accurate, flexible or prosocial use.

Context-sensitive recovery

Access can return elsewhere

A child may remain expressive with safe people, in private or later in life. Suppression in one relationship is not proof of global incapacity.

07 · Initial evidence ledger

What the current sources can carry

These anchors prepare the research programme. No single study establishes the full developmental sequence above.

  • Gross & Levenson (1993)
    DIRECT · EXPRESSION

    Experimental suppression reduced visible expression without reducing subjective emotion and altered physiology. Adult, short-term evidence; it does not establish developmental effects.

  • Waters et al. (2020)
    DIRECT · DYADIC EXPERIMENT

    Parent suppression changed physiological linkage and reduced warmth and engagement during parent–child interaction. It tests an immediate interaction, not a chronic child outcome.

  • Reschke et al. (2024)
    BRIDGE · LONGITUDINAL

    Parent questions and emotion talk showed prospective relations with children’s emotion knowledge. Association does not make language the only developmental mechanism.

  • Edler et al. (2025)
    BRIDGE · LONGITUDINAL

    Emotion-dismissing family profiles were longitudinally associated with later adjustment outcomes. Profiles are heterogeneous and do not identify Gradient Positions.

  • Yap et al. (2008)
    BRIDGE · POSITIVE AFFECT

    Dampening or invalidating adolescents’ positive affect related to dysregulated behaviour, strategies and depressive symptoms. The design does not prove a single causal route.

  • O’Neal et al. (2017)
    BRIDGE · ANGER-SPECIFIC

    Observed discouragement of preschool anger predicted change in later depression, moderated by child anger intensity. It shows why emotion and temperament must be specified.

  • Louie et al. (2013)
    CONTEXT BOUNDARY

    Links among parental control and children’s expression differed across Korean, Asian American and European American families. Meaning cannot be detached from culture.

  • Zhu et al. (2024)
    BIDIRECTIONALITY BOUNDARY

    Observed mother–child coaching, dismissing and expression changed dynamically from ages three to six. Children participate in the socialisation process; arrows cannot be assumed one-way.

08 · Research before Chronic rows

Questions the next search must answer

Which emotion?

Fear, anger, sadness, shame, joy, love, pride and need may receive different responses and produce different adaptations.

Which layer changed?

Separate bodily representation, access, differentiation, expression, action and belief rather than using “awareness” as one variable.

What was the relational contingency?

Measure support, minimisation, punishment, withdrawal, role reversal, inconsistency and repair—not only a total adversity score.

Was danger still present?

Do not call strategic suppression dysfunctional when expression remains genuinely unsafe in the current environment.

Did the pattern generalise?

Test whether it appears across people and contexts, and whether safe relationships restore access or expression.

What remained possible?

Look for resilience, selective access, private feeling, alternative caregivers, later learning and successful recovery—not only impairment.

Model boundary. F2 can ground mechanisms by which emotion participation may be shaped. It cannot establish a child’s history from adult behaviour, turn socialisation into a diagnosis, equate emotional restraint with pathology or use protective learning to erase later impact and responsibility. The next Chronic pass must describe the present organisation first; developmental history remains one possible explanatory route, not a conclusion contained in the cell.
