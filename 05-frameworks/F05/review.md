# F5 · Social Valuation, Status and Unequal Conditions

## Decision

**Keep F5 as a Foundational Framework, but move from “worth as regulation” to the observable processes of social valuation, status and unequal conditions.**

Provisional title:

> **Social Valuation, Status and Unequal Conditions**

Suggested explanatory line:

> How groups and institutions rank people, roles, needs or knowledge—and how those rankings shape recognition, credibility, protection, access, resources and exposure.

The original title identifies a real problem. Some differences become more than differences: they become reasons why one person's account is believed, another person's distress is dismissed, one group receives protection and another carries more risk.

“Worth,” however, contains two different questions. Human worth is an ethical commitment; it is not a biological quantity that science can measure. Social valuation is observable. It appears in status, credibility, attention, opportunity, material distribution, policy and the consequences attached to a category or position.

F5 should study that social valuation without suggesting that hierarchy is produced by a shared nervous-system need. Unequal conditions can affect health and organism organisation. Those effects are downstream consequences and feedback routes, not the mechanism that created the hierarchy.

## The question F5 owns

> How are people, roles, needs, knowledge and social positions valued differently, through what practices and institutions, and with what effects on recognition, access, conditions and future possibility?

F5 owns questions concerning:

- which social categories and positions are being distinguished;
- what value, credibility, competence, deservingness or danger is attached to them;
- who has authority to make or apply the classification;
- whose needs, knowledge and reality receive recognition;
- how protection, opportunity, labour, risk and resources are distributed;
- how apparently neutral criteria produce different effects;
- how advantage and disadvantage accumulate across time and institutions;
- how people anticipate, internalise, contest or refuse social valuation;
- how hierarchies change, reproduce or become more difficult to see.

It does not own intrinsic human worth, one person's motive for seeking status, the biological cause of inequality, every difference in role or outcome, a diagnosis of people in advantaged or disadvantaged positions, or the full assessment of coercion, oppression and repair.

## The core insight worth preserving

A hierarchy becomes visible when a difference changes what someone can realistically expect.

Two people may speak with equal clarity while only one is treated as credible. Two children may show the same need while one is described as sensitive and the other as difficult. Two workers may perform comparable labour while one receives recognition and the other remains replaceable. The important observation is not only that people are ranked. The ranking changes conditions.

The strongest original insight is therefore:

> Social valuation can become materially consequential by shaping whose needs, knowledge, safety and future possibilities receive protection or investment.

Several distinctions follow:

- human worth is not the same as socially assigned value;
- difference is not automatically hierarchy;
- hierarchy is not automatically oppression;
- role authority is not proof of greater human worth;
- status is not one psychological motive;
- unequal outcome is not sufficient evidence of one cause;
- equal treatment can preserve unequal conditions;
- unequal support can sometimes be required for equitable participation;
- internalised inadequacy is not an inevitable response to exclusion;
- material advantage is not proof of ease, health or emotional integration;
- disadvantage does not indicate incapacity;
- biological consequence does not make a social hierarchy biological in origin.

## The status of the Filter of Worth

**Filter of Worth can remain as a TEG-Blue recognition label, but not as F5's central mechanism.**

It names a particular confusion:

> Social access, recognition or protection is treated as evidence of a person's inherent value, while deprivation or exclusion is treated as evidence of personal inadequacy.

That confusion may appear in self-understanding, interaction, policy, institutional practice or cultural narrative. The routes are not identical. A person may internalise a ranking, strategically accommodate it, openly reject it, move between responses or experience several at once. An institution may reproduce a hierarchy without any participant holding one fully conscious belief about worth.

The inquiry should therefore be decomposed:

```text
social category or position
        ↓
assigned meaning, status or credibility
        ↓
treatment, access, protection or exposure
        ↓
immediate and cumulative consequences
        ↓
interpretation, participation, resistance or revision
```

The label is useful when it helps expose the movement from social treatment to assumed human value. It should not become a score, person-type or hidden explanation for every hierarchy.

## Seven F5 construct families

| Construct family | Governing question | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **F5-A · Classification and social boundary** | Which categories, roles or distinctions organise treatment, and how are their boundaries produced or changed? | A category may describe, enable, protect, stigmatise or do several things; classification is not automatically hierarchy |
| **F5-B · Valuation, status and prestige** | What respect, competence, deservingness or priority is assigned to each position? | Status may be formal, informal, local or contested and does not measure human worth |
| **F5-C · Recognition and credibility** | Whose experience, knowledge, needs and testimony are treated as legible or authoritative? | Disagreement or correction is not automatically epistemic harm; pattern, power and treatment of evidence matter |
| **F5-D · Access, resources and exposure** | Who receives protection, time, care, education, income, opportunity or support, and who carries risk, labour, surveillance or deprivation? | Distribution cannot be explained by attitudes alone; policy, history, markets, institutions and material constraints may participate |
| **F5-E · Reproduction and cumulative effect** | How do advantages and disadvantages continue, compound or change across events, institutions and generations? | Continuity may be social, material, legal, educational, geographic or biological; these routes must not be collapsed |
| **F5-F · Anticipation, internalisation and participation** | How do people anticipate evaluation and respond through presentation, striving, silence, accommodation, refusal, solidarity or resistance? | A person's response does not prove endorsement, inferiority, pathology or the complete origin of the hierarchy |
| **F5-G · Contestation, redistribution and recognition repair** | How are categories, criteria, resources and decision processes challenged or revised? | Inclusion language or policy change does not by itself redistribute resources, restore credibility or repair accumulated effects |

These families are a provisional TEG-Blue integration. They are not validated stages, types or a claim that every hierarchy develops through the same sequence.

## F5 is not a nervous-system theory of inequality

The original account treats status and resources mainly as ways to stabilise nervous systems. This makes material and political conditions sound secondary to individual regulation.

Money can determine whether someone has housing, food, healthcare, legal help, time and the ability to leave. Credibility can determine whether a report is investigated. Citizenship can determine access and exposure. These are conditions before they are signals about conditions.

The Gradient may help examine how a person organises within unequal conditions. It cannot explain why wages differ, why a category is stigmatised, how property or law distributes power, or why an institution recognises one account over another.

The direction of explanation must remain visible:

```text
social, material and institutional conditions
        ↓
different access, demand, exposure and available action
        ↓
possible organism-level effects within people
```

Feedback may run back into behaviour and institutions, but a physiological effect does not turn the original social process into biology.

## How F5 connects to the whole

| Current layer | What F5 receives or contributes | Boundary of the connection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **F1 · evolutionary foundations** | Broad capacities for social comparison, affiliation, coalition, learning and status sensitivity may contribute to human social life | F1 cannot establish a modern hierarchy, justify inequality or make a social category biologically natural |
| **F2 · developmental shaping** | Children develop within unequal material, relational and institutional conditions and may learn how their identity or needs are treated | Adult position cannot reconstruct childhood, and developmental effects do not explain the social structure that produced exposure |
| **F3 · adult maintenance and revision** | Adults interpret status, anticipate evaluation and participate through ambition, accommodation, concealment, solidarity, refusal or change | One inner strategy cannot explain a hierarchy, and status-seeking cannot be assumed to be disguised safety-seeking |
| **F4 · shared rules** | F4 hands forward rules and practices that distribute recognition, credibility, access, labour, protection or resources unevenly | Not every rule or role difference is a worth hierarchy; patterned valuation and differential consequence must be shown |
| **Model 1 and Model 2** | Social position and unequal treatment can be part of the present conditions a person detects and organises within | A Gradient Position cannot be inferred from class, race, gender, disability, occupation or status |
| **Chronic Gradient** | Recurrent discrimination, deprivation, insecurity or exclusion may contribute to repeated demand, constrained action and difficult recovery | Chronic organisation does not prove oppression, and population inequality cannot diagnose an individual nervous system |
| **Harm, Power and Repair** | F5 identifies unequal status, credibility and resources; HPR examines how power is used, whose agency changes and what repair requires | Asymmetry is not automatically abuse, while absence of visible aggression does not make an unequal arrangement harmless |
| **F6 · bias** | F5 hands forward the categories and valuations that may shape attention, interpretation, credibility and decision | Bias cannot be inferred only from unequal outcome, and F6 needs its own mechanisms and evidence |
| **F7 · domination** | F5 can identify durable asymmetries and unequal option fields that may become available for enforcement | Hierarchy does not inevitably become domination; behaviour, authority, imposed cost and restricted agency must be observed |
| **F8 · repair** | Redistribution, restored recognition, institutional change and changed access may be part of repair | Personal insight or apology does not redistribute resources or repair institutional effects |
| **F10 · generational bridges** | Resources, neighbourhoods, education, roles, stories, law and institutional treatment can continue or change across generations | Intergenerational inequality must not be reduced to epigenetic or caregiver transmission |

## Reading the original F5 language against the current system

### Preserve, with precise translation

| Original direction | Current reading |
| --- | --- |
| Rules can begin deciding whose needs and reality matter | Repeated rules and practices may distribute recognition, credibility, protection and access unevenly |
| Resources affect the conditions in which development occurs | Economic, social, cultural, political and institutional resources can change actual exposure, opportunity and available action |
| Hierarchies can reproduce themselves | Material transfer, networks, education, law, geography, institutional practice and social learning may contribute through different routes |
| Social invisibility can carry cost | Repeated non-recognition, exclusion or credibility loss may affect participation, opportunity, distress and health depending on context and duration |
| Assigned worth may become self-understanding | People may internalise, anticipate, strategically accommodate, reinterpret or resist social valuation |
| Advantage can compound | Small differences in access or recognition may accumulate across decisions and institutions, while intervention and changed conditions may alter the pathway |

### Narrow, revise or retire

| Map wording or implication | Why it needs care | Safer direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Once rules exist, they sort” | Some rules coordinate without ranking human value; sorting may also precede or shape the rule | Ask whether and how the rule distributes status, credibility, access, protection, labour or resources |
| Status-seeking “underneath” works as a safety strategy | Ambition and status participation can involve interest, mastery, recognition, duty, competition, material need, identity or several motives | Examine stated goals, conditions, anticipated consequences and behaviour without assigning one hidden motive |
| Resources are nervous-system stabilisers | Resources first alter actual conditions and options; physiological effects are one possible downstream route | Name the resource, who controls it, the practical difference it makes and any measured health pathway separately |
| Hierarchy distributes how the next generation's capacities are built | Unequal conditions can shape developmental opportunity, but capacities, outcomes and pathways vary | Measure exposure, timing, process, opportunity, support and later change rather than treating position as destiny |
| Position reproduces itself “partly through biology” | This can imply biological inheritance of hierarchy and hide social, material, legal and institutional continuity | Separate prenatal, health, caregiving, learning, wealth, education, geography, policy and discrimination pathways |
| Deprivation becomes internalised inadequacy | Internalisation is possible but not inevitable; people may retain critical understanding, solidarity and resistance | Ask how social treatment is interpreted, what alternative accounts are available and how response varies by context |
| The filter is maintained “from both sides” through the same work | This flattens power asymmetry and can make people carrying disadvantage equally responsible for the hierarchy | Distinguish institutional production, enforcement, advantaged participation, constrained compliance, adaptation and resistance |
| “Chronic invisibility is chronic social threat” | Invisibility, misrecognition, exclusion and threat are not interchangeable and require measures, context and timescale | Specify the experience, conditions, outcome and population; then test biological and behavioural pathways separately |
| “Cost: equity” | Some formal hierarchy or differentiated support may serve coordination, expertise, care or accessibility | Examine necessity, proportionality, process, contestability, distribution and effect rather than assigning one universal cost |

## Evidence status of the original source list

The original alignment gathers useful lineages, but it combines sociological theory, psychological constructs, public-health hypotheses and TEG-Blue synthesis as if they were one evidential layer.

- **Bourdieu's forms of capital** can help distinguish economic, social, cultural and symbolic resources and the conditions they alter. Reading capital “physiologically” is a TEG-Blue bridge, not Bourdieu's empirical mechanism.
- **Social-rank and power research** may inform status perception, approach, inhibition and interaction. It cannot explain the production of class, race, gender, caste, disability or institutional hierarchy by itself.
- **Inequality and health research** requires exact exposure, scale, population, outcome and comparison. Allostatic-load findings may help study possible consequences; they do not establish hierarchy as one biological mechanism.
- **The weathering hypothesis** should remain a specific research programme about accumulated exposure and health, not shorthand for every form of social disadvantage.
- **Stigma, contingent self-worth and internalised-oppression research** concern different levels and processes. They should not be merged into one Filter of Worth mechanism.
- **The Matthew effect and cumulative-advantage research** may inform compounding pathways when the resource, decision process and timescale are specified.
- **System justification** should be retained narrowly and claim by claim. It cannot establish that people in different positions maintain a hierarchy equally or for the same reason.
- **Intersectionality** helps examine how social positions and power relations are constituted together. It should not be reduced to an individual identity checklist or used as evidence for “both sides” maintaining a filter.

No dedicated F5 claim decomposition or source-level review has yet been completed. The original names should guide the search, not stand as validation of the framework.

## F5's handoffs

F5 receives a rule or practice from F4 only when differential valuation and consequence are visible:

```text
category, role, rule or institutional practice
        ↓
assigned value, status, credibility or deservingness
        ↓
distribution of access, protection, resources, labour or exposure
        ↓
immediate and cumulative effects
        ↓
participation, resistance, revision or reproduction
```

Three handoffs matter most:

1. **To F6:** when the question becomes how categories and valuations shape attention, interpretation, evidence use or judgement.
2. **To HPR and F7:** when unequal power is used to restrict another person's viable options, enforce an outcome or preserve the hierarchy through imposed cost.
3. **To F10:** when material, social, institutional and narrative routes continue or change across generations.

F5 does not require every hierarchy to progress toward bias or domination. It identifies the sorting and its consequences so later frameworks can ask what happens next.

## Result of this pass

- **Framework status:** retain and substantially reframe.
- **Collection role:** collective framework for examining social valuation and the unequal distribution of recognition, credibility, protection, access, resources and exposure.
- **Title:** revise from “Worth Hierarchies Regulate” to “Social Valuation, Status and Unequal Conditions.”
- **Filter of Worth:** retain as a TEG-Blue recognition label after decomposition, not as a mechanism, score or person-type.
- **Core architecture:** use seven construct families—classification, valuation, recognition, distribution, reproduction, internalisation and participation, and contestation and redistribution.
- **Main protection:** do not reduce hierarchy to nervous-system regulation, infer motive from status, mistake social treatment for human worth or make people carrying disadvantage equally responsible for the structure.
- **F4 connection:** only rules that create patterned valuation or differential consequence enter F5.
- **Evidence status:** architectural first pass only; the original source list requires claim-level separation and cross-disciplinary review.

## Grounding science and knowledge fields

F5 needs fields that can examine inequality at interactional, organisational, population, institutional and historical levels. Individual psychology can inform participation and internalisation, but it cannot ground the social structure alone.

| Field | What it can ground in F5 | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sociology and social stratification research** | Class, status, mobility, institutions, social closure, inequality and patterned life chances | A biological hierarchy or one person's motive from their social position |
| **Social psychology** | Status perception, stigma, social identity, stereotyping, belonging, internalisation and intergroup processes | The material or historical production of a hierarchy by attitudes alone |
| **Political sociology and political science** | Power, citizenship, governance, representation, policy, state action and political inequality | Individual nervous-system organisation or lived experience without corresponding evidence |
| **Economics, labour economics and political economy** | Income, wealth, work, incentives, ownership, labour markets, public finance and resource distribution | Human value reduced to productivity, preference, price or market outcome |
| **Public health and social epidemiology** | Population patterns linking social conditions, discrimination, work, housing and resources with health | A unique causal pathway or individual outcome from a group association |
| **Demography and population studies** | Mobility, mortality, fertility, household structure, migration and inequality across populations and time | Meaning, motive or mechanism without additional evidence |
| **Human geography and urban studies** | Place, segregation, mobility, infrastructure, environmental exposure and spatial access | Individual preference inferred from residence or movement alone |
| **Social and cultural anthropology** | Lived meanings of status, exchange, kinship, obligation, hierarchy and resistance across communities | A culture as uniform or one hierarchy as universal and timeless |
| **History and historical sociology** | The formation and change of categories, property, institutions, labour systems and social movements | Present function or personal belief inferred directly from historical origin |
| **Law-and-society, sociolegal and critical legal research** | How law creates categories, rights, status, eligibility, enforcement and unequal access to remedy | That legality establishes legitimacy, equality or safety in practice |
| **Education research and sociology of education** | Tracking, discipline, recognition, access, attainment, curriculum and institutional opportunity | Ability, motivation or family history inferred from educational outcome alone |
| **Organisational science and sociology of work** | Hiring, promotion, occupational status, emotional labour, authority, networks and workplace inequality | Direct transfer from one organisation or occupation to society as a whole |
| **Criminology and criminal-justice research** | Criminalisation, policing, sentencing, incarceration, victim recognition and unequal system effects | Dangerousness, guilt or criminal intent inferred from social category or disadvantage |
| **Forensic and legal psychology** | Bias, memory, testimony, assessment and decision-making within explicit psycholegal contexts | A general measure of credibility, moral worth or risk outside a bounded legal question |
| **Critical race, feminist, disability, queer, decolonial and Indigenous scholarship** | How categories, institutions, knowledge and power operate together, including experiences dominant measures may miss | One generic theory, a uniform group voice or decorative confirmation of a pre-existing model |
| **Social work, community psychology and participatory research** | Service access, community conditions, lived priorities, implementation, collective action and repair | Independent proof of every mechanism proposed by a practice or advocacy model |
| **Social epistemology and philosophy of recognition** | Credibility, testimony, epistemic authority, misrecognition and the ethical meaning of equal worth | Empirical proof of the distribution or effects of recognition without observation |
| **Behavioural and decision sciences** | Judgement, choice, incentives, categorisation and responses to unequal conditions | A complete explanation of institutions, culture or political economy from individual decisions |
| **Genetics and epigenetics** | Carefully specified biological variation or exposure-related pathways where directly measured | Biological inheritance of class, race, caste, stigma or an intergenerational social hierarchy |

### Priority evidence

F5 should combine longitudinal population data, administrative records, audit and correspondence studies, field and survey experiments, natural experiments and policy evaluation with ethnography, interviews, historical research and participatory evidence. Analyses should state the social position, comparator, institution, timescale and outcome; distinguish descriptive inequality from causal explanation; and examine variation within categories rather than treating identity labels as mechanisms.
