## Decision

**F1 explains the evolutionary foundations of the whole Fluid Gradient.**

It does not explain emotion alone, and it does not present the seven Gradient Positions as seven stages in evolution. It asks how a layered repertoire of viability sensing, bodily coordination, action, care, communication, learning, prediction, reflection and recovery became available—and how those capacities can participate together in present-responsive organisation now.

Emotion belongs inside that account as one important form of biological information. It is neither the whole Gradient nor an irrational interruption to it.

### Artifact ownership

The three current surfaces have different jobs:

- **F1-Timeline** owns the provisional eight-expansion deep-time orientation.
- **F1 → Fluid Gradient crosswalk** owns the compact many-to-many handoff from evolutionary capacity to present Fluid organisation.
- **The Nervous System Organisation Gradient** remains the canonical source for the full seven-position table and its detailed rows.

The bridge table should remain beside F1 rather than becoming a second full table inside the governing organism-organisation premise. The governing premise defines how every Position must be modelled; the canonical Model 2 page defines the current Position content. This keeps the bridge visible without creating two sources of truth.

## The question F1 owns

> How did organisms acquire and retain capacities to detect consequential conditions, reorganise physiology, attention, feeling, thought, relationship and action around them, and update, recover and Return as conditions change—and how does the Fluid Gradient organise those capacities in the present without turning its Positions into evolutionary stages?

F1 owns the deep-time foundations and evolutionary availability of:

- viability maintenance and internal condition sensing;
- sensitivity to environmental opportunities and constraints;
- coordinated movement, conservation, mobilisation and action readiness;
- affective and motivational organisation;
- autonomic, endocrine, immune and metabolic participation;
- caregiving, attachment, play, cooperation and social coordination;
- prelinguistic communication through multiple embodied channels;
- learning, memory, prediction, planning and flexible action;
- language, symbolic representation and cumulative culture;
- updating, recovery, restoration and renewed availability.

F1 does not own how this repertoire is calibrated during one person's development, how a particular present event is organised, why an organisation becomes chronic, or what a person does to someone else.

## Two timescales, one explanation

F1 must keep two clocks visible.

### Deep time: capacities became available

Different capacities have different and overlapping evolutionary histories. Later capacities did not simply replace earlier ones. They joined, modified and depended upon already living forms of sensing, coordination and action.

### Present time: capacities organise together

The Fluid Gradient describes how a living person may organise across a present event. X, A, A↔B, B, C, D and Z are **Positions within a current whole-organism model**. They are not geological periods, species levels, brain layers or steps from primitive to advanced.

This gives F1 its central distinction:

```text
EVOLUTIONARY CAPACITY FIELD

viability sensing · bodily coordination · action · care · communication
learning · prediction · symbolic extension · updating · recovery

                    many-to-many participation
                               ↓

PRESENT FLUID ORGANISATION

              X · A · A↔B · B · C · D · Z
```

No one evolutionary capacity belongs to only one Position. No Position is made from only one evolutionary layer.

## A rough evolutionary capacity timeline

This is a provisional orientation map, not a dated ladder. Its purpose is to help us ask what each broad expansion made more manageable. The capacities overlap, recur across lineages and remain open to comparative review.

| Broad expansion in the living repertoire | Conditions or problems it helped make more manageable | Relevance to the present Fluid Gradient | Boundary for the claim |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Viability maintenance and internal sensitivity** | Detecting and responding to changes relevant to temperature, energy, hydration, tissue integrity and other conditions of continued living | Grounds the Gradient in a living organism whose available resources and internal conditions change | X is not identical to homeostasis, and present Gradient Positions are not cellular or evolutionary stages |
| **Coordinated sensation, movement and orientation** | Approaching, avoiding, pausing, feeding, seeking shelter and selecting among changing environmental possibilities | Contributes to perception, action readiness and the ability to alter contact with conditions | Present behaviour cannot be reduced to one ancient reflex or purpose |
| **Conservation, mobilisation and wider bodily coordination** | Adjusting energy use, cardiovascular activity, respiration, endocrine participation and action under differing demands | Helps make different configurations of availability and protection biologically possible | Conservation, mobilisation and immobilisation should not be arranged into an unsupported fixed phylogenetic sequence |
| **Care, affiliation and social coordination** | Protecting dependent young, obtaining help, coordinating activity, learning from others, playing and cooperating | Makes relationship materially capable of changing conditions, resources, information and regulatory demand | Care is not exclusive to mammals; connection is not automatically safe, reciprocal or regulating |
| **Learning, memory, prediction and flexible action** | Using prior outcomes to discriminate conditions, anticipate change, revise action and prepare across longer horizons | Supports present-responsive comparison rather than response to the immediate stimulus alone | Cognition did not begin with human language and does not belong only to C or D |
| **Prelinguistic social-affective communication** | Coordinating attention, distance, care, alarm, invitation, refusal and shared activity before or without symbolic speech | Continues to shape how bodies, faces, voices, gaze, touch, rhythm and movement become socially informative | Humans did not communicate through emotion alone; communication was and remains multi-channel |
| **Language, symbolic modelling and cumulative culture** | Referring to absent events, sharing explanations, transmitting knowledge, building plans and norms, and coordinating beyond immediate perception | Expands reflective-symbolic participation and the ways present meaning can alter organisation | Language did not create cognition or replace emotional–somatic information |
| **Expanded flexibility, updating and recovery** | Changing course when conditions change, ending costly mobilisation, restoring resources and making other actions available again | Helps explain why Fluid organisation depends on responsiveness, reversibility and renewed availability | Return is not one universal pathway, and restoration is not exclusive to X |

The sequence is deliberately rough. It identifies a research programme: each row needs comparative evidence, lineage-specific qualification and careful distinction among homology, convergence and analogy. F1 should not use an approximate ordering as proof that any present Position has one age or one biological purpose.

## Before symbolic language

F1 needs to reach back before humans could explain their experience in words. But the precise proposition is not that humans once communicated **only through emotions**.

Before symbolic language—and still today—social-affective communication could involve:

- vocalisation and changes in tone or rhythm;
- facial and bodily expression;
- gaze, gesture and orientation;
- touch, proximity and withdrawal;
- coordinated movement and timing;
- attention to what another individual was attending to or doing.

These channels could organise approach, avoidance, care, alarm, play, conflict, reassurance and collective action. They made internal and relational conditions socially consequential without requiring a spoken explanation.

Not every feeling is primarily communication to another person. Feeling cold can make a changing thermal condition available to the organism and organise movement towards shelter, warmth or a change in activity. Hunger can make energy need behaviourally relevant and organise attention and action towards food. In the same broad sense, emotional events can make a situation, relationship, loss, opportunity or boundary matter to the organism and alter what becomes perceptually, physiologically and behaviourally available.

Language later expanded what humans could do with that information. People could name experience, compare it with the past, imagine absent and future conditions, negotiate meaning, transmit explanations, coordinate complex plans and question an initial interpretation. Language did not replace the embodied channels. It became another way the organism could participate in understanding and action.

## Overlapping survival-relevant problem-spaces

Two recurring problem-spaces help organise F1, provided that we do not mistake them for a complete biological taxonomy.

### Organism protection

The organism may need to preserve tissue integrity, energy, mobility, access to resources and the possibility of continued action. Seeking shelter when cold, obtaining food when hungry, withdrawing from immediate danger and mobilising against obstruction can all participate in organism protection.

### Connection preservation

For social organisms, access to care, belonging, cooperation, information and coordinated action can also affect survival and development. When a valued relationship appears uncertain or at risk, attention and action may reorganise around preserving or restoring that connection.

These problem-spaces overlap:

- connection can contribute directly to organism protection;
- organism protection may depend on obtaining help from others;
- caregiving can organise one organism to protect another;
- preserving connection can sometimes conflict with immediate self-protection;
- a relationship can be valued or expected while also being unsafe;
- play, exploration, reproduction, opportunity and collective coordination do not fit completely inside either category.

### Where the condition must change

The two problem-spaces can also differ in where the condition required for
updating exists.

- **Organism-protection is not inherently relational.** The relevant condition
  may change through the organism's own action, a change in the environment or
  practical help from another person. Shelter can reduce exposure; food can
  change energy availability; distance or an effective boundary can change
  contact with danger. Another person may be essential in a particular event,
  but reciprocity is not built into the protective problem itself.
- **Connection-preservation can be relationally contingent.** When the
  unresolved condition is another person's availability, recognition,
  reciprocity, accountability, trust or willingness to repair, one organism
  cannot produce mutual restoration alone. The other person's response is new
  information and may also be part of the condition that needs to change.
- **Dependent offspring can couple the two problems.** For a young child,
  access to a caregiver may also be access to warmth, nourishment, protection,
  orientation and many of the conditions within which physiological recovery
  becomes possible. Preserving connection can therefore participate directly
  in organism-protection.

This distinction is a **TEG-Blue synthesis**, not a claim that emotions divide
into two fixed natural kinds. The same sadness, fear, anger or longing may
participate in connection-preservation, organism-protection or both, depending
on what is at stake and where change is possible.

It also requires three outcomes to remain separate:

1. **Relational restoration:** mutual recognition, reciprocity, accountability,
   trust or repair becomes available.
2. **Organism-level updating and Return:** the relevant demand changes enough
   for organisation to release and wider participation or restoration to become
   available.
3. **Return without relational restoration:** the relationship remains
   unavailable or unrepaired, but grief, an effective boundary, revised
   expectation or reorganisation allows the organism to update to that reality.

Another nervous system may be necessary for the first outcome. It is not
therefore necessary for every form of regulation, updating or Return.

F1 should therefore distinguish **connection being available** from **connection-preservation becoming necessary because access appears uncertain or at risk**. Position A can include relational availability, play, exploration and cooperation; it should not be reduced to the defence of attachment.

## How the evolutionary repertoire participates in the Fluid Gradient

The Gradient is a present-event organisation of inherited, developed and currently available capacities. The following is a functional orientation, not a one-capacity-per-Position map.

| Fluid Position | Present organisational contribution | Evolutionary boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **X · Available capacity and restoration** | Internal resources, maintenance and restorative processes support wider availability | X is not the oldest biological layer, a passive absence of response or the whole of homeostasis |
| **A · Available relationship and engagement** | Contact, play, exploration, care, cooperation and flexible participation can remain available | A is not a mammalian invention, and it does not require another nervous system to be physically present in every event |
| **A↔B · Orientation under relational or situational uncertainty** | Attention and action can organise around clarifying whether safety, belonging, access or a valued condition may be lost | It is not a discrete evolutionary transition or a single attachment mechanism |
| **B · Active immediate protection** | Rapid mobilisation can alter distance, contact, obstruction or exposure | B is not simply a reptilian or sympathetic stage and does not dictate aggression |
| **C · Sustained anticipatory management** | Prediction, sequencing, vigilance and longer-horizon action can organise around continuing demand | Cognition, planning and social interpretation also participate elsewhere in the Gradient |
| **D · Maximum survival action** | Extraordinary mobilisation or narrowed action can occur when ordinary protection appears insufficient | D is not the most evolved, most cognitive or morally strongest Position |
| **Z · Conservation under constrained action** | Activity and expenditure may narrow when active mobilisation is unavailable, unsuccessful or too costly | Z is not a primitive remainder, a single dorsal pathway or one psychological meaning |

Each Position is an integrated present configuration. Multiple neural, autonomic, endocrine, immune, metabolic, perceptual, cognitive, motor and relational processes may participate. The labels make differences in organisation discussable; they do not name seven separate biological systems.

## Emotion within F1

Emotion is one domain through which consequential conditions can become biologically relevant. A feeling can change attention, priority, bodily preparation, interpretation, relationship and action without functioning as a complete explanation or command.

F1 carries four principles:

1. **Emotion is biological information, not an instruction.** A feeling may indicate that something matters without proving the cause, establishing the complete meaning or authorising one response.
2. **Emotional events involve the whole organism.** Neural, autonomic, endocrine, immune, metabolic, motor, perceptual, cognitive and relational processes may participate. No one chemical, organ or pathway is the emotion.
3. **Body Signals and Situation Signals identify the condition being registered, not two sealed biological systems.** Cold and hunger have different initiating conditions from fear, grief or relational uncertainty, but all are embodied, contextual and open to learning and conscious participation.
4. **Fluid does not mean unlearned, calm or biologically pure.** It describes a present-responsive comparison in which information, action and new evidence can still alter organisation.

The detailed account belongs in [Emotions and Feelings · The Fluid Gradient](../../01-signal-map/emotion/fluid.html). That page can explain named emotions, signal families, proposed functions, felt experience, conditions and updating. F1 explains why such capacities can participate at all. It should not assign every emotion one evolved purpose, one Gradient Position or one completion route.

## ESS, CLS and ME

F1 must not become an old-emotional-brain/new-rational-brain story.

- **ESS and CLS are equally important, biological, concurrent and recurrent forms of information processing.**
- Emotional–somatic capacities have deep evolutionary histories; so do learning, memory, discrimination, prediction, planning and social inference.
- Human language and reflective-symbolic modelling expanded particular possibilities, but cognition did not arrive as a detached system built to rule an irrational body.
- ESS and CLS are TEG-Blue educational groupings, not two sealed anatomical systems and not two evolutionary eras.
- ME is the present lived participant who may gain differing access to information and action. ME should not be projected backwards as an evolutionary stage or placed above the organism as an executive controller.

What matters in the model is what information becomes available to ME, how it is interpreted, what other information can participate, and what the person does with it.

## Fluidity, updating and Return

The evolutionary value of a repertoire is not only that it can mobilise. It is also that organisation can remain sensitive to changing evidence.

In a Fluid comparison:

```text
conditions become detectable
        · significance and priority can change
        · organism organisation and action availability can change
        · outcomes can be sampled
        · weighting and expectation can update
        · costly demand can end
        · resources and wider participation can become available again
```

F1 can investigate why flexibility, reversibility, recovery and restoration would matter to organisms with finite resources. It should not assume that every action reaches completion, that recovery follows one sequence, or that Return always requires relationship. Exact pathways belong to the Gradient's ESC, Return, autonomic, neurochemical and emotional-information accounts.

## Reading the original F1 language against the current system

### Preserve, with precise translation

| Original direction | Current reading |
| --- | --- |
| Life orients towards what sustains it and away from harm | Living systems regulate viability and respond to consequential gradients; “safety–threat orientation” is an accessible TEG-Blue translation, not the literal question every organism asks |
| Nervous systems inherited rather than invented the viability problem | Nervous systems expanded the coordination, learning and action possibilities available to living organisms |
| Protection and connection both matter | Organism protection and connection preservation are overlapping, survival-relevant problem-spaces; they are not an exhaustive taxonomy |
| What is inherited is architecture rather than calibration | Keep the handoff: inherited capacities become part of the input to F2's developmental question |
| Sustained demand can carry cost | Route present-demand and cumulative-cost claims through allostasis, recovery and Chronic evidence rather than treating cost as one inherited state |

### Narrow, revise or retire

| Map wording or implication | Why it needs care | Safer direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One evolutionary origin for each Position | Treats a present modelling column as though it were a trait or species transition | Map capacities many-to-many into present organisation |
| “Conservation and shutdown first, mobilisation later” | Can sound like a settled linear autonomic phylogeny and import contested Polyvagal claims | Treat conservation, immobilisation and mobilisation as widespread protective possibilities; keep precise ordering as a comparative question |
| Mammals introduced the system that makes safety possible through relationship | Care and social regulation exist beyond mammals, and relationship is not the only condition supporting regulation | Mammalian dependence intensified some forms of care and affective coordination; social care also evolved in other lineages |
| An infant “borrows a regulated adult” | Collapses caregiving, social buffering, reciprocal coordination, active help and physiological change | Retain only as public shorthand beside a precise account of what another person's presence and action can change |
| “Cognition, arriving last” | Learning, prediction and flexible planning have non-human and prelinguistic precursors | Human language expanded symbolic comparison and long-horizon coordination while embodied cognition continued |
| Humans communicated through emotion alone before language | Excludes gesture, gaze, vocalisation, touch, rhythm, movement and shared attention | Describe prelinguistic communication as embodied, social-affective and multi-channel |
| Old hardware in new conditions | Too general to explain a present difficulty | Name the precise mismatch, present conditions, learned expectation and affected capacity claim by claim |
| The body protects connection | Can obscure harmful or materially coercive relationships | Distinguish connection availability, preservation under possible loss, expected support, actual safety, power and available action |

## F1's handoff

F1 ends with an available, layered repertoire. F2 begins with how repeated experience, invalidation, relationship and material conditions shape access, expectation, expression and action within that repertoire.

```text
evolutionary availability and retained capacities
        ↓
developmental shaping and learned weighting
        ↓
present information and participation
        ↓
present whole-organism organisation
        ↓
updating, recovery or possible persistence
```

F1 is foundational because it supplies the deep-time conditions of possibility. It does not explain every later outcome.

## Grounding science and knowledge fields

This map identifies the fields F1 needs. It does not mean that a finding in any one field validates the full evolutionary sequence or the TEG-Blue systems built from it.

| Field | What it can ground in F1 | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Evolutionary biology and phylogenetics** | Common ancestry, divergence, conservation and the historical distribution of traits | The purpose of a present human feeling or a simple ladder from old to new |
| **Evolutionary developmental biology** | How developmental processes and evolutionary change interact across lineages | A direct route from species history to one person's developmental pattern |
| **Comparative physiology and neuroendocrinology** | Conserved and divergent ways organisms coordinate energy, stress, reproduction, immunity and action | One emotion–chemical fingerprint or one mechanism for a Gradient Position |
| **Comparative neuroscience and neuroanatomy** | Similarities and differences in neural organisation across species | A clean old-brain/new-brain division or the age of ESS and CLS as systems |
| **Ethology and animal behaviour** | Behaviour under natural and controlled conditions across levels of biological organisation | Human meaning, morality or culture inferred directly from another species |
| **Behavioural ecology** | How behaviour varies with ecological conditions, trade-offs and reproductive or survival pressures | That a present behaviour is optimal, fixed or caused by one adaptation |
| **Comparative and evolutionary psychology** | Cross-species questions about learning, memory, social cognition, affect and action | A universal evolutionary story for each named emotion or social behaviour |
| **Comparative communication, language evolution and psycholinguistics** | Evidence about vocal, gestural, affective and symbolic communication and the capacities supporting language | Direct access to the subjective meaning of ancestral communication or proof of one boundary between prelanguage and language |
| **Affective science and philosophy of emotion** | Distinctions among affect, appraisal, feeling, motivation, action tendency, context and conscious report | One settled definition of emotion, one universal function for each feeling or validation of the Fluid families as a natural taxonomy |
| **Systems biology, theoretical biology and ecological psychology** | Organism–environment relations, distributed coordination, viability, affordances and multi-level explanation | A direct mapping from a general principle to one named Gradient Position |
| **Biological anthropology and palaeoanthropology** | Human biological variation, life history and possible changes across hominin evolution | Direct access to the subjective experience or social organisation of past populations |
| **Archaeology and deep-history research** | Material evidence about past environments, technologies, care, subsistence and social practice | A nervous-system state, emotion or psychological motive read directly from an artefact |
| **Genetics, genomics and population biology** | Heritable variation, population history and gene–environment relations | A gene for an emotion, culture, personality, relational pattern or Gradient Position |
| **Cultural evolution and gene–culture coevolution research** | How socially learned practices and biological processes may influence one another over time | A single universal culture sequence or biological validation of a modern norm |
| **History and philosophy of biology** | Clarification of adaptation, function, homology, analogy, levels of selection and inference limits | Empirical support for a biological claim without corresponding evidence |

Forensic psychology is not a core grounding field for F1. It becomes relevant later only where evolutionary claims are applied to legal or administrative questions, and even there it cannot convert an evolutionary possibility into an opinion about one person's motive or responsibility.

### Priority evidence

F1 should favour comparative datasets, phylogenetic methods, field and laboratory animal-behaviour research, cross-species physiology, developmental and communication studies, fossil and archaeological evidence, and reviews that distinguish homology from analogy. Human social interpretation should remain connected to anthropology and history rather than being derived from biology alone.
