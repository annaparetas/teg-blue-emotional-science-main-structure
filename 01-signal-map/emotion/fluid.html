What a feeling reports
A feeling reports something: thirst reports that body water has dropped. It does not say where water is, and it says nothing about anything else. It is bounded information: what it means still depends on bodily state, context, interpretation and time. Reading a feeling this way — as information rather than as a problem — is the starting point for everything on this page.
A feeling is not a chemical
A feeling is a coordinated event across nerves, autonomic activity, hormones, immune signalling, metabolism, muscle and breath, shaped by learning and by what is happening now. No single substance is the feeling, and no measurement of one reports it.

Where feelings arise, and who reads them
Emotional signals form through fast, embodied processing that works largely outside conscious awareness. Whether a signal becomes differentiated feeling — and what it is then made to mean — involves all three of these. ESS · Emotional Somatic SystemRegisters bodily, relational and environmental significance and generates the signal. CLS · Cognitive Logical SystemInterprets the signal — compares, explains and plans across time. ME · conscious participantCan notice the feeling, test the interpretation, choose what happens next.

Four questions for every feeling

Every feeling on this page is read through the same four questions. The reading starts with the body, because that is where it is easiest to check.

Function
What does the signal do? What does it interrupt, drive, protect, slow, or make available?
Body
Where does it become perceptible? How the feeling characteristically shows up in breath, chest, stomach, face, muscle.
Conditions
What does it report? The internal or external condition the signal is organised to register.
Completion
What lets it update? The condition that allows the signal to complete, change, or be recognised as no longer recoverable.

Felt descriptions are characteristic, not mandatory. Intensity, context, learning, current state, access, and body variation change how a feeling is experienced. What a signal needs names the condition for updating or completion; it does not prescribe or authorise a behaviour.

What emotions are for

The families on this page are a working functional classification, informed by evolutionary and affective research: each gathers signals by what they help the organism register and do. Beneath every signal TEG-Blue reads two survival functions, organism-protection and connection-preservation. That pairing is an organising synthesis for comparison, not an exhaustive scientific taxonomy. An emotion may organise either function or both — the active problem depends on what is at stake in the event, not on the emotion's name.

Two groups of feelings

Every feeling on this page belongs to one of two groups, distinguished by where the signal begins.

Group 1 · Internal physiological conditions

Body Signals

What does the body itself need?

Where the signal begins
A regulated bodily condition changes: breathing chemistry, tissue condition, temperature, bladder or bowel filling, water or energy availability, or the need for sleep and recovery.
How “what it needs” becomes clear
The signal often points relatively directly towards correction: air, protection, cooling, warmth, emptying, water, nourishment, rest or sleep.
Survival problem
Organism-protection. The signal helps keep internal conditions within a workable range.
Working functional clusters · family status not settled
  • Respiratory · breathlessness
  • Tissue protection · pain
  • Thermoregulation · overheating, cold
  • Elimination · bladder, bowels
  • Resource regulation · thirst, hunger
  • Recovery · tiredness

Group 2 · Current and represented conditions

Situation Signals

What does the organism register about its relationship to conditions?

Where the signal begins
The ESS registers what relational, physical-environmental, action/goal, collective-systemic or represented conditions may mean for connection, safety, action and survival.
How “what it needs” becomes clear
The feeling alone is not enough. Its function shows what must become clear, change, stop, become available or be recognised as no longer recoverable.
Survival problem
Connection-preservation, organism-protection, or both. The active problem changes with what is at stake.
Established functional families · characteristic Fluid references shown separately
  • Belonging Emotions · value with viable participation · Fluid reference A
  • Belonging-at-Risk · valued conditions uncertain or disrupted · Fluid reference A↔B
  • Threat-Protection · a specific source of possible harm · Fluid reference B
  • Survival-Rage · ordinary protection registered as insufficient · Fluid reference D
Different starting points
Body Signals begin with internal physiological conditions. Situation Signals begin with what current or represented conditions mean for the organism.
Different routes to completion
Body Signal needs are often legible through correction. Situation Signal needs become legible through the signal’s function and the condition it concerns.
Where they meet
Both are embodied. Situation Signals change breath, temperature, pain, hunger and energy; Body Signals can reveal danger. Conscious interoception can therefore access bodily information from both groups.

The rows run by how long there is — air in minutes, heat and cold in hours, water in days, food in weeks. Pain sits second by severity rather than by clock, and is the one row this ordering does not cleanly hold.

Pain is not a damage meter. Damage without pain and pain without damage are both ordinary. “Tissue is being damaged” is what the signal is designed to report, not a guarantee of what is true.

Body Signal completion is not whole-organism Return. The final cell identifies the bodily condition that may allow the signal to update. Return concerns the wider Gradient organisation, and recovery can continue after urgency, pain or conscious sensation changes.

Situation signals

Situation signals register connection, progress, possibility, obstruction, loss, danger, or safety across relational and non-person conditions. Their four functional families group signals by the broad kind of condition relationship they may make available. The families are not stages and do not prescribe an emotional order. In one event, signals from more than one family may coexist or become foregrounded as different aspects of the situation become relevant. The condition domain separately identifies where that information comes from. Every emotion is read through its function, bodily experience, condition information, an example in context, a possible completing condition, and what may remain relevant when that condition remains unavailable.

“What the signal needs” is read differently in the two groups. A Body Signal often points relatively directly towards the bodily condition that needs restoring: water, food, breathable conditions, warmth, cooling, emptying, rest, or protection from tissue harm. A Situation Signal becomes legible through its function. Fear needs danger accurately located and safety or protection restored; shame needs accurate information about belonging and whether repair is possible; anger needs the crossing or obstruction understood and an effective boundary or route restored. The emotion does not simply need to stop. The relevant condition may need to change, become sufficiently clear, or be recognised as no longer recoverable. The final cell names that possible completing condition; it does not establish that completion, updating, Return, recovery, or repair occurred. Open the full completion–Return audit and preliminary neurochemistry map.

A completing condition is not automatically something another person owes. The route may involve environmental change, one’s own action, another person’s voluntary response, protection, new evidence, grief, or reorganisation around what cannot be restored. Compare four relational completion cases.

The final column describes possibilities, not a prediction. The original signal may remain present or influential while another signal becomes relevant or more prominent. Several signals may coexist. What becomes consciously accessible, how the organism is organised, and what unfolds through time are separate Model 1, Model 2 and Model 3 (ESC) questions. “Not reciprocated” applies to relational bids; continuing danger, obstruction or uncertainty are different forms of non-completion.

The order of the families is a reading aid, not a pathway. The family identifies what a signal concerns. The Fluid Gradient separately describes how the organism is organised while one or more signals are being processed. No family sequence, emotion, or non-completion entry establishes a person’s Position.

Characteristic association is not exclusive membership. The same emotion may occur across different Positions, one Position may contain signals from more than one family, and no individual emotion establishes a Position.

Emotion family and condition domain answer different questions. Fear remains a threat signal whether its condition is an attacker, unstable ground, or an anticipated event. Pride remains an achievement signal whether it is privately registered or socially recognised.

The survival problem is separate from both. An emotion may organise connection-preservation, organism-protection, or both. These are not mutually exclusive emotion buckets: the active problem depends on what is at stake in the event. Belonging-at-Risk is the clearest overlap territory because threatened connection can simultaneously endanger access to others and recruit protection of the organism.

Belonging Emotions

Functional centre · value with viable participation

Belonging signals register that something valued — a person, relationship, condition, achievement, contribution, shared identity, or possibility — is sufficiently available or significant for meaningful participation, whether personal, relational, or collective. In the current Fluid reference, they may be especially readable and usable around Position A, where connection and wider participation remain available. This is a characteristic association, not the source or definition of the family. The established family holds two internal working clusters. Love, trust, gratitude, compassion, and admiration form a relational-value cluster: they make another person's value, reliability, welfare, or significance available to participation, and their completion can genuinely involve reception, recognition, reciprocity, or useful care. Happiness, joy, pride, and hope form a favourable-participation cluster: they make a favourable condition, achievement, contribution, shared identity, or possibility available for participation, continuation, or investment. Another person is not required for the emotion's identity. The condition may be personal, relational, or collective, and event-specific completion may sometimes require another person's freely offered participation. Both clusters remain within one family: something has value and meaningful participation remains available or possible.

Relational participation is not one switch. Another person's participation may form part of the condition being registered; it may be needed for event-specific completion where mutual recognition, reciprocity, shared celebration, group membership, or coordinated action is genuinely involved; or it may deepen, sustain, or extend a feeling without creating it. A private occurrence can remain genuine, and an emotion never prescribes another person's participation.

Pride Registers meaningful effort, growth, contribution, integrity, or membership in something valued, and supports stable self-value. Someone finishes difficult work and shares the achievement with a person whose recognition matters to the event. The achievement, contribution, or shared value becomes consciously registered. Event-specific completion includes recognition or belonging only where either genuinely formed part of the condition. The achievement or contribution can remain real without recognition. Where recognition or belonging formed part of the condition, pride may remain unshared or difficult to use while disappointment, shame, anger, social comparison, or envy also becomes relevant. Pride does not turn into another emotion; each experience needs its own condition read.
Hope Keeps the organism oriented toward possibility when something meaningful still feels reachable. The chest lifts, breath opens, and energy leans forward toward the future. A valued future outcome remains possible. A treatment, application, or conversation offers a credible route towards something that matters. Possibility remains credible enough to guide participation, or expectation updates if the route changes or closes. When the outcome depends on other people, shared commitment or coordinated participation may be part of keeping the route viable. If the route closes, disappointment, sadness, or grief may help expectation change. If the route depends on others and their participation does not arrive, loneliness or disappointment may also become relevant, and whether the possibility remains jointly viable may need revision.

Belonging-at-Risk

Survival problem · connection-preservation ↔ organism-protection

These signals concern safety, belonging, support, standing, or another valued condition becoming uncertain, disrupted, obstructed, lost, or unavailable. In the current Fluid reference, they may be characteristically foregrounded around Position A↔B, where organisation checks what changed and whether safety or belonging remain available. This association does not define the family or make any one signal proof of A↔B. They often sit across both survival problems. The same sadness, anxiety, frustration, shame, or stress can track threatened belonging, danger to the organism, or both at once. The emotion name alone does not settle which problem is active; the conditions and what the signal is trying to preserve do.

Heat rises through the face and neck as the chest drops, the belly contracts, and the gaze turns down. Belonging, standing, or the acceptable self may be at risk. Guilt Signals that one’s action may have caused harm, and supports accountability, learning, and repair. Heaviness or tension sits in the chest and stomach, with a pull back toward the impact. One’s action may have caused harm or carried a cost for someone else. A person forgets an agreement and learns that the omission created a real cost for somebody else. Impact is established, responsibility is proportionate, and protective change or repair is attempted where possible. Felt guilt is not proof of harm. Uncertainty or guilt may persist when impact cannot be clarified or repair is refused. Repair can remain open even if internal pressure falls; defensiveness is possible but not inevitable.

Needed connection, witness, or belonging is missing. Someone repeatedly reaches out but does not feel known, received, or included. Disappointment Registers that an expected bond, outcome, support, or possibility did not arrive. The chest or stomach drops, breath releases, and energy falls away from expectation. An expected bond, outcome, support, or possibility did not arrive. A promised visit, response, or opportunity does not happen. The mismatch is registered and the expectation, goal, or relationship read revises. Repeated expectation may continue to meet the same mismatch. Sadness, frustration, or resentment may also become relevant, while a revised route may become available.
Sadness Slows the organism around loss, absence, hurt, or unmet need, and invites care or shared grief. Heaviness gathers in the chest, throat, and eyes as movement and breath slow. A needed person, bond, support, or possibility is absent, lost, or was never available. A friend dismisses an expressed hurt, or an important possibility ends. The absence or loss is acknowledged, with enough time or support for response and expectation to reorganise. Sadness may remain or return while loneliness or grief also becomes relevant. Withdrawal may become available; support elsewhere cannot complete missing recognition or restore the lost condition.
Grief Helps the organism register deep loss and reorganise around what can no longer be restored. Pain and pressure fill the chest and throat, breath breaks or constricts, and the whole body becomes heavy. A significant person, bond, role, or part of life has been irreversibly lost. A person, relationship, role, or expected future is no longer recoverable in its previous form. Irreversibility becomes increasingly registerable, with enough time and support for life to reorganise around what cannot be restored. Grief may remain or return while the person continues meeting the loss or support remains limited. Continuing pain, love, or renewed grief does not by itself show that the loss has not been understood.
Confusion Signals that meaning, expectation, or coherence has broken down and needs reorientation. Tension gathers around the forehead and eyes while the body pauses and the stomach may unsettle. Available information conflicts with the current understanding of the situation. Two credible accounts conflict, or another person’s words and behaviour do not fit together. Enough reliable information or orientation distinguishes what is known, unknown, and still ambiguous. False certainty is not required. Confusion may remain prominent while scanning or pausing continues. Anxiety or suspicion may also become relevant; the person may keep uncertainty open rather than adopt a false explanation.
Frustration Signals blocked movement toward something that matters and mobilises renewed effort. Pressure and heat build through the jaw, shoulders, hands, or chest against the obstacle. An obstacle is blocking progress toward a valued outcome. A tool repeatedly fails, a process stalls, or another person’s action blocks the next step. The obstacle changes, another route becomes usable, the goal revises, or impossibility becomes sufficiently clear. Frustration may remain while anger or exhaustion also becomes relevant. Renewed effort, withdrawal, or goal revision may become available; none is required by the signal.
Anxiety Anticipates possible rupture, loss, exposure, or instability before it is fully clear. The chest tightens, breath rises, the stomach unsettles, and tingling or restlessness spreads. Rupture, loss, exposure, or instability may be approaching. An important message goes unanswered and several outcomes remain possible. Evidence, protection, or increased capacity makes uncertainty more discriminable and allows prediction to update; reassurance alone may only reduce pressure. Anxiety may remain prominent while scanning, reassurance-seeking, or avoidance becomes available. Fear or sadness may also become relevant as conditions become clearer; anxiety does not turn into them.
Stress Signals that demand is exceeding available capacity and the system needs support, reduction, or reorganisation. The jaw, neck, and shoulders brace as breath quickens and pressure spreads through the body. Current demands are pressing against the organism’s available capacity. Work, caregiving, or material demand exceeds the time, support, or energy currently available. Demand reduces, capacity or support increases, priorities reorganise, or the continuing mismatch becomes accurately known. The demand–capacity mismatch may remain relevant. Stress may remain prominent while frustration, fear, exhaustion, or reduced participation also becomes relevant; quietness alone does not show that the mismatch changed.

Relational completion: four different cases

Relational signals do not all need the same outcome. The examples below distinguish a response from the person involved, support from somebody else, and updating around a relationship that cannot be restored.

Relational case Signals that may participate Example What the signal needsPossible condition for relational completion or updating If relational completion does not occurPossible persistence, change or carryover — not an inevitable outcome
A favourable signal reaches the relevant person Love, trust, gratitude, compassion, admiration; sometimes joy or pride when sharing is part of the event. Someone expresses love or gratitude and the other person receives it, recognises its meaning and responds in a way that fits the relationship. The signal becomes expressible and sufficiently received or mutually recognised to inform care, reciprocity and participation. A particular moment can complete without the bond or feeling ending. The value of the feeling may remain internally known, while this relational bid remains unanswered. Depending on the relationship and pattern, disappointment, loneliness, shame, uncertainty or protective distance may become relevant.
A rupture remains open to repair Shame, guilt, sadness, disappointment, anxiety and anger, depending on what happened and whose signal is being followed. After a broken promise causes hurt, the person responsible listens, recognises the impact, takes proportionate responsibility and changes the behaviour. Accurate information about impact and responsibility, followed by acknowledgement, accountability and protective change. Relational repair also requires the affected person’s voluntary participation; trust is a later outcome, not proof supplied by an apology. The rupture can remain open even if either person calms down. Shame, guilt, sadness, disappointment, anxiety or anger may remain relevant, and other protective signals may join them. A boundary, withdrawal, grief or a revised relationship read may become available; self-regulation cannot supply the missing acknowledgement or changed conduct.
Another person helps carry an unmet relational signal Loneliness, sadness, shame, disappointment, anxiety or grief after non-response, rejection or rupture. A friend listens and helps someone make sense of being rejected by a partner who will not discuss what happened. The available person can offer witness, care, reality-checking and regulatory support. This may help the signal become tolerable and usable, but it does not provide the original partner’s recognition, reciprocity or repair. The original relational condition remains unresolved. Support elsewhere may still enable Return, clearer choice, grief and integration; it should not be described as completing a repair that the relevant person did not participate in.
The original relationship cannot be restored Grief, sadness, loneliness, disappointment, love and sometimes anger. A person has died, a relationship has ended permanently, or repeated refusal makes mutual restoration unavailable. Relational restoration in its original form is unavailable. Updating may instead require the irreversibility to become increasingly registerable, with enough time, support, grief and revised expectation for life to reorganise around the loss. The signal may persist or return while the loss is being integrated. Other relationships can support recovery and renewed belonging, but they do not erase the bond, replace the person or retroactively complete the unavailable relationship.

Completion and carryover can coexist. One part of an event may update while another remains open. A person may understand what happened and regain wider organisational flexibility while recognition, repair, trust or grief still carries into later conditions.

Threat-Protection

Survival problem · organism-protection

These signals concern a specific danger, violation, contaminating condition, or possible source of harm. In the current Fluid reference, they may be characteristically foregrounded around Position B, where protection receives greater priority. This association does not define the family or establish the person's current Position. Their function is to locate possible danger and recruit protection.

Emotion Emotion function How it feels in the body What it signals about conditions Example in contextOne possible event, not a definition What the signal needsPossible condition for updating or completion If the relevant completing condition does not occurPossible signal relationships while the condition remains unresolved — not a prediction
Fear Alerts the organism to danger, increases caution, and prepares protection, escape, or freezing. The heart and breath accelerate, the stomach drops, and cold, tingling, or trembling may spread. The organism detects a possible source of harm in the present environment. A car swerves into one’s path, or another person’s behaviour creates an immediate possibility of harm. Danger is sufficiently located; threat ends, protection succeeds, credible safety evidence arrives, or unresolved uncertainty remains accurately represented. Fear may remain prominent while escape, resistance, or stillness becomes available. If danger changes or no route is available, other protective or survival signals may also become relevant; fear does not establish movement to one Position.
Anger Brings boundary energy when something feels invasive, unfair, harmful, blocked, or violated. Heat and pressure build through the chest, face, jaw, arms, or hands as muscles brace. A boundary, value, need, or route is being crossed or blocked. A stated boundary is ignored and the unwanted action continues. The crossing stops, a boundary becomes effective, agency or route is restored, or impossibility is recognised and participation changes. Discharge is not required. Anger may remain prominent while frustration or fear also becomes relevant. Firmer boundaries, distance, withdrawal, or other protection may become available; none is prescribed, and calming alone does not restore agency.
Disgust Protects the organism from what feels contaminating, unsafe, degrading, or too close. The stomach and throat contract, the face recoils, and the body pulls away. A substance, person, act, or proximity may be contaminating, degrading, or unsafe. Food appears spoiled, or an unwanted act or proximity feels contaminating or degrading. Separation becomes possible, or credible evidence revises the contamination or violation read. Disgust may remain prominent while recoil, nausea, avoidance, or vigilance continues. Anger may also become relevant while separation is unavailable. What happened, and whether coercion or harm occurred, still requires separate evidence.
Suspicion Increases scanning when trust feels uncertain or hidden threat may be present. The eyes and face sharpen, breath holds, and tension gathers in the stomach or chest. An appearance, account, or stated intention may not match the underlying reality. Explanations repeatedly change while observable behaviour does not fit what is being said. Evidence becomes sufficient to confirm, revise, or maintain concern proportionately. Suspicion is not evidence by itself. Suspicion may remain prominent while scanning, provisional trust, checking, or protective boundaries become available. Anxiety, fear, or anger may also become relevant; repetition does not turn concern into proof.

Survival-Rage

Survival problem · organism-protection

These signals concern maximum-survival mobilisation when ordinary protection is registered as insufficient, blocked, or unavailable. In the current Fluid reference, they may be characteristically foregrounded around Position D, where maximum force and movement receive priority. Neither the emotion nor the association proves objective life threat or authorises force. These signals can recruit maximum available force, movement, and survival action until danger ends or a viable route becomes available.

Rage Recruits everything available when survival is at stake and ordinary protection is not enough. Heat and force surge through the chest, arms, and hands as breath, muscles, and attention intensify. Survival is at stake and ordinary defence is not enough. A person is cornered during a violent threat and ordinary defence or escape has failed. Danger stops, escape or effective protection becomes available, and maximum force is no longer required. Substantial recovery may remain. The danger may remain unresolved while rage stays prominent or other survival signals also become relevant. Pain, depletion, or vigilance may coexist; neither the emotion nor a later reduction in movement establishes whether protection completed.
Fury Holds maximum force on a perceived source of danger until the danger stops. The gaze narrows while the jaw, neck, chest, and limbs hold sustained force. The danger continues and has a perceived source. A source of danger remains present and repeatedly defeats ordinary protective action. The danger ends or another viable route makes sustained maximum force unnecessary; recovery can then begin. The perceived source of danger may remain central while fury stays prominent or fear, rage, or exhaustion also becomes relevant. Escape, assistance, or changed conditions may remain possible completing routes.
Frenzy Drives unplanned movement when maximum survival force has no viable route. Activation floods the whole body as breath and movement become rapid, forceful, and uncoordinated. Survival demand exceeds ordinary response capacity, with no viable action route available. A person faces immediate danger but cannot locate an exit or organise an effective response. A viable route, assistance, or changed conditions allow coordination to re-enter and danger to end. Reduced movement from depletion is not completion. Survival demand may remain unresolved while no viable route is found. Frenzy may stay prominent or coexist with fear, rage, or exhaustion; reduced movement alone does not show that protection completed.

None of these feelings is a chemical. A feeling is a coordinated event across nerves, autonomic activity, hormones, immune signalling, metabolism, muscle and breath, shaped by learning and by what is happening now. No single substance is the feeling, and no measurement of one reports it.

How changing conditions can change the emotional read

A Situation Signal makes a relevant condition available to the organism. Which signals remain relevant or become foregrounded may change when the condition changes, becomes clearer, or is recognised as unavailable or irreversible. If the condition remains open, the original signal may remain present or influential while another signal also becomes relevant. This does not create a family sequence: uncertain belonging, possible harm, and life threat are different condition readings, not mandatory stages. If restoration is no longer possible, grief or other signals may help make loss and reorganisation relevant.

What a signal needs is not necessarily something another person must provide. Completion may depend on new information, recognition, reciprocity, a boundary, protective action, physiological recovery, or accepting and grieving what cannot be restored. The Signal Map names possible completing conditions; Model 3 (ESC) asks how response, feedback, completion, and updating unfold through the event.

A completing condition is only one part of the outcome

A completing condition belongs to this Signal Map. ESC then asks what actually changed through the event, and the Gradient asks whether wider organisation became available.

Condition · Signal Map
Did the relevant bodily, material, relational, or represented condition change?
Information · Model 1 and ESC
Did credible evidence alter what became accessible, prioritised, or understood?
Recruitment · Model 2
Could the previous protective or problem-solving task reduce or change course?
Return · Model 2
Did wider organisational flexibility and more restorative capacity become available?
Recovery · Model 2 and ESC
What spent resources or affected channels still need rebuilding?
Carryover · Model 3 (ESC)
What demand, weighting, expectation, depleted capacity, or cost enters the next cycle?

The answers can differ. A signal can update while recovery continues. Pressure can fall while the condition remains. Return can begin before recovery is complete. Repair may still be required after the internal recruitment changes.

Where the completing condition exists

The two survival problems differ not only in what is at stake, but in where change can occur. This is a TEG-Blue synthesis: it organises supported processes without proposing two fixed biological kinds of emotion.

Relational restoration
When the condition is mutual availability, recognition, reciprocity, accountability, trust or repair, one person cannot restore it alone. Another person's response is part of the condition that must change.
Organism-level updating and Return
When demand changes through effective action, environmental change, assistance, new evidence or physiological recovery, organisation may release and wider participation can become available.
Return without relational restoration
The relationship may remain unavailable or unrepaired while grief, an effective boundary, revised expectation or reorganisation allows the organism to update to that reality.

Pathways of completion

The condition domain changes what can complete the configuration. Completion may use more than one pathway, and only some pathways require another nervous system.

Environmental change
Danger ends, distance or shelter becomes available, a resource arrives, or the material obstruction changes.
One’s own action
Protection succeeds, a boundary becomes effective, a task completes, a solution works, or a goal is revised.
Relational response
Reception, recognition, reciprocity, clarification, co-regulatory support, accountability or repair changes a condition that genuinely exists between people.
Physiological recovery
Mobilisation releases and the body receives the rest, nourishment, treatment, sleep, or restoration still required.
Grief and reorganisation
What cannot be restored is registered, expectation changes, and life reorganises around the irreversible condition.

The pathway is determined by what the signal concerns. External praise cannot complete a purely physical escape route, and solitary calming cannot supply mutual recognition when the unresolved condition is genuinely relational. Another person may be required to restore a relationship without being required for every form of regulation or Return.
