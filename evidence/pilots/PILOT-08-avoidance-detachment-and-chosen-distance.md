# Pilot 08 · Avoidance, Detachment and Chosen Distance

Status: first claim-level evidence pass · PEND-044 cluster 4
Scope: behavioural withdrawal · social anxiety · avoidant personality functioning · attachment avoidance · safety behaviour · experiential avoidance · detachment · social reward · shutdown · solitude · boundary-setting
Purpose: decompose outward distance into distinguishable phenomena, functions and conditions so that TEG-Blue neither diagnoses withdrawal nor mistakes protection and chosen solitude for pathology

## Distance is the visible form, not its explanation

Two people may both decline an invitation, stop speaking or leave a relationship. One fears humiliation but wants connection. One expects little pleasure from contact. One is overloaded. One has learned to deactivate attachment need. One is conserving limited capacity. One is leaving danger. One simply prefers solitude. One has made a considered boundary.

The outward form does not identify the mechanism, history, diagnosis or wisdom of the action.

## Eleven constructs that must remain separate

| Construct | Working meaning | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Behavioural withdrawal** | reduced approach, participation or contact that can be observed | motive, fear, preference, incapacity or diagnosis |
| **Social anxiety** | fear or anxiety organised around possible scrutiny, embarrassment or negative evaluation | avoidant personality disorder, low desire for connection or inaccurate threat perception |
| **Avoidant personality functioning** | pervasive social inhibition, inadequacy and hypersensitivity to negative evaluation with broader personality and interpersonal impairment | every avoidance pattern or attachment avoidance |
| **Attachment avoidance / deactivation** | reducing attention to attachment need, distress or support-seeking when closeness is expected to be unavailable or costly | absence of physiological distress, lack of care or avoidant personality disorder |
| **Safety behaviour** | action used to prevent or limit a feared outcome while entering or remaining near a situation | deliberate boundary, actual safety action or inevitable maintenance of anxiety |
| **Experiential avoidance** | efforts to escape, suppress or control unwanted internal experience | external withdrawal, one diagnosis or proof that the experience should be approached |
| **Detachment** | low interpersonal engagement, expressivity or affiliative involvement | fear, hostility, incapacity or one clinical category |
| **Reduced social reward / social anhedonia** | reduced anticipation or experience of pleasure from social contact | chosen solitude, social anxiety or lack of moral concern |
| **Shutdown or capacity-limited withdrawal** | reduced participation associated with exhaustion, dissociation, severe stress, illness or unavailable processing capacity | stable preference or personality structure |
| **Chosen solitude** | time alone selected for its own value, regulation, concentration or restoration | loneliness, rejection, fear or impairment |
| **Protective boundary / safe disengagement** | reducing access or contact because engagement is unsafe, unwanted, costly or no longer consented to | fear-driven avoidance, punitive withdrawal or failure to repair |

## The function-and-condition test

Before naming withdrawal as avoidance, ask:

1. **Away from what?** Scrutiny, affect, uncertainty, conflict, sensory load, demand, danger or unwanted access?
2. **Towards or in service of what?** Safety, relief, rest, concentration, autonomy, punishment, control or valued solitude?
3. **What is expected?** Rejection, humiliation, engulfment, depletion, retaliation, no reward or nothing in particular?
4. **What is actually present?** A safe opportunity, an ambiguous cue, a known harmful person, coercive conditions or exhausted capacity?
5. **Can the response update?** Does distance change across people, contexts, evidence and recovery?
6. **What does it cost and whom does it constrain?** The person's own life, another person's legitimate autonomy, or an abusive person's access?

This prevents the framework from treating all approach as health and all distance as failure.

## Social anxiety is not one detached personality

Social anxiety commonly involves an approach–avoidance conflict: connection or participation matters, but anticipated evaluation makes it threatening. Avoidant personality functioning may include similar anxiety alongside more pervasive inadequacy, shame, inhibition and interpersonal difficulty. Research supports overlap, but not a clean rule that one is merely a more severe version of the other.

TEG-Blue should therefore describe the evidenced component—fear of negative evaluation, nonassertiveness, negative self-concept, interpersonal hypersensitivity, broad inhibition—before invoking a diagnostic research family.

## Safety behaviour is not the same as getting safe

A safety behaviour in anxiety research is performed to prevent a feared outcome, often while entering or enduring the situation. Avoiding eye contact, rehearsing every sentence or monitoring one's appearance may prevent a feared prediction from being fairly tested. Different subtypes have different interpersonal and learning effects.

This language must not be applied indiscriminately to actions that reduce actual danger. Blocking an abusive contact, leaving a coercive room or withholding vulnerable information from someone who exploits it are **protective actions** when the conditions support that reading. Their success is not evidence that the danger was imagined.

## Experiential avoidance is internal, not geographical

Experiential avoidance concerns the person's relationship to unwanted thoughts, feelings, sensations and memories. It can happen while a person remains physically present: compulsive distraction, emotional suppression or rigid control may reduce contact with internal experience. Conversely, a person may leave a situation while clearly feeling, representing and acting from what is happening.

The practical question is not “Did they stay?” It is “Could relevant experience become readable and usable, and did the action fit the condition?”

## Low social reward requires measurement humility

Anticipating little pleasure, remembering little pleasure, reporting little pleasure during interaction and participating less are not identical. Experience-sampling findings in psychosis research show that conclusions about social pleasure change with the item used. This does not generalise psychosis findings to configuration `100`; it warns TEG-Blue not to infer an absent capacity from low participation or a questionnaire label.

## Chosen solitude and relational boundaries

Solitude can regulate arousal, support concentration and protect autonomy. It becomes concerning through evidence of distress, unwanted restriction, rigidity, impairment or inability to approach valued contact—not merely through quantity of time alone.

A boundary may disappoint another person and remain valid. HPR should distinguish:

- removal of another person's presumed access;
- temporary space with clear communication;
- withdrawal for immediate safety;
- punitive silence intended to control another person's behaviour;
- abandonment of responsibilities that cannot ethically be discarded;
- fear-driven retreat from a valued and sufficiently safe opportunity.

The form “no contact” cannot settle which one occurred.

## F2 hypotheses—not conclusions

F2 may later test whether a recurrent pattern was shaped by:

- behavioural inhibition or sensitivity to social evaluation;
- rejection, ridicule, exclusion or chronic peer victimisation;
- caregiving that was unavailable, intrusive, unpredictable or punishing of need;
- learning that support-seeking increased danger or disappointment;
- environments in which emotional deactivation preserved functioning;
- sensory, neurodevelopmental, health or disability-related load;
- repeated overwhelm, dissociation, depression or depleted capacity;
- actual interpersonal danger that made distance protective;
- cultural meanings of privacy, sociability, duty and independence.

These are heterogeneous candidate routes. No present pattern proves a childhood history.

## F3 and HPR ownership

F3 should examine the current prediction, valued direction, self-explanation, relief cycle, identity and capacity to update. It asks whether distance preserves a life the person chooses or increasingly replaces it.

HPR should examine what happened before and after access was reduced, whether another person respected the boundary, whether withdrawal was used to punish or coerce, and whether practical safety required disengagement. A person does not owe renewed access merely because the other person requests “repair”.

## Terminology decisions

### Use “withdrawal” for the observed form

> **Behavioural withdrawal** is reduced approach, participation or contact. Its function and conditions remain to be established.

### Use “avoidance” only when the avoided event or experience is named

Prefer **social-evaluative avoidance**, **experiential avoidance**, **conflict avoidance**, **attachment deactivation** or **avoidance safety behaviour** when the evidence supports that component.

### Retain “detachment” as a research-family term, not a verdict

Detachment can route the reader towards trait and clinical literature. It must not translate ordinary introversion, rest, cultural reserve or a boundary into pathology.

### Use “chosen distance” as a framework umbrella with components

> **Chosen distance** is voluntarily reduced contact in service of safety, restoration, concentration, preference or a boundary. The choice must remain distinguishable from fear, coercion, exclusion and unavailable capacity.

## What this permits TEG-Blue to say

TEG-Blue can say that identical outward distance may arise from different combinations of threat prediction, reward expectation, attachment strategy, bodily capacity, contextual danger and deliberate choice. It can show when recurrent withdrawal has become rigid and costly without making sociability the standard of health.

It should not say that configuration `100` is an avoidant personality, that solitude means shutdown, that attachment avoidance means no emotion, that every safety behaviour is harmful, that leaving prevents integration or that reconciliation is required for repair.
