## The central distinction

People can experience contradiction, criticism, failure, rejection or loss of status as painful and destabilising. That experience does not tell us what they will do next.

Some people withdraw. Some become ashamed, angry or defensive. Some reconsider. Some ask for reassurance. Some attack the feedback source. Harm becomes a power question when preserving the self-position requires another person's silence, submission, discrediting, punishment or reduced agency.

The governing sequence is therefore:

```text
challenge to a valued self-position
        ↓
meaning assigned to the challenge
        ↓
available regulation and response routes
        ↓
possible entitlement to preserve position or retaliate
        ↓
available interpersonal, material or institutional power
        ↓
observable treatment of contradiction and the person supplying it
        ↓
impact, recurrence, accountability and repair
```

No arrow is inevitable. Self-threat may help explain pressure on behaviour. It does not authorise the behaviour or establish its mechanism after the fact.

## Seven constructs that must remain separate

| Construct | Working meaning | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Self-concept clarity** | how clearly, consistently and stably self-beliefs are represented | accuracy, self-worth, flexibility, morality or safety |
| **Contingent self-worth** | the domains or outcomes on which a person's sense of worth depends | fragility in every context, narcissism or coercion |
| **Self-position threat** | a perceived challenge to valued identity, status, competence, goodness, authority or entitlement | objective harm, inevitable dysregulation or retaliation |
| **Narcissistic antagonism** | an antagonistic personality dimension involving entitlement, exploitativeness, arrogance or low regard for others | NPD, one hidden vulnerability or violent conduct |
| **Psychological entitlement** | a generalised expectation of deserving more or receiving preferential treatment | legitimate claim, authority, abuse or actual enforcement |
| **Power** | capacity to shape conditions, consequences, resources, credibility or another person's options | corruption, coercion or harmful use |
| **Retaliation** | action that imposes cost in response to perceived offence, refusal, contradiction or accountability | one emotion, diagnosis, nervous-system state or motive |

## The difference between self-protection and position preservation

Self-protection can preserve bodily safety, dignity, privacy, a boundary or freedom from attack. Position preservation protects a particular conclusion about who the person is, what they deserve or whose account must prevail.

They can look similar from the inside. Both may involve urgency, anger or withdrawal. Their relational effects can be very different.

Position preservation becomes visible when:

- disagreement is treated as disloyalty or attack;
- loss of admiration becomes grounds for punishment;
- accountability becomes persecution;
- another person's boundary becomes an offence against status;
- explanation is used to cancel impact rather than examine it;
- the source of feedback is discredited, isolated or made afraid;
- compliance restores calm and reinforces the same route.

This is not inferred from emotion. It is observed through what happens to contradiction, evidence and another person's options.

## Narcissism: what the stronger evidence changes

The older threatened-egotism study remains useful, but it no longer needs to carry the argument alone. Two larger meta-analyses show a reliable average association between narcissism and aggression. Provocation strengthens the association, and entitlement, vulnerability and especially interpersonal antagonism help identify the more relevant components.

This supports a narrower formulation:

> **Narcissistic antagonism and entitlement can increase the likelihood that perceived insult, rejection or status loss is met with aggressive or retaliatory behaviour. They do not determine whether one person will harm another, and agentic confidence should not be treated as equivalent to antagonism.**

The dynamic evidence adds another correction. Grandiosity and vulnerability can shift over time, but the pattern is not a universal swing between two fixed states. A threatened self-position may produce shame, insecurity, withdrawal, anger, renewed status-seeking or several responses. Behaviour still needs its own evidence.

## Power does not create one outcome

Power changes what a response can do. It can amplify care by making protection, resources and accountability possible. It can also amplify self-interest by making contradiction easier to ignore and retaliation easier to impose.

Several findings illustrate the difference:

- experimentally induced power has sometimes reduced spontaneous perspective adjustment;
- power has also activated responsibility among communally oriented participants and self-interest among exchange-oriented participants;
- high-power participants have sometimes individuated others more accurately when responsibility required it;
- construing power as entrusted rather than personally achieved has been associated with greater other-orientation;
- leader power became especially damaging when verbal dominance combined with team acquiescence.

The relevant question is not simply **does this person have power?** It is:

> **Which goal does power make easier to pursue, what correction remains available, and who carries the cost when the goal is challenged?**

## Retaliation as information control

Retaliation does more than impose an immediate cost. It changes what information can enter the relationship later.

```text
feedback or refusal appears
        ↓
source is attacked, discredited, punished or role-reversed
        ↓
future contradiction becomes more costly
        ↓
less corrective information reaches the actor
        ↓
the protected interpretation appears increasingly uncontested
        ↓
the route becomes easier to repeat
```

This is one bridge between F3 and HPR. F3 examines the explanation that converts feedback into attack or persecution. HPR examines whether the source actually lost credibility, safety, access or viable refusal.

DARVO research supports one specific observer effect within this route: denial, attack and reversal can shift judgements against the harmed person. It does not allow the acronym to settle what happened in an individual case.

## F2 hypotheses—not conclusions

Later developmental review may examine whether sensitivity and position-preserving routes are shaped by:

- conditional approval or worth tied narrowly to performance, virtue, obedience, appearance or status;
- humiliation, unstable admiration or punishment of ordinary limitation;
- environments where error threatens belonging, safety or identity;
- modelling of entitlement, hierarchy or retaliatory authority;
- repeated powerlessness followed by overreliance on control;
- caregiving that confuses accountability with global shame;
- social systems that reward dominance, impunity or image protection;
- temperament and broader personality development interacting with these conditions.

None is a universal origin of narcissism, entitlement or harmful behaviour. Trauma does not explain every controlling person, and developmental explanation does not transfer responsibility to the people later subjected to harm.

## F3 ownership

F3 should examine:

- which self-position must be preserved;
- what contradiction is taken to mean;
- whether entitlement converts preference into claimed right;
- whether the other person becomes witness, audience, obstacle or regulation instrument;
- how aggression, withdrawal, justification or victim-position restoration reduces pressure;
- whether feedback can revise the account;
- whether the person treats their distress as evidence that another person caused wrongful harm;
- whether responsibility remains possible without collapse or counterattack.

## HPR ownership

HPR should establish:

- what response followed contradiction, refusal or loss of status;
- what power the actor could use;
- whether the response imposed fear, punishment, deprivation, discrediting or restricted options;
- whether the other person retained a viable separate account;
- whether future feedback became more costly;
- recurrence, escalation and spread across domains;
- cessation, accountability, restitution and later conduct under similar pressure.

One serious retaliatory act may justify protection. Recurrence is useful for understanding organisation; it is not a requirement for recognising danger.

## Terminology decisions

### Use “self-position threat” rather than “narcissistic injury” as the general mechanism term

“Narcissistic injury” carries clinical and popular assumptions that exceed what can be inferred from an event. The broader educational term is:

> **Self-position threat** describes a perceived challenge to a valued identity, status, competence, goodness, authority or claim. It does not identify the person's diagnosis, the accuracy of the threat or the behaviour that follows.

Use **status threat**, **ego threat**, **self-concept threat**, **contingent self-worth threat** or **narcissistic vulnerability** only when the evidence and construct warrant the narrower term.

### Keep “psychological entitlement” as a research construct

Entitlement should not become a synonym for confidence, need, desire, privilege or harmful power:

> **Psychological entitlement** is a relatively generalised expectation of deserving more or receiving preferential treatment. A coercive entitlement claim becomes observable when that expectation is enforced at another person's expense.

### Use “retaliatory response to feedback” behaviourally

The phrase should refer to an imposed cost following contradiction, refusal, reported impact or accountability pressure. Anger, disagreement, explanation, temporary withdrawal or delayed processing are not retaliation without the added cost.

## What this permits TEG-Blue to say

TEG-Blue can say that a person may experience contradiction as a threat to a self-position and recruit entitlement, aggression or available power to preserve it. It can say that retaliation protects more than emotion: it can protect an interpretation by making future correction dangerous.

It should not say that insecurity causes domination, that grandiosity always hides shame, that narcissistic traits predict individual violence, that power corrupts everyone or that DARVO language proves culpability.
