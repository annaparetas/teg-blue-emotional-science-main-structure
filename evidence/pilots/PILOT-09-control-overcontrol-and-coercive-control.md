# Pilot 09 · Control, Over-Control and Coercive Control

Status: first claim-level evidence pass · PEND-044 cluster 5
Scope: agency · perceived control · intolerance of uncertainty · worry · perfectionism · over-controlled coping · obsessive-compulsive personality functioning · behavioural and psychological control · social power · dominance · coercive control
Purpose: distinguish control that organises the self or a task from control that intrudes on another person's inner life or restricts their practical options

## Control is not one behaviour

Two people may both make detailed plans and dislike last-minute change. One is coordinating a difficult task. One is reducing uncertainty. One believes mistakes determine personal worth. One cannot delegate because only one method feels acceptable. One is keeping a child physically safe. One uses guilt to control what the child feels. One manages a partner's money, movement and relationships until refusal is no longer viable.

The amount of structure does not settle what kind of control is present.

## Twelve constructs that must remain separate

| Construct | Working meaning | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Agency / effective control** | ability to influence one's own action or relevant conditions | unlimited responsibility or power over other people |
| **Perceived control** | belief that outcomes or responses can be influenced | objective control, accuracy or blame for uncontrollable events |
| **Planning and task structure** | organising actions, roles, information and time towards an outcome | rigidity, anxiety or interpersonal domination |
| **Intolerance of uncertainty** | tendency to experience uncertain situations or possible unknown outcomes as difficult or unacceptable | preference for clarity, accurate risk assessment or one diagnosis |
| **Worry / checking / reassurance routes** | cognitive or behavioural attempts to anticipate and reduce uncertain threat | effective preparation or proof of compulsivity |
| **Perfectionistic strivings** | setting or pursuing exceptionally high standards | psychopathology, harmfulness or perfectionistic concerns |
| **Perfectionistic concerns** | fear of mistakes, harsh self-evaluation, discrepancy and evaluation-based worth | conscientiousness or skilled work |
| **Over-controlled coping** | proposed transdiagnostic pattern of inhibition, rigidity, emotional restraint and reduced openness | established diagnosis, one biology or proven mechanism across conditions |
| **Obsessive-compulsive personality functioning** | pervasive orderliness, perfectionism and mental/interpersonal control at the expense of flexibility and efficiency | OCD, ordinary conscientiousness or every controlling act |
| **Behavioural guidance / limit-setting** | directing observable behaviour, responsibilities or safety within a legitimate role | control of another person's thoughts, feelings or identity |
| **Psychological control** | intrusive or manipulative pressure on another person's emotional experience, attachment or self-expression | all rules, monitoring, caregiving authority or coercive control as a whole |
| **Coercive control** | patterned restriction of another person's autonomy and options through surveillance, isolation, deprivation, intimidation, credible threat or related tactics | conflict, influence, a single preference or merely wanting control |

## The target–authority–option test

Before describing behaviour as control, ask:

1. **What is being organised?** The person's own action, a task, shared coordination, another person's behaviour, their inner experience or their available options?
2. **What authority exists?** Self-responsibility, agreed role, caregiving duty, professional mandate, resource ownership or no legitimate authority?
3. **What condition is present?** Real danger, uncertainty, performance demand, conflict, independent preference or resistance?
4. **What methods are used?** Information, planning, request, rule, monitoring, guilt, shame, surveillance, deprivation, threat or force?
5. **Can the other person refuse, negotiate, revise or leave?** What happens when they do?
6. **Can the strategy update?** Does structure relax when danger, uncertainty or responsibility changes?

The key transition is not from little control to much control. It is from organising conditions to overriding another person's authorship or removing their routes.

## Self-control can protect agency—or narrow it

Planning, inhibition, persistence and delay can help a person act in line with what matters. They become a different problem when the method can no longer respond to feedback, mistakes become threats to worth, detail replaces the purpose of the task, or emotional expression and help-seeking become impossible.

TEG-Blue should describe the particular narrowing: error intolerance, rule rigidity, compulsive checking, non-delegation, emotional inhibition or inability to revise. “Too much control” is not precise enough.

## Uncertainty is not danger

Uncertainty means that an outcome is not fully known. Danger means that available evidence indicates meaningful threat. They can coexist, but they are not interchangeable.

A person may plan because the risk is real. Another may repeatedly plan because no amount of preparation produces certainty. The difference becomes visible through proportionality, stopping rules, updating and cost.

## High standards are not perfectionistic concerns

Someone can work carefully, pursue excellence and revise after mistakes without organising their worth around flawless performance. Perfectionistic concerns become more relevant when mistakes invite harsh self-judgement, discrepancy remains chronically salient, or standards persist despite clear cost.

This distinction matters socially. Demanding work from oneself does not provide authority to demand another person's compliance. F3 should examine when a personal standard silently becomes a rule for everyone nearby.

## Psychological control targets the person from inside

Parenting research offers a useful distinction. Behavioural control addresses observable conduct: where a child can go, what safety rule applies, which responsibility must be completed. Psychological control uses intrusion, guilt, love withdrawal, invalidation or manipulation to govern what the child thinks, feels or expresses.

The distinction does not make every behavioural rule appropriate. It identifies a different target. HPR still asks whether the limit was proportionate, transparent, revisable and connected to a legitimate responsibility.

## Coercive control changes the option field

Coercive control is not established because one person is decisive, dislikes uncertainty or wins an argument. It becomes visible across a pattern:

```text
surveillance · isolation · degradation · resource control · intimidation · threats
                              ↓
                refusal and independent action become costly
                              ↓
          daily choices reorganise around preventing retaliation
                              ↓
               practical autonomy and credible routes shrink
```

Intent may be difficult to establish. The behaviour, credible threat, patterned restriction and consequences can still be examined. This is why HPR records the option field rather than diagnosing the person using control.

## F2 hypotheses—not conclusions

F2 may later test whether recurrent control-related patterns were shaped by:

- unpredictable environments in which anticipation improved safety;
- punishment of mistakes, uncertainty, spontaneity or emotional expression;
- conditional worth tied to achievement, order or obedience;
- early responsibility beyond developmental capacity;
- modelling of rigid, intrusive or coercive authority;
- lack of reliable external protection or material stability;
- temperament, threat sensitivity or neurodevelopmental differences;
- roles in which precision and vigilance were genuinely necessary;
- experience of coercion, helplessness or repeated loss of agency.

These routes can produce very different adult behaviour. Being controlled does not predict becoming coercive.

## F3 ownership

F3 should examine:

- what uncertainty, error or disagreement is taken to mean;
- whether self-worth depends on correctness, productivity or obedience;
- whether control produces usable safety or only brief relief;
- when a personal standard becomes an interpersonal demand;
- whether another person's independence feels like danger, insult or loss of self-position;
- whether feedback can revise the strategy;
- whether control is justified as care while preserving the controller's preferred outcome.

F3 explains current organisation. It does not determine whether coercion occurred.

## HPR ownership

HPR should examine:

- who controlled money, housing, movement, information, technology, reputation or access to others;
- whether requests became demands through credible consequences;
- what happened after disagreement, delay, error or refusal;
- whether rules were reciprocal and negotiable;
- whether monitoring served a legitimate safety role or removed privacy and agency;
- whether fear changed ordinary decisions;
- whether the person using control restored options and accepted independent choice.

The central evidence is not “They needed control.” It is what they did with the power available to them.

## Terminology decisions

### Keep “controlling” as a recognition term only

> **Controlling behaviour** is behaviour intended or functioning to direct an outcome. Scientific-facing language should name its target, method, authority and effect.

### Prefer “over-controlled coping” to “over-controlled personality”

The coping formulation leaves room for context, change and uneven expression. It should carry an emerging-evidence label when linked to RO DBT theory.

### Separate psychological control from coercive control

Psychological control intrudes on another person's inner experience or attachment. Coercive control is the broader patterned restriction of autonomy and practical routes. They can overlap without being synonyms.

### Use “option-field restriction” for the HPR mechanism

> **Option-field restriction** occurs when another person's realistically executable choices are reduced through power, imposed cost or credible threat.

This term describes the relational effect without requiring a personality label.

## What this permits TEG-Blue to say

TEG-Blue can show how uncertainty, threat sensitivity, perfectionistic concern, rigid self-control and power may combine without collapsing them. It can distinguish a person who structures their own life from one who makes independent action costly for somebody else.

It should not say that anxiety causes coercion, that perfectionists are controlling partners, that strong self-control is illness, that all parenting limits violate autonomy or that a capacity configuration reveals who will dominate.
