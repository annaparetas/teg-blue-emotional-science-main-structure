## Intensity is not one mechanism

Two people may both become overwhelmed during conflict. One strongly shares the other person's affect but still knows whose feeling it is. One experiences personal distress and loses access to the other's needs. One becomes certain about an untested interpretation. One cannot find a stable sense of what they want. One dissociates. One temporarily loses skills that remain available in safer conditions.

“Fusion” recognises that boundaries have become difficult to locate. It does not tell us which boundary, process or capacity changed.

## Twelve constructs that must remain separate

| Construct | Working meaning | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Affective sharing** | another person's affect is represented through a related affective response in oneself | loss of self–other distinction, concern, accuracy or identical feeling |
| **Emotional contagion** | another person's emotion is caught or mirrored with limited awareness of its source | empathy as a whole, caring action or pathology |
| **Self–other distinction** | capacity to locate an action, feeling, perspective or intention as one's own or another person's | emotional distance, independence or perfect accuracy |
| **Personal distress** | self-focused aversive arousal in response to another person's distress | selfishness, absence of empathy or one behavioural outcome |
| **Emotion regulation** | processes that influence which emotions arise, when they arise and how they are experienced or expressed | emotional suppression, calmness or moral conduct |
| **Affective instability** | repeated or rapid change in emotional state across time or cues | one diagnosis, manipulation or absence of meaningful triggers |
| **Mentalizing under stress** | ability to represent mental states when arousal, threat or attachment relevance increases | fixed mentalizing capacity or certain failure in BPD |
| **Hypermentalizing / ungrounded certainty** | elaborate or confident mental-state inference that exceeds available evidence | all complex inference, intuition or deliberate deception |
| **Identity disturbance** | difficulty sustaining coherent continuity across self-image, values, goals, roles or relationships | ordinary development, contradiction or one stable form |
| **Self-concept clarity** | extent to which self-beliefs are clearly defined, internally consistent and stable | self-esteem, healthy rigidity or culturally universal selfhood |
| **Dissociation / state disconnection** | disruption in normally integrated consciousness, memory, identity, perception or embodiment | BPD, fabrication or permanent incapacity |
| **Borderline personality disorder research family** | heterogeneous clinical literature spanning affect, identity, relationships, impulsivity and stress-related symptoms | one person type, one cause, dangerousness or a capacity configuration |

## The source–state–certainty test

When self and other become difficult to separate, ask:

1. **What changed?** Feeling, bodily state, memory, identity, perspective, intention or confidence?
2. **Whose information is it?** Can the person distinguish what began in themselves, the other person or the interaction?
3. **What state is present?** Calm, attachment alarm, shame, anger, dissociation, exhaustion or acute threat?
4. **How certain is the interpretation?** Is uncertainty preserved, or has one account become fact without checking?
5. **Can contradictory information enter?** What happens when the other person offers a different account?
6. **What action follows?** Seeking clarification, withdrawing, appeasing, attacking, self-harming, controlling, pausing or repairing?

This moves the framework from “fused or not fused” towards a profile of what remains available under which conditions.

## Sharing another person's feeling is not losing oneself

Affective sharing can coexist with clear source awareness: “I feel something because you are distressed, and I still know this response is happening in me.” Emotional contagion places less emphasis on that distinction. Personal distress describes the observer's aversive response, which may create urgency to reduce their own arousal.

These processes can occur together. They should not be converted into a moral ladder. Someone may care deeply and become overwhelmed; someone may remain differentiated and choose not to help; someone may help without sharing much affect.

## Mentalizing does not simply switch off

Under relational pressure, a person may still make mental-state inferences while losing balance. They may:

- become certain about another person's intention too quickly;
- treat a feeling as proof of an external fact;
- lose access to alternative explanations;
- focus on visible behaviour while missing context;
- become unable to represent their own state;
- understand one person well and another poorly;
- recover reflective capacity when arousal falls.

This is why **mentalizing quality under condition** is more useful than “has mentalizing / lacks mentalizing”.

## Identity can change without being absent

People normally change across relationships, roles and stages of life. Identity disturbance becomes a more specific concern when continuity, coherence, goals, values or a usable sense of authorship become difficult to sustain and the instability creates distress or impairment.

Self-concept clarity contributes one measurable component. It does not define the whole of identity, and a highly fixed self-description is not automatically healthier. A coherent identity must still be able to learn.

## BPD is a research family, not a configuration

The diagnostic criteria permit many combinations. Studies also identify different profiles organised around affective instability, identity disturbance, impulsivity, relationship difficulty, rejection sensitivity or dissociation. The diagnosis therefore cannot be mapped onto `010`, `110` or any other capacity code.

This boundary matters because stereotypes around BPD can change how distress is interpreted. A request for help may be dismissed as manipulation; anger may be treated as dangerousness; changing accounts may be treated as deceit without assessing memory, state, context or new information. The framework must describe the observed process before importing the label.

## F2 hypotheses—not conclusions

F2 may later test whether uneven self–other and identity organisation was shaped by:

- temperament and early emotional sensitivity;
- caregiving that inconsistently marked the child's experience as the child's own;
- repeated misattunement, invalidation, role reversal or intrusion;
- attachment relationships in which closeness also increased alarm;
- maltreatment, neglect, loss or chronic relational unpredictability;
- environments requiring rapid reading of another person's mood;
- disrupted opportunities to name, compare and revise internal experience;
- developmental, neurodivergent, cultural or minority-stress conditions affecting identity formation;
- dissociation or state-dependent access following overwhelming experience.

No current pattern proves one of these histories. Prospective research finds multiple risk domains and weak disorder specificity.

## F3 ownership

F3 should examine:

- what a strong feeling is taken to prove;
- how certainty changes as attachment alarm rises;
- whether the person's own state remains locatable;
- whether identity depends on another person's immediate response;
- which explanation restores coherence after conflict;
- whether mentalizing returns when arousal falls;
- whether alternative accounts can be considered without felt annihilation or abandonment;
- which behaviours regulate distress and which maintain the cycle.

F3 explains current maintenance. It does not decide whether the other person's account is true or whether harm occurred.

## HPR ownership

HPR should examine:

- what each person actually did during and after dysregulation;
- whether threats, force, surveillance, punishment or option restriction occurred;
- whether distress was used to make another person responsible for preventing escalation;
- whether boundaries and withdrawal of consent were respected;
- whether the person could later recognise impact and revise their account;
- whether repair restored safety and agency rather than only emotional closeness.

Intense suffering deserves care. It does not transfer responsibility for harmful action, and it does not prove that harmful action occurred.

## Terminology decisions

### Keep “fusion” only as an accessible umbrella

> **Fusion** describes a moment or pattern in which self-information, another person's state and the interaction become difficult to distinguish. Scientific-facing language should identify the affected component.

Prefer **emotional contagion**, **reduced self–other distinction**, **personal distress**, **identity instability**, **attachment-related mentalizing difficulty** or **state-dependent access** when those are what the evidence supports.

### Prefer “ungrounded certainty” when the inference problem is broader than hypermentalizing

Hypermentalizing is useful when mental states are inferred beyond the available evidence. **Ungrounded certainty** can also include certainty generated by affect, memory, role or threat prediction. The term should describe the inference, not the person.

### Use “identity disturbance” only after naming the dimension

State whether the concern involves clarity, coherence, continuity, values, goals, roles, self-worth or relational dependence. Ordinary identity development and revision must remain visible.

### Never use “borderline” as a behavioural adjective

Use BPD only for the diagnosed clinical research family or explicitly measured features. It should never replace a description of the behaviour under review.

## What this permits TEG-Blue to say

TEG-Blue can show that the three relational capacities may be available in calm conditions and become uneven under attachment stress. It can describe affective sharing without self-location, mentalizing that becomes overconfident, or identity information that changes with relationship conditions.

It should not say that configuration `110` is BPD, that emotional intensity means fusion, that BPD means excessive empathy, that changing states mean dishonesty or that attachment adversity determines adult harm.
