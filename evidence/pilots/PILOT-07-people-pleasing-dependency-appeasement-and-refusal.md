## What “people-pleasing” may be describing

Two people may both agree quickly, suppress disagreement and organise around another person's emotional state. Their internal and relational conditions may still be entirely different.

One may be offering care freely. Another may believe a good relationship requires silence. Another may anxiously expect rejection. Another may seek reassurance because uncertainty has become difficult to hold. Another may depend materially on the person asking. Another may be responding to prior punishment. Another may be unable to resist during acute threat.

“People-pleasing” is useful as a recognition term. It is too broad to function as a scientific construct.

## Eight constructs that must remain separate

| Construct | Working meaning | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Self-silencing** | inhibiting needs, feelings or disagreement to preserve intimacy or avoid relational cost | passivity in every context, consent or coercion by another person |
| **Unmitigated communion** | strong other-focus combined with neglect of one's own needs or agency | ordinary care, empathy, mutual support or moral superiority |
| **Interpersonal dependency** | looking to others for guidance, protection, approval or support, expressed through several possible strategies | passivity, disorder, incapacity or unhealthy dependence in every context |
| **Rejection sensitivity** | anxiously expecting, readily perceiving and strongly responding to possible rejection | accurate rejection detection, appeasement or one diagnosis |
| **Excessive reassurance seeking** | repeatedly seeking confirmation of worth, relationship security, decisions or safety after reassurance fails to settle uncertainty | all support seeking, manipulation or dependency as a whole |
| **Appeasement** | behaviour intended to reduce interpersonal threat, anger, rejection or conflict by accommodating the other person | one discrete autonomic pathway, free agreement or permanent personality |
| **Non-resistance** | absence of active opposition, which may reflect choice, uncertainty, freezing, inhibition, fear, exhaustion or unavailable routes | consent, desire or acceptance |
| **Viable refusal** | a sufficiently real ability to say no, delay, revise or leave without an added punishment or coercive cost | absence of every consequence or discomfort |

## Care is not self-erasure

Care can involve generosity, accommodation, sacrifice and attention to another person's needs. It becomes a different construct when the self repeatedly disappears from the calculation.

The distinction is not how much a person gives. It is whether:

- their own signals remain readable;
- disagreement can be expressed;
- support is reciprocal or at least freely chosen;
- saying no remains possible;
- another person's distress automatically becomes their responsibility;
- care continues after meaningful harm without access to revision;
- the pattern changes when conditions change.

Unmitigated-communion research is useful here because it separates connection from connection organised at the persistent expense of self.

## Dependency is not the opposite of autonomy

All humans depend on other people, systems and material conditions. Healthy participation includes receiving guidance, care, protection and reassurance.

Dependency becomes concerning when it is rigid, global, non-reciprocal or organised around a belief that independent action is impossible. It may involve:

- perceiving oneself as powerless and others as necessarily more capable;
- strong need for guidance, approval or protection;
- anxiety when autonomous action is required;
- active strategies to secure or preserve access to a caregiver;
- inability to use support as a bridge back into one's own participation.

These components can appear separately. Someone may actively manage a relationship to prevent abandonment rather than appearing passive. Someone may require substantial support while retaining clear agency. A diagnosis cannot be inferred from needing people.

## Rejection sensitivity and relational prediction

Rejection sensitivity helps explain one possible route from anticipated relational loss to rapid accommodation:

```text
earlier or repeated rejection-related learning
        ↓
anxious expectation of rejection
        ↓
ambiguous cue receives rejection-related meaning
        ↓
distress and urgency increase
        ↓
appeasement, reassurance seeking, hostility, withdrawal or self-silencing
        ↓
relationship response becomes new learning
```

The route is not always appeasement. Rejection sensitivity can also organise anger, jealousy, testing, withdrawal or reduced supportiveness. That is why it cannot stand in for “people-pleasing”.

The developmental evidence is also proportionate rather than deterministic. Maltreatment shows a small average association with later rejection sensitivity. It does not establish the history of an individual or make rejection sensitivity inevitable after adversity.

## Appeasement is behaviour before it is a state

TEG-Blue should describe what the person does and under which conditions before assigning a biological explanation.

Appeasement may include:

- rapid agreement;
- soothing or praising the threatening person;
- taking responsibility for their emotion;
- minimising one's own pain or objection;
- offering access, information or compliance to reduce danger;
- monitoring mood and adjusting presentation;
- becoming exceptionally helpful when anger or abandonment feels near.

Some of these behaviours can also occur in safe relationships for benign reasons. The threat-related interpretation requires evidence of fear, anticipated cost, constrained options, learning history or actual power conditions.

Use **appeasement**, **submissive behaviour**, **conflict avoidance**, **relationship-preserving accommodation** or **affiliation under threat** according to the specific claim. Keep **fawn response** as an accessible translation with an explicit evidence boundary, not as a fifth universal autonomic state.

## The difference between internal difficulty and external route removal

Someone may find refusal difficult because rejection feels unbearable. Someone else may be perfectly clear that they want to refuse but know that refusal will cost housing, money, physical safety, contact with children, immigration status or social credibility.

These can coexist. They must not be collapsed.

| Internal or learned narrowing | External route removal |
| --- | --- |
| rejection is anticipated | retaliation has occurred or is credible |
| own preference is difficult to locate | preference is clear but cannot be enacted safely |
| guilt or distress follows saying no | punishment, deprivation or force follows saying no |
| reassurance temporarily restores connection | compliance prevents an imposed consequence |
| F2/F3 investigate formation and maintenance | HPR establishes power, option field and reduced agency |

Calling externally constrained compliance “people-pleasing” can hide coercion by relocating the problem inside the person with less power.

## F2 hypotheses—not conclusions

F2 may later test whether particular patterns were shaped by:

- emotional neglect, inconsistent responsiveness or repeated rejection;
- conditional regard and approval tied to compliance;
- punishment, ridicule or withdrawal following independent expression;
- parentification or responsibility for a caregiver's emotional stability;
- overprotection or psychologically controlling caregiving;
- environments in which close monitoring of others improved safety;
- dependence created by disability, illness, poverty, isolation or structural exclusion;
- direct coercion that made refusal dangerous;
- cultural and relational norms governing care, duty, hierarchy and self-expression.

The evidence must distinguish perceived, learned and materially real dependence. It must also avoid treating individualistic autonomy as the universal standard of health.

## F3 ownership

F3 should examine:

- what the person expects will happen if they disagree;
- whether another person's emotion is treated as information or command;
- whether self-worth depends on approval or uninterrupted connection;
- whether reassurance produces usable updating or only brief relief;
- how care, guilt, fear and identity sustain the pattern;
- whether support expands participation or replaces it;
- whether the person can revise the strategy in safer relationships;
- whether hostility, testing or withdrawal also appear when appeasement fails.

## HPR ownership

HPR should examine:

- whether refusal was practically viable;
- what happened after hesitation, disagreement or withdrawal of consent;
- which resources or forms of credibility one person controlled;
- whether surveillance, isolation, threats or deprivation narrowed alternatives;
- whether non-resistance was treated opportunistically as agreement;
- whether dependency was created, intensified or exploited;
- whether the person using power accepted later boundaries and restored options.

The vulnerable person's configuration never establishes the other person's conduct. HPR requires evidence of what the person with power did.

## Terminology decisions

### Keep “people-pleasing” only as a recognition umbrella

> **People-pleasing** is an accessible description for behaviour organised around gaining approval, preventing rejection, reducing conflict or managing another person's response. It does not identify one mechanism, diagnosis or developmental history.

Scientific-facing companion terms should name the component actually meant: self-silencing, unmitigated communion, rejection sensitivity, reassurance seeking, interpersonal dependency, submissive behaviour or appeasement.

### Prefer “appeasement” or “affiliation under threat” to an unqualified “fawn response”

> **Appeasement** describes accommodation intended to reduce interpersonal threat or relational cost. Its biological, learned, strategic and coercive contributors must be assessed rather than assumed.

### Use “refusal access” and “viable refusal” separately

- **Refusal access:** can the person recognise, represent and express their no?
- **Viable refusal:** can the no be enacted without an added coercive punishment or loss of essential options?

This distinction connects the capacity card to HPR without blaming constrained people for the conditions around them.

## What this permits TEG-Blue to say

TEG-Blue can say that another person's affect, anticipated rejection and learned relational cost may become so organising that self-information and refusal become difficult to use. It can say that care, dependency and support seeking are not inherently unhealthy. It can also show how coercive power may exploit or externally create the same outward pattern.

It should not say that people-pleasers cause abusive relationships, that dependency means passivity, that non-resistance means agreement, that every quick yes is a trauma response or that fawning is one established autonomic pathway.
